# The word "evolution" — history of the term and of its progress colouring

Research note, 2026-09-28. Every claim is marked **[confirmed]** (seen in a primary text or a reliable secondary source fetched this session) or **[unverified]** (reported by secondary/tertiary sources only, or not found where expected). Sources at the end.

## 1. Etymology and the pre-Darwinian senses

- Latin *evolvere* "to unroll, roll out, unfold"; *evolutio* "unrolling (of a book/scroll)". English *evolution* attested 1620s; *evolve* 1640s. **[confirmed — Etymonline]**
- OED's earliest English example is **1616**, a translation of Aelian's military treatise: a military manoeuvre / change of formation. Early 17th c. also "the process of unrolling, opening out, or revealing". **[confirmed — OUP blog citing OED, 2015]** From the 1660s used in medicine, mathematics and general prose for growth to maturity of an individual. **[confirmed — Etymonline]**
- Embryological sense: **Albrecht von Haller, 1744**, introduced the word into biological writing for the preformationist theory of generation (the germ as a miniature that merely unfolds); **Charles Bonnet** (1762 and after) made "evolution" and "development" synonyms for the expansion of a preformed germ. **[confirmed — Huxley, "Evolution in Biology", Encyclopaedia Britannica 9th ed., 1878, at Darwin Online; Bowler 1975 as reported by shellsandpebbles.com]** Huxley 1878: "In the former half of the eighteenth century, the term 'evolution' was introduced into biological writings, in order to denote the mode in which some of the most eminent physiologists of that time conceived that the generations of living things took place"; "'Evolution' and 'development' are, for Bonnet, synonymous terms"; and, after Wolff's epigenesis (1759) refuted preformation, "the words retained their application to the process by which the embryos of living beings gradually make their appearance ... by writers who would emphatically deny that 'Development' or 'Entwickelung' or 'Evolution' in the sense in which these words were usually employed by Bonnet or by Haller, ever occurs." **[confirmed — primary text]**
- Erasmus Darwin, *Zoonomia* (OED cites 1801 edn): "The world ... might have been gradually produced from very small beginnings ... rather than by a sudden evolution of the whole" — still the unfolding/preformation sense. **[confirmed — OUP blog citing OED]**
- First application to species: **Charles Lyell, *Principles of Geology* vol. 2 (1832), ch. I, p. 15**, summarising Lamarck: "the testacea of the ocean existed first, until some of them, by gradual evolution, were *improved* into those inhabiting the land." It is the only occurrence in the volume and it is Lyell paraphrasing (and opposing) Lamarck. **[confirmed — Darwin Online text; OED via OUP blog gives 1832 as first species-sense attestation]**
- Chambers's *Vestiges* (1844) used "development" ("Hypothesis of the development of the vegetable and animal kingdoms"); the Victorian name for the idea was "the development hypothesis". A Wikipedia-derived note says "evolution" in the species sense appears in Spencer's *Social Statics* (1851) and was "not in general use until about 1865–70". **[Vestiges terminology confirmed; Social Statics 1851 and the 1865–70 dating unverified — Wikipedia pointer only]** French usage: "transformism" came into common use c. 1835. **[confirmed — SEP]**
- SEP, "Evolutionary Thought Before Darwin": before the 19th c. "evolution" designated development of the individual embryo; Spencer (1852) applied it to cosmic and biological change from homogeneity to heterogeneity and spoke of a "theory of evolution"; "in the 1860s the term was used in some contexts to designate species change"; Darwin did not use it for his theory until *Descent of Man* (1871). **[confirmed — SEP]**
- **Richards, *The Meaning of Evolution* (Chicago, 1992)**: thesis that the embryological and species senses were not separate in the 19th-c. mind. Publisher's summary: in the 1600s–1700s "evolution" referred to preformation; "by the early 1800s ... the idea of preformation had become the concept of evolutionary recapitulation"; for Darwin "embryological recapitulation provided a graphic model of how species evolve", and Darwin "drew on the traditional embryological meanings of the terms 'evolution' and 'descent with modification'." The book argues against Bowler's 1975 view that Darwin's theory broke with the embryological meaning. **[confirmed — U. Chicago Press description; the anti-Bowler framing is unverified this session, since the preface could not be fetched]**
- Bowler, "The Changing Meaning of 'Evolution'", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 36 (1975), 95–114: the term originally meant literal unfolding of pre-existing parts, first applied in 18th-c. embryology; by the late 19th c. it no longer implied unfolding of pre-existing structures. **[confirmed — citation and summary via search; article not read]**

## 2. Spencer

- "The Development Hypothesis", *The Leader*, 20 March 1852 (anonymous). Uses "the Theory of Evolution" and "evolved" ("all races of beings, man inclusive, may in process of time have been evolved from the simplest monad"), and explicitly runs the embryological and species senses together: "every individual being is so evolved [from a single cell] ... a cell may, in the course of millions of years, give origin to the human race." **[confirmed — Darwin Online text]** Note this is seven years before *Origin* and eleven before the 1860s general currency.
- "Progress: Its Law and Cause", *Westminster Review*, April 1857: "organic progress consists in a change from the homogeneous to the heterogeneous ... this law of organic progress is the law of all progress"; "this same evolution of the simple into the complex, through a process of continuous differentiation, holds throughout." **[confirmed via search snippets from full-text hosts; not read in full]** The essay is where progress and evolution are formally identified.
- *First Principles* (1862), first edition definition: "a change from an indefinite incoherent homogeneity, to a definite, coherent heterogeneity, through continuous differentiations and integrations." The familiar formula — "an integration of matter and concomitant dissipation of motion; during which the matter passes from an indefinite, incoherent homogeneity to a definite, coherent heterogeneity; and during which the retained motion undergoes a parallel transformation" — belongs to the revised later editions (2nd ed. 1867 onward; the search source says "sixth edition"). **[confirmed that the two wordings differ by edition; exact edition of the change unverified]** NB the repo Spencer page attributes the second wording to *First Principles* 1862 without qualification.
- "Survival of the fittest": coined in *Principles of Biology* (1864), "in response to reading *Origin of Species* five years after the first edition was published." Wallace to Darwin, 2 July 1866: long letter urging replacement of "natural selection" because readers took it to personify nature; Darwin to Wallace, 5 July 1866: Wallace's letter "as clear as daylight. I fully agree with all that you say on the advantages of H. Spencer's excellent expression"; Darwin "introduced the phrase in a few places in his works from 5th edition of *Origin* in 1869", but "never abandoned the term 'natural selection' and only saw 'survival of the fittest' as a synonym or auxiliary phrase." Wallace went through his own copy of *Origin* crossing out "natural selection" and pencilling in the Spencer phrase. **[confirmed — Darwin Correspondence Project]**

## 3. Darwin and the word

- *Origin* 1st ed. (1859): "evolution" does not occur; the only form is the last word — "from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved." **[confirmed — Gutenberg 1st-ed. text grep; Darwin Correspondence Project: "his first use of the word 'evolution', in any form, in print"]**
- First use of the noun: *Descent of Man* vol. 1 (1871), p. 2: "Of the older and honoured chiefs in natural science, many unfortunately are still opposed to evolution in every form." Freeman (Darwin Online editorial introduction): "The word 'evolution' occurs, for the first time in any of Darwin's works, on page 2 of the first volume of the first edition, that is to say before its appearance in the sixth edition of *The origin of species* in the following year." **[confirmed — Darwin Online text and Freeman]** So: *Descent* 1871 first, *Origin* 6th ed. 1872 second.
- *Origin* 6th ed. (1872): "evolution/evolutionists" c. 12 occurrences (grep of Gutenberg text), e.g. "At the present day almost all naturalists admit evolution under some form"; "Everyone who believes in slow and gradual evolution..."; "the theory of evolution through natural selection". **[confirmed]**
- Darwin's stated reasons for avoiding the word: none found in his own words. Etymonline and Lewontin (NYRB 2005) give the standard explanation — prior association with Bonnet's preformationism and with Lamarckian unfolding of a predetermined sequence — but this is historians' inference. **[unverified as Darwin's own statement]**
- Huxley: used "evolution" freely; his 1878 Britannica article is the first standard history of the word (above) and defines the modern embryological sense: "the process of evolution consists in a succession of changes of the form, structure, and functions of the germ ... from an extreme simplicity, or relative homogeneity ... to a greater or less degree of complexity or heterogeneity" — Spencer's vocabulary applied to embryology. He mentions natural selection only once in the article. **[confirmed — primary text; the "only once" via Britannica/secondary]**

## 4. Progress and teleology

- Spencer's 1857 identification of evolution with progress (above). Darwin's counter-statements:
  - Notebook B, p. 74 (1837–38): "It is absurd to talk of one animal being higher than another." **[confirmed — Darwin Correspondence Project citation B74]**
  - The "Never say higher or lower" note: reported as a marginal note in Darwin's copy of *Vestiges*, and Lewontin (NYRB 2005) refers to "marginal notes advising never to use hierarchical language like 'higher and lower'". One source (Evolution Talk) says the *Vestiges* location has not been confirmed. The marginalia themselves were not checked this session. **[unverified as to location; the sentiment is confirmed in Notebook B]**
  - Letter to Alpheus Hyatt, 4 Dec [1872]: "After long reflection I cannot avoid the conviction that no innate tendency to progressive development exists, as is now held by so many able naturalists, & perhaps by yourself." **[confirmed — Darwin Correspondence Project]**
  - *Origin* 6th ed. (1872), ch. IV: "natural selection, or the survival of the fittest, does not necessarily include progressive development — it only takes advantage of such variations as arise and are beneficial to each creature under its complex relations of life." **[confirmed — Gutenberg 6th-ed. text]** The often-quoted "includes no necessary and universal law of advancement" was not found in the 1859 or 1872 texts. **[unverified]**
  - Against this, *Origin* 1859 ch. XIV (retained 1872): "as natural selection works solely by and for the good of each being, all corporeal and mental endowments will tend to progress towards perfection." **[confirmed — both texts]** Richards cites this for Darwin's progressivism; Lewontin replies that Darwin lists laws of nature in the closing passage and no "law of progress" is among them (NYRB exchange "Darwin & Progress", 15 Dec 2005). **[confirmed — NYRB]**
- 20th c.: Julian Huxley, *Evolution: The Modern Synthesis* (1942), expanded from his 1936 British Association address "Natural selection and evolutionary progress", contains a chapter "Evolutionary progress"; his definition (as cited by Newman 1956): "Evolution is a directional and essentially irreversible process occurring in time." **[confirmed — Wikipedia pointer; NCSE]** Gould, *Full House* (1996): progress is "random motion away from simple beginnings, not directed impetus toward complexity"; *Homo sapiens* is not the topmost branch. **[confirmed — Wikipedia pointer/reviews]** Ruse, *Monad to Man: The Concept of Progress in Evolutionary Biology* (Harvard, 1996): evolution and progress have been closely linked throughout the idea's history; the profession has been shaped by embarrassment about the linkage. **[confirmed — Wikipedia pointer/reviews]**

## 5. Modern definitions and their narrowness

- Population-genetic definition: "any change in the frequency of alleles within a population from one generation to the next" — SEP attributes it to the modern synthesis generally; NCSE quotes Curtis & Barnes 1989 for the "precisely defined" version. **[confirmed]** The attribution to Dobzhansky 1937 ("a change in the frequency of an allele within a gene pool") circulates widely (Wikipedia, textbooks) but "gene pool" is a 1950 coinage and the 1937 wording could not be checked (archive.org text is login-gated). **[unverified — treat the Dobzhansky attribution as folklore until the 1937 text is checked; the 1937 book does say evolution is change in the genetic composition of populations, per standard summaries]**
- Futuyma, *Evolution* (2005 and later eds.): "[biological evolution] is change in the properties of groups of organisms over the course of generations ... it embraces everything from slight changes in the proportions of different forms of a gene within a population to the alterations that led from the earliest organism to dinosaurs, bees, oaks, and humans"; 1986 wording: "change in the properties of populations of organisms that transcend the lifetime of a single individual." **[confirmed — SEP; NCSE]**
- Objections: Mayr (1988): the reductionist "evolution is a change in gene frequencies in populations — is not only not explanatory, but is in fact misleading"; his alternative "Evolution is change in the adaptation and in the diversity of populations of organisms." Endler (1986): the population-genetics definition "often results in an overemphasis on changes in allele frequencies and an underemphasis on ... the origin of the different alleles." Van Valen (1973): "the control of development by ecology." **[confirmed — NCSE; SEP]**
- Mayr, *One Long Argument* (1991): Darwin's *Origin* contains five separable theories — "evolution as such" (the world is steadily changing and organisms are transformed in time), common descent, multiplication of species, gradualism, natural selection. "Evolution as such" is the fact; natural selection is the mechanism. **[confirmed — multiple secondary]**

## Authors who carry the word's history

- **Albrecht von Haller** (1744) — brought "evolution" into biology as the name of preformationist generation.
- **Charles Bonnet** (1762–69) — made evolution = development of a preformed germ; the sense Darwin's generation inherited and rejected.
- **Charles Lyell** (1832) — first English use for species transformation, paraphrasing Lamarck in order to refute him.
- **Herbert Spencer** (1852, 1857, 1862, 1864) — the word's actual sponsor: theory of evolution, evolution = progress, the cosmic definition, "survival of the fittest".
- **Charles Darwin** (1859, 1871, 1872) — avoided the noun for twelve years; "evolved" then "evolution"; denied any innate tendency to progress while writing that endowments "tend to progress towards perfection".
- **T. H. Huxley** (1878) — wrote the first history of the word and fixed the two-sense account (preformation vs modern).
- **Peter Bowler** (1975) / **Robert J. Richards** (1992) — the two modern historians' readings: word broke from embryology (Bowler) vs embryological recapitulation as the carrier of the species sense (Richards).
- **Ernst Mayr** (1988, 1991) — "evolution as such" vs mechanism; the objection that the allele-frequency definition is "misleading".
- **Michael Ruse** (1996) / **Stephen Jay Gould** (1996) — the progress question restated: progress as the idea's hidden motor (Ruse); progress as artefact of a skewed distribution (Gould).

## 1. The embryological origin and the shift to transmutation

**What "evolution" means here:** the unrolling (Latin *evolutio*, the unrolling of a scroll) of a form already present — a single organism's development, later a directional unfolding of nature as a whole. No population, no heredity as mechanism.

- **[C]** *Evolutio* = unrolling of a scroll (Cicero cited); 1670 *Phil. Trans.* review of Swammerdam already speaks of "gradual and natural Evolution and Growth" of insects. (wordorigins.org, citing OED 3rd ed., June 2008.)
- **[C]** Haller 1744 used "evolution" for the preformationist theory of embryonic development; Bonnet (1762, *Considérations sur les corps organisés*) used it both for the preformed embryo and for a progressive succession of species. (shellsandpebbles.com genealogy, citing Bowler 1975 and Richards 1992.)
- **[C]** SEP "Theories of Biological Development": "Nineteenth-century uses of the term 'evolution' included a sense of unfolding of preexisting form, a sort of preformationism in contrast to the epigenesis of the day. Discussions of 'evolution' and 'epigenesis' can therefore be misleading in retrospect since the former term has assumed a meaning closer to the older meaning of epigenesis" (citing Bowler 1975).
- **[C]** OED note reproduced on Wikipedia "Preformationism": the preformation theory "was formerly known as the 'theory of evolution'".
- **[C] Lyell 1832**, *Principles of Geology* vol. 2 (Darwin Online transcription), summarising Lamarck: "the testacea of the ocean existed first, until some of them, by gradual evolution, were improved into those inhabiting the land"; and, rejecting it, "none whatever in favour of the fancied evolution of one species out of another." The same volume also uses "evolution of carbonic acid" (chemical release sense) and "these evolutions" of the brain through embryonic periods. So in 1832 the word sits astride embryology, chemistry and Lamarck's transmutation at once.
- **[C] Chambers 1844**, *Vestiges* (Gutenberg #7116 text): zero occurrences of "evolution"; 69 of "development"; the transmutation chapter is "Hypothesis of the Development of the Vegetable and Animal Kingdoms". The pre-Darwinian name was "the development hypothesis".
- **[C] Spencer 1852**, "The Development Hypothesis" (*Leader*, 20 March 1852; Darwin Online, 1890 reprint): "Those who cavalierly reject the Theory of Evolution as not being adequately supported by facts…" — with the editorial note that the 1852 original read "the Theory of Lamarck".
- **[C] Spencer 1857**, "Progress: Its Law and Cause" (*Westminster Review*; text from the 1858 *Essays*, Darwin Online PDF): "Whether it be in the development of the Earth, in the development of Life upon its surface, in the development of Society … this same evolution of the simple into the complex, through successive differentiations, holds throughout. From the earliest traceable cosmical changes down to the latest results of civilization, we shall find that the transformation of the homogeneous into the heterogeneous, is that in which Progress essentially consists." He explicitly derives the law from von Baer's embryology ("the previous evolution of the embryo") and applies it to the Nebular Hypothesis, the Earth, life, society, language, art. The 1862 *First Principles* definition ("change from an indefinite, incoherent homogeneity, to a definite, coherent heterogeneity") **[C]** via shellsandpebbles/Wikipedia.
- **[C] Darwin.** 1st edition (1859): no "evolution"; only the closing "evolved" (Chaisson 2009 also notes this). Darwin Online editorial introduction (Freeman) to *Descent of Man*: "The word 'evolution' occurs, for the first time in any of Darwin's works, on page 2 of the first volume of the first edition, that is to say before its appearance in the sixth edition of *The origin of species* in the following year." 6th edition (1872, F391 transcription): "At the present day almost all naturalists admit evolution under some form" (p. 201); "the theory of evolution through natural selection"; "Now things are wholly changed, and almost every naturalist admits the great principle of evolution." The final sentence keeps "are being, evolved".
- **Bowler.** **[C]** Peter J. Bowler, "The Changing Meaning of 'Evolution'", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 36 (1975): 95–114 — the standard account; SEP "Evolutionary Thought Before Darwin" summarises it: "Prior to the second half of the nineteenth century, the term was used primarily, if not exclusively, in an embryological sense … In 1852 the English philosopher Herbert Spencer used the term to denote both cosmic and biological changes from 'homogeneity' to 'heterogeneity', and spoke there of a 'theory of evolution' … Darwin himself did not use this specific term for his theory until the *Descent of Man* (1871)." **[U]** A verbatim passage from *Evolution: The History of an Idea* (1983; 3rd ed. 2003; 25th-anniversary ed. 2009) was not reached (Google Books/archive.org text not accessible); the book exists in those editions and Bowler's 1975 article is the citable statement.

**Figures:** Haller, Bonnet (preformation); Lyell (1832, "gradual evolution" for Lamarck); Chambers ("development"); Spencer (1852 theory of evolution; 1857 universal law); Darwin (1871/1872 adoption); Bowler (historian).

## 2. Stellar evolution

**What "evolution" means here:** the life history of one star — nebula to condensation to cooling in the 19th century, nuclear-burning stages today. Strictly a lifecycle; the sequence is inferred by comparing many stars at different ages, never by descent.

- **[C] Herschel 1789**, "Catalogue of a Second Thousand of new Nebulae" (*Phil. Trans.*, archive.org text): the heavens "now are seen to resemble a luxuriant garden, which contains the greatest variety of productions, in different flourishing beds"; "is it not almost the same thing, whether we live successively to witness the germination, blooming, foliage, fecundity, fading, withering, and corruption of a plant, or whether a vast number of specimens, selected from every stage through which the plant passes in the course of its existence, be brought at once to our view?" (p. 226). Herschel's 1811 paper extends this to nebulae condensing into stars **[C]** (secondary). Whether Herschel himself used the word "evolution" **[U]**.
- **[C] Lockyer.** "The History of a Star" (*Popular Science Monthly*, Nov 1890): all celestial bodies "are due to an exquisitely simple evolution of matter in the form of meteoritic dust"; temperature curve "like one of the arches of Westminster Bridge flattened at the top" — rising while the swarm condenses, then cooling. *The Meteoritic Hypothesis* (1890); *Chemistry of the Sun* (1887, dissociation hypothesis). *Inorganic Evolution as Studied by Spectrum Analysis* (1900, archive.org text): preface — "we have really been collecting facts concerning the evolution of the chemical elements"; Book V ch. XIX "What Evolution means: Organic Evolution" opens by borrowing the meaning from biology ("that line of work has to do, not with inanimate forms, like the chemical elements and the stars, but with living things"), then ch. XX–XXIV "inorganic evolution" of elements and stars; he explicitly places his work "just as the work of Darwin … was foreshadowed". Note: Lockyer's "evolution of the elements" = elements built up from simpler units as temperature falls; no population.
- **[C] Russell 1913–14.** Diagram presented to the RAS 13 June 1913 and AAS 30 Dec 1913 (EBSCO); "Relations between the Spectra and other Characteristics of the Stars" (*Popular Astronomy* 22, 1914). Gingerich (arXiv 1302.0862) quotes Russell introducing the diagram: "If that is also the order of advancing age, we are led at once back to Norman Lockyer's hypothesis that a star is hottest near the middle of its history … The giant stars then represent successive stages in the heating up of a body, and must be more primitive the redder they are; the dwarf stars represent successive stages in later cooling." So the H–R diagram was read from the start as a single-star contraction track (Lane's law). "Stellar evolution" was the term used at the time (Russell 1925 *Nature* paper "The Problem of Stellar Evolution").
- **[C] Overturning.** Eddington's mass–luminosity relation (1924; *Internal Constitution of the Stars* 1926) was "the first grave objection against the then widely held hypothesis of stellar evolution from giants to dwarfs"; Bethe 1939 "Energy Production in Stars" supplied hydrogen fusion; the modern sense (main sequence → red giant → white dwarf, by nuclear-burning stages, mass-dependent) dates from the 1930s–50s. Wikipedia lead: stellar evolution is "the process by which a star changes over the course of time" and "is not studied by observing the life of a single star" but by comparing many.
- **[C] "Population" in astronomy is non-Darwinian.** Baade, "The resolution of Messier 32, NGC 205, and the central region of the Andromeda nebula", *ApJ* 100 (1944): 137–146 — Population I (disk, blue, metal-rich) vs Population II (bulge/globular, red, metal-poor). Groupings by age and chemical composition, not lineages.

**Figures:** Herschel (garden/lifecycle), Lockyer (meteoritic hypothesis, inorganic evolution), Hertzsprung, Russell (giant–dwarf contraction sequence), Eddington, Bethe (nuclear), Baade (populations).

## 3. Cosmic evolution

**What "evolution" means here:** change writ large across epochs, with "selection" widened to any preferential interaction of a system with its environment. A directional narrative, not a population mechanism.

- **[C] Chaisson**, *Cosmic Evolution: The Rise of Complexity in Nature* (Harvard UP, 2001); "Cosmic Evolution: State of the Science" (NASA *Cosmos and Culture*, 2009, PDF read): "Cosmic evolution is the study of the many varied developmental and generational changes in the assembly and composition of radiation, matter, and life throughout all space and across all time." Section "Evolution, Broadly Considered": "The word evolution need not be the sole province of biology … Charles Darwin never used it as a noun, in fact only once as a verb in the very last sentence of his 1859 classic"; "selection can be generally taken to mean preferential interaction of any object with its environment"; "selection occurs in the inanimate world as well as among animate objects". Metric: free energy rate density. arXiv 1406.2730: "the most familiar kind of evolution—biological evolution, or neo-Darwinism—is just one, albeit important, subset of broader evolutionary action … what Darwinian change does for plants and animals, cosmic evolution aspires to do for all material systems"; "cosmic evolution is an aimless, meandering process". Seven epochs (particulate, galactic, stellar, planetary, chemical, biological, cultural) **[C]** via sociostudies.org summary.
- **[C]** Priority (Dick / sociostudies): Shapley's "cosmography" (1930, *Flights from Chaos*) as mid-century antecedent; "Sagan (1980), Jantsch (1980), Reeves (1981), and Chaisson (1981) all advanced the idea of complex systems naturally emerging with the pace of natural history." Sagan's *Cosmos* (1980) subtitle in some editions: "The Story of Cosmic Evolution, Science and Civilisation" **[C]** (Goodreads listing only).

**Figures:** Shapley, Sagan, Jantsch, Reeves, Chaisson; Big History (Christian) as the pedagogical carrier.

## 4. Chemical evolution — and where selection enters

**What "evolution" means here:** in Calvin, a *period* of increasing molecular complexity before life (unfolding); but Calvin already imports variation-and-selection via autocatalysis; Eigen makes selection a physical law of replicating molecules — the hand-over from unfolding to descent.

- **[C] Oparin 1924 / Haldane 1929** — reducing atmosphere, primordial soup (Wikipedia "Abiogenesis"; Calvin's own 1955 text cites Haldane "about 1926" and Oparin).
- **[C] Calvin 1955**, "Chemical Evolution and the Origin of Life", UCRL-2124 Rev (11 Aug 1955, eScholarship): divides time into four kinds of evolution — "nuclear evolution", "chemical evolution" ("the period after the formation of the earth and before biological evolution can be said to have begun"), biological, "psychosocial evolution". Then: "I must return to the concept of variation and selection as it has been developed in biological evolution, and apply it directly to this chemical system … there is no selection; it is a random process, so far … some of the chemicals that are formed may themselves be catalysts for their own formation. As soon as you recognize this, then you have available the essence of the process of selection within the chemical system itself." Calvin, *Chemical Evolution: Molecular Evolution Towards the Origin of Living Systems on the Earth and Elsewhere* (Clarendon/OUP, 1969) **[C]** (reviews in *Geol. Mag.*, *Science*). The 1956 *American Scientist* article **[U]** (not located).
- **[C] Eigen 1971**, "Selforganization of matter and the evolution of biological macromolecules", *Naturwissenschaften* 58 (10): 465–523 (Semantic Scholar/EuropePMC records; Schuster's "Hypercycle" entry). Eigen "succeeded in deriving Darwinian selection processes at the molecular level" (quasispecies; error threshold); Eigen & Schuster, "The hypercycle. A principle of natural self-organization", *Naturwissenschaften* 64 (1977): 541–565, 65 (1978): 7–41; book 1979. Abstract text of 1971 paper **[U]** (paywalled). Wong et al. 2023 also cite Eigen as the origin of molecular selection ideas **[U]** (not checked in text).

**Figures:** Oparin, Haldane, Calvin (chemical evolution as a period; autocatalysis → selection), Eigen & Schuster (molecular Darwinism).

## 5. Mineral evolution and the 2023 "law"

**What "evolution" means here:** irreversible, cumulative diversification of a planet's mineral inventory; explicitly not Darwinian (2008); then, in 2023, re-described as one instance of a general selection-for-function law.

- **[C] Hazen et al. 2008**, "Mineral evolution", *American Mineralogist* 93: 1693–1720, p. 1712, "A comment on the term 'evolution'": "The use of the term 'evolution' in the context of mineralogy may strike some readers as odd, perhaps even inappropriate. In spite of similar previous geological applications [notably Norman Bowen's (1928) *The Evolution of the Igneous Rocks*], the word continues to have contentious overtones … At the most basic level, evolution is simply defined as 'change over time,' and in this respect there can be no question that mineral evolution has occurred. However, we suggest that mineral evolution implies something more, as it arises in part from a sequence of deterministic, irreversible processes that lead from the mineralogical parsimony of the pre-solar era to progressively more diverse and complex phase assemblages. In this sense, mineral evolution is a fascinating specific example of the more general process of cosmic evolution (Chaisson 2001 …). Nevertheless, mineral evolution is not analogous to biological evolution through Darwinian natural selection (Darwin 1859). … minerals do not mutate or compete for geochemical niches in the biological sense, nor do they pass information to progeny through a genetic mechanism. For example, quartz does not out-compete olivine for crystallization from granite magma. Minimization of Gibbs free energy simply leads to nucleation and growth of quartz."
- **[C]** Objections: Rosing, "On the evolution of minerals", *Nature* 456 (2008): 456–458; Eldredge 2009 (per Hazen & Eldredge, *Elements* 6 (2010): 43–46, "Themes and Variations in Complex Systems", which says the term applies to such systems "only in the most general sense of 'change over time,' or perhaps 'change with diversification over time'").
- **[C] Wong, Cleland, Arend, Bartlett, Cleaves, Demarest, Prabhu, Lunine, Hazen 2023**, "On the roles of function and selection in evolving systems", *PNAS* 120 (43): e2310223120 (PMC10614609). Significance: "all evolving systems—including but not limited to life—are composed of diverse components that can combine into configurational states that are then selected for or against based on function … [we] propose a time-asymmetric law that states that the functional information of a system will increase over time when subjected to selection for function(s)." Three characteristics of evolving systems: many components able to adopt combinatorially vast configurations; processes generating many configurations; configurations "preferentially selected based on function". Three sources of selection: static persistence ("Configurations of matter tend to persist unless kinetically favorable avenues exist for their incorporation into more stable configurations"), dynamic persistence, novelty generation. Darwinian natural selection is presented as "one conceptually equivalent example of a far more general natural process"; applied alike to stellar nucleosynthesis, minerals, life. So the 2023 law deliberately covers both lineages by redefining "selection" (static persistence counts) — the same move Chaisson makes.
- **[C]** Follow-up: Hazen group, "Open-ended versus bounded evolution: Mineral evolution as a case study", *PNAS Nexus* 3 (7) (2024): functional information rises monotonically across nine stages (22.5 → 138.6 bits) but "mineral evolution is not open-ended" — a bounded system, distinguished from life. Critique: Root-Bernstein, "Evolution is not driven by and toward increasing information and complexity", *PNAS* 2024; reply by Wong et al.

**Figures:** Bowen (1928 precedent), Hazen (and Papineau, Bleeker, Downs, Ferry, McCoy, Sverjensky, Yang), Rosing and Eldredge (objectors), Wong et al. (2023 law), Root-Bernstein (critic).

# Language change / historical linguistics as a home of "evolution" — research note

Purpose: raw material for a neutral doorway page that sorts uses of "evolution" by meaning (unfolding vs descent-with-modification; within descent, how much of the Darwinian apparatus a case carries). Each claim is marked **[confirmed]** (checked against a fetched source) or **[unverified]** (from memory or secondary paraphrase only; check before use). Sources are listed at the end. No position is argued.

## 1. Genealogy before Darwin (1786–1863)

- **Jones 1786.** William Jones, Third Anniversary Discourse to the Asiatic Society (Calcutta): Sanskrit, Greek and Latin bear "a stronger affinity, both in the roots of verbs and the forms of grammar, than could possibly have been produced by accident" — read ever since as the founding statement of common descent for Indo-European. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Comparative method]**
- **Rask 1818 / Bopp 1816 / Grimm 1822.** Rasmus Rask's prize essay (1818) set out regular sound correspondences between Germanic and Greek/Latin; Franz Bopp's *Conjugationssystem* (1816) made the first systematic grammatical comparison of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian and Germanic; Jacob Grimm's *Deutsche Grammatik* (1819; the consonant-shift statement — "Grimm's law" — in the 2nd ed., 1822) gave the first systematic study of diachronic sound change. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Comparative method; the "1822 = 2nd edition" detail is standard but unverified here]**
- **Schleicher's Stammbaum.** August Schleicher (1821–68) first drew a language family as a tree in 1853 (*Die ersten Spaltungen des indogermanischen Urvolkes*); by *Die deutsche Sprache* (1860) the tree diagram was his standard device. This is six years before *Origin* (1859). Robert J. Richards ("The Linguistic Creation of Man") establishes the 1853 date and notes Schleicher's trees depicted actual languages, not the abstract lettered branches of Darwin's *Origin* diagram. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Schleicher; Richards]**
- **Schleicher's vocabulary was Entwicklung/organism, not Darwinian.** Schleicher was a Hegelian (Tübingen); his 1848 *Zur vergleichenden Sprachengeschichte* and 1850 *Die Sprachen Europas* already treat languages as "natural organisms" describable in biological terms (genus, species, variety) with a life-cycle of growth, maturity and decline — a pre-Darwinian, developmental picture ("even the simplest language is the product of a gradual growth"; "language declines both in sound and in form"). **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Schleicher; Britannica summary via search]**
- **The word "evolution" among 19th-c. linguists.** A history-of-terms paper (Lingua 1989, "The evolution of the concept of 'linguistic evolution' in the 19th and 20th century") reports that "transformation", "evolution" and "growth" were all in use among linguists influenced by biology; after 1859 the biological metaphor intensified, but "most of the linguists continued to use [evolution] in a pre-Darwinian way" — i.e. as unfolding/development, not variation-plus-selection. Müller's own preferred word was "growth" (and "decay"); Whitney's 1875 title is *The Life and Growth of Language*. **[confirmed for the Lingua abstract claim via search snippet; the Müller/Whitney word choice confirmed from titles; full paper not read]**
- **1863 open letter.** *Die Darwinsche Theorie und die Sprachwissenschaft* (Weimar 1863), an open letter to Ernst Haeckel, who had given Schleicher a copy of *Origin*. Richards summarises its four moves: (i) languages are natural organisms obeying natural laws; (ii) the linguistic Stammbaum is *empirical* evidence of descent, which Darwin's biology lacked (answering Bronn's objection); (iii) competition among languages mirrors natural selection; (iv) polygenesis of languages implies polygenesis of human races. English translation 1869 as *Darwinism Tested by the Science of Language*; reviewed in *Nature* 1870. Haeckel took the tree method and the polygenesis thesis into *Generelle Morphologie* (1866) and *Natürliche Schöpfungsgeschichte* (1868), crediting Schleicher. **[confirmed — Richards; Wikipedia, Evolutionary linguistics; Nature 1870 listing]**
- Schleicher's 1865 *Über die Bedeutung der Sprache für die Naturgeschichte des Menschen* (language as the criterion for classifying humans) was read by Darwin via Haeckel; Darwin's notes show "extensive" interest. **[confirmed — Richards]**

Sorting note (descriptive): the pre-1859 field already had descent-with-modification and genealogical classification (trees, regular correspondences) but no selection mechanism; its self-description was "development/growth/decay". That is descent without the Darwinian apparatus, wearing an unfolding vocabulary.

## 2. Darwin's use of language; Müller's opposition

- **Origin (1859), ch. XIII (Classification):** "If we possessed a perfect pedigree of mankind, a genealogical arrangement of the races of man would afford the best classification of the various languages now spoken throughout the world; and if all extinct languages, and all intermediate and slowly changing dialects, had to be included, such an arrangement would, I think, be the only possible one." Language here illustrates *genealogical classification* only. **[confirmed — Wikisource/Gutenberg text via search]**
- **Descent (1871), vol. I ch. II "Comparison of the Mental Powers…" (= ch. III in the 1874 2nd ed.):** "The formation of different languages and of distinct species, and the proofs that both have been developed through a gradual process, are curiously parallel" (1871 wording: "curiously the same"). Darwin lists the parallels — groups under groups, natural vs artificial classification, rudiments, dominant languages spreading and extinguishing others, no resurrection of an extinct language — then: "A struggle for life is constantly going on amongst the words and grammatical forms in each language" (quoting Max Müller), and "The survival or preservation of certain favoured words in the struggle for existence is natural selection." Language is "an art, like brewing or baking", though with an instinctive tendency to speak. **Note: the task's "ch. VI" is not where this sits; ch. VI of Descent is "On the Affinities and Genealogy of Man".** **[confirmed — Wikisource 1874 ch. III; Radick 2008 for the 1871 wording]**
- **Müller.** Friedrich Max Müller, *Lectures on the Science of Language* (1861–64): language studied as a "physical science" with natural laws of growth and decay; language "forms an impassable barrier between man and beast" (the Rubicon). Three lectures "On Darwin's Philosophy of Language" (1873, Royal Institution) reject descent of language from animal calls. Yet Müller himself supplied the "struggle for life among words" / "natural elimination" of the less fit words line that Darwin quoted — so Müller accepts selection *within* language change while denying continuity of the language faculty with animals. Müller's opposition is to the origin of the faculty, not to a genealogy of languages. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Max Müller; Nature 1873 notice "Max Müller on Darwin's Philosophy of Language"; PubMed abstract Knoll 1986; the exact "less strong, less happy, less fertile" wording is from a search snippet, unverified against the Lectures]**
- **Whitney.** William Dwight Whitney, *The Life and Growth of Language* (1875) and "Are Languages Institutions?" (Contemporary Review 1875): languages are human *institutions*, social not organic; explicit polemic against Schleicher and Müller, defending linguistics as a "moral science". A standard anti-organism counter-position of the period. **[confirmed — Oxford Bibliographies summary via search]**

## 3. The neogrammarians and the field's "mechanism"; wave vs tree

- **Osthoff & Brugmann 1878**, preface to *Morphologische Untersuchungen* I (the "manifesto"): "Aller Lautwandel, so weit er mechanisch vor sich geht, vollzieht sich nach ausnahmslosen Gesetzen" — every sound change, insofar as it proceeds mechanically, follows exceptionless laws: same direction for all members of a speech community (barring dialect split), all words in the same phonetic environment affected. Second principle: analogy accounts for apparent exceptions. Leskien had stated exceptionlessness in 1876; Verner's law (1875) — the "exception" to Grimm explained by accent — was the showpiece. Hermann Paul's *Prinzipien der Sprachgeschichte* (1880) is the codification; Paul holds the historical study is the only scientific study of language. The neogrammarians rejected Schleicher's organism model and located change in the individual speaker (idiolect) and psychology. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Neogrammarian; Rice "Foundations" translation; LRC Texas reader]**
- Modern challenges to exceptionlessness: lexical diffusion (Wang 1969) and grammatical conditioning. **[confirmed for the two headings — Wikipedia; Wang date unverified]**
- **Wave model.** Johannes Schmidt, *Die Verwandtschaftsverhältnisse der indogermanischen Sprachen* (1872): innovations spread "from speaker to speaker, from dialect to dialect, in the same fashion as waves on the water", a direct critique of Schleicher's tree, which requires change only through splitting. Marginal for most of the 20th c., revived for dialect continua, areal linguistics and phylogenetic networks. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Wave model]**

Sorting note: the sound law is a regularity of *transmission*, not a selection mechanism; the field's mechanism-talk (regular change + analogy) is Darwinian in neither sense. The tree/wave dispute is about the *shape of descent* (branching vs reticulate), independent of any selection story.

## 4. Saussure's turn

- *Cours de linguistique générale* (posthumous, 1916): the synchronic/diachronic distinction. Saussure first uses the labels "static" and "evolutionary" linguistics ("linguistique statique et linguistique évolutive"), then prefers synchronic/diachronic. Synchrony = the system in the collective mind of speakers; diachrony = successive terms substituted one for another "without forming a system". He rejects the Schleicher/Müller picture: "Language was considered a specific sphere, a fourth natural kingdom; this led to methods of reasoning which would have caused astonishment in other sciences." Effect: "evolution" survives as the *name* of the diachronic axis, but the organism/descent framing is demoted; the diachronic becomes a sequence of blind, unsystematic events, and the structural centre of gravity moves to synchrony. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Diachrony and synchrony; Evolutionary linguistics; Rice "Foundations" essay]**
- The **Société de Linguistique de Paris 1866** statute banning papers on the origin of language is the standard marker of the 19th-c. field's retreat from the faculty question. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Origin of language]**

## 5. The modern return

- **Labov.** Martha's Vineyard study (MA thesis, 1963; centralised diphthongs correlate with island loyalty) and *The Social Stratification of English in New York City* (1966) founded variationist sociolinguistics: change is observable in progress via the apparent-time method and is driven by social evaluation. *Principles of Linguistic Change* (3 vols: internal 1994, social 2001, cognitive/cultural 2010). Died Dec 2024. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Labov; Language change]**
- **Keller.** Rudi Keller, *Sprachwandel* (1990), English *On Language Change: The Invisible Hand in Language* (1994): language is a "phenomenon of the third kind" — neither natural nor artefact but a spontaneous order, "the result of human action but not of human design"; change is explained by invisible-hand processes (Smith/Mandeville/Hayek lineage), not selection. **[confirmed — publisher/ERIC summaries via search]**
- **Lass.** Roger Lass, *Historical Linguistics and Language Change* (Cambridge 1997): critical survey of explanation in historical linguistics; introduced "exaptation" into linguistics (1990) while insisting "languages are not biological systems in any deep sense"; warns that borrowed biological ideas are often "sloppy metaphors". Read as the sceptical pole. **[confirmed — Google Books, Norde & Van de Velde summaries via search]**
- **Croft.** William Croft, *Explaining Language Change: An Evolutionary Approach* (Longman 2000): the Theory of Utterance Selection, built on David Hull's generalised selection theory. Replicator = the *lingueme* (a linguistic unit in an utterance); interactor = the speaker; replication happens in utterance production; *normal* vs *altered* replication; **innovation** has functional/pragmatic causes, **propagation** social ones (this two-step is the book's distinctive claim). Croft is explicit that this is generalised (Hull) rather than biological Darwinism. Reviews: Cysouw; Raumolin-Brunberg, J. Hist. Pragmatics 2005; Journal of Linguistics. Critique noted: linguistic change often involves conscious choice, straining "blind" replication. **[confirmed — Cysouw review; catalogue entries]**
- **Mufwene.** Salikoko Mufwene, *The Ecology of Language Evolution* (2001), *Language Evolution: Contact, Competition and Change* (2008): a language as a *species* = a population of idiolects; the **feature pool** from which speakers select in contact; explicit analogy to parasitic/viral populations dependent on hosts; creoles as the test case. Uses "evolution" for change-of-languages throughout. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Mufwene]**
- **Phylogenetics.** Gray & Atkinson, *Nature* 2003, "Language-tree divergence times support the Anatolian theory of Indo-European origin": Bayesian phylogenetic methods on 87 languages / 2,449 lexical items; IE root dated 7,800–9,800 BP. Bouckaert et al., *Science* 2012, "Mapping the origins and expansion of the Indo-European language family": Bayesian phylogeography on 103 languages, Anatolian origin 8,000–9,500 BP. Both contested by steppe-hypothesis linguists (e.g. GeoCurrents critique 2012). Method imports the biology *toolkit* (cognate = character, tree, molecular-clock-style dating), not a selection claim. **[confirmed — Nature/Science abstracts]**
- **Pagel.** Mark Pagel: Pagel, Atkinson & Meade, *Nature* 2007 (frequency of word use predicts rate of lexical replacement); Atkinson et al., *Science* 2008 (punctuational bursts at language splits); Pagel, *Nature Reviews Genetics* 2009, "Human language as a culturally transmitted replicator"; Pagel et al., *PNAS* 2013 (ultraconserved words); *Wired for Culture* (2012). Carries the fullest Darwinian apparatus (replicator, rates, selection on frequency). **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Pagel]**
- Dawkins's *Selfish Gene* (1976) is the usual dating for the replicator/memetics reframing that usage-based linguistics inherited. **[confirmed — Wikipedia, Evolutionary linguistics]**

## 6. How the field answers "does language evolve in the Darwinian sense?"

Two questions are routinely distinguished and the doorway page should keep them apart:

**(a) Change of languages (glossogeny).** Positions, in order of apparatus carried:
1. *Full generalised-Darwinian*: Croft 2000 (lingueme/utterance selection, Hull), Mufwene 2001/2008 (population of idiolects, feature pool), Pagel 2009 (culturally transmitted replicator). **[confirmed]**
2. *Descent-with-modification as method only*: comparative method, Stammbaum, Gray–Atkinson/Bouckaert phylogenetics — trees and dating without commitment to selection. **[confirmed]**
3. *Spontaneous order, not selection*: Keller 1994 (invisible hand). **[confirmed]**
4. *Sceptical of the biological import*: Lass 1997 ("sloppy metaphors"; languages "not biological systems in any deep sense"); Whitney 1875 (institution not organism) is the 19th-c. ancestor; Saussure 1916 the structural rejection. **[confirmed]**
5. *Pre-Darwinian unfolding*: Schleicher's growth/decay life-cycle, Müller's growth and decay — "evolution" as development. **[confirmed]**

**(b) Evolution of the language faculty (phylogeny of the capacity).** Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch, *Science* 2002, "The faculty of language: what is it, who has it, and how did it evolve?": FLB (sensory-motor + conceptual-intentional + computation) vs FLN, with FLN possibly only recursion and possibly not evolved for communication. Pinker & Jackendoff, *Cognition* 2005, "The faculty of language: what's special about it?": language is a complex adaptation for communication evolved piecemeal (continuing Pinker & Bloom 1990, "Natural language and natural selection"); the recursion-only claim rests on the Minimalist Program and ignores phonology, morphology, words. Fitch, Hauser & Chomsky replied in *Cognition* 2005. This debate is about biological evolution in the strict sense and is a different "home" of the word from (a); Müller's Rubicon is its 19th-c. precursor. **[confirmed — Science/Cognition abstracts; Pinker & Bloom 1990 title from memory, unverified]**

## 1. Economics and institutions

**Veblen 1898.** "Why Is Economics Not an Evolutionary Science?", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 12(3), 373–97. **C** (Wikipedia, Evolutionary economics). Veblen coined "cumulative causation" as the mode of institutional change (habits of thought under material circumstances). **C** (Cairn, Commons/cumulative-causation paper). Meaning: descent-with-modification in the loose sense — no equilibrium end-state, sequence of cumulative changes, no explicit selection/inheritance apparatus. Hodgson 2004, *The Evolution of Institutional Economics* (Routledge), argues institutionalism began Darwinian with Veblen and lost that content through Commons and Ayres — chapters "John R. Commons and the tangled jungle", "The evolution of Clarence Ayres", "The Ayresian dichotomies". **C** (Hodgson site, Routledge). Commons, *Institutional Economics* (1934) and Ayres, *The Theory of Economic Progress* (1944): existence and dates **U** (from memory; not fetched). Ayres' instrumental/ceremonial dichotomy as a directional (unfolding, technology-driven progress) reading of institutional change **U**.

**Marshall.** "The Mecca of the economist lies in economic biology rather than in economic dynamics" — Preface to *Principles of Economics*, 8th ed. (1920); the passage appears in the prefaces of the last four editions; Marshall adds that mechanical analogies and "equilibrium" are retained for convenience because "biological conceptions are more complex than those of mechanics". **C** (worldfinance.com; Cambridge JHET "Biological analogies in Marshall's work"). Mokyr notes Marshall "did little or no work to carry out this program" and that Marshall (1890, p. xv) wanted "living force and movement" as the central idea. **C** (Mokyr, Ziman chapter PDF). Meaning: aspiration to organic/developmental analysis; no selection apparatus.

**Schumpeter.** Uses "evolution" for endogenous qualitative change (creative destruction), but explicitly refused biological analogy: in *History of Economic Analysis* (1954) "no appeal to biology would be of the slightest use"; in *Business Cycles* (1939) he kept the word "evolution" though "objectionable on several counts" because "it comes nearer to expressing our meaning than does any other". **C** (Springer, Hodgson 1997 "The evolutionary and non-Darwinian economics of Joseph Schumpeter", *J. Evol. Econ.* 7(2), 131–145; Fagerberg 2002). Hodgson's verdict: legitimately "evolutionary economist", "no legitimate basis for describing his approach as Darwinian". **C**. Meaning: development/unfolding from within (Entwicklung), entrepreneur as source of novelty — not descent-with-modification.

**Hayek.** Cultural evolution as group selection of rules of conduct, developed across *Constitution of Liberty* (1960), "Notes on the Evolution of Systems of Rules of Conduct" (1967), *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79), *The Fatal Conceit* (1988). Decisive factor "not the selection of physical and inheritable properties of individuals but the selection by imitation of successful institutions and habits"; "Cultural selection is not a rational process; it is not guided by but it creates reason." **C** (Mises Institute survey; Cambridge JOIE; Goodreads quotes page for LLL). Critics: Vanberg 1986 "Spontaneous Market Order and Social Rules: A Critique of F. A. Hayek's Theory of Cultural Evolution" (*Economics and Philosophy*); Zywicki "Was Hayek Right about Group Selection After All?" (*Rev. Austrian Econ.* 2000) reads Sober & Wilson as vindicating him. **C**. Meaning: descent-with-modification with explicit selection, group as the unit, inheritance by imitation/learning; variation is not blind (rules are tried). Group selection is the contested part.

**Alchian 1950.** "Uncertainty, Evolution, and Economic Theory", *Journal of Political Economy* 58(2), 211–22. Under uncertainty maximisation is ill-defined; markets select on "realized positive profits" regardless of managerial intent, so maximising outcomes can emerge without maximising agents. **C** (Wikipedia article; ICLE/Truth on the Market). Friedman 1953 borrowed the argument to defend as-if maximisation; Penrose 1952 objected that it excises human agency (and later that biological analogies were "a bad thing"). **C**. Meaning: selection without inheritance mechanism — pure differential survival; a defence of equilibrium theory rather than an alternative to it.

**Nelson & Winter 1982.** *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* (Harvard UP). Verbatim from the text (p. 14): "In our evolutionary theory, these routines play the role that genes play in biological evolutionary theory. They are a persistent feature of the organism and determine its possible behavior ... they are heritable ... and they are selectable". Firms search (the analogue of mutation) and are sorted in a selection environment. On variation (p. 11): the blind/deliberate distinction "is unhelpful and distracting in the context of our theory of the business firm"; models "interweave 'blind' and 'deliberate' processes"; "our theory is unabashedly Lamarckian: it contemplates both the 'inheritance' of acquired characteristics and the timely appearance of variation under the stimulus of adversity." They "emphatically disavow any intention to pursue biological analogies for their own sake, or even for the sake of progress toward an abstract, higher-level evolutionary theory". **C** (primary PDF). Field founded: Dosi 1982 "Technological paradigms and technological trajectories", *Research Policy* 11, 147–162 (Kuhn's paradigm transposed to technology) **C**; Metcalfe 1998 *Evolutionary Economics and Creative Destruction* (Manchester/Routledge), competition as selection with Fisher's principle / replicator dynamics **C**; Witt 2003 *The Evolving Economy* (Elgar) **C**; *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* and the International Schumpeter Society as institutional homes **U** (not fetched directly).

**Generalised Darwinism vs continuity hypothesis.** Hodgson & Knudsen, *Darwin's Conjecture: The Search for General Principles of Social and Economic Evolution* (Chicago 2010): variation, selection, inheritance (and replicator/interactor) apply to social evolution as abstract principles, with domain-specific mechanisms supplied separately. **C** (UChicago Press; Hodgson site). Earlier: Aldrich, Hodgson, Hull, Knudsen, Mokyr, Vanberg 2008 "In defence of generalized Darwinism", *J. Evol. Econ.* **C**. Witt's continuity hypothesis (2004 "Darwinism in economics: from analogy to continuity", *J. Evol. Econ.* 16(5), 529–541; 2008 "What is specific about evolutionary economics?"): economic evolution is not analogous to but continuous with biological evolution — a process "in its own right", emergent from natural history, "naturalistic", not requiring Darwinian principles as a template. **C**. Critics of GD listed by Vromen/Hodgson: Buenstorf 2006, Cordes 2006, Nelson 2006/2007, Nooteboom 2006, Witt 2003–06 — disputing the relevance of variation–selection–retention and of the replicator/interactor distinction, stressing dissimilarity of domains. **C** (Vromen SSRN; Aldrich et al. 2008; Fudan PDF "Beyond generalized Darwinism and its critics"). Note: Nelson himself is among the sceptics of GD. **C**.

## 2. Technology

**Basalla 1988.** *The Evolution of Technology* (Cambridge UP). Four concepts: diversity, continuity, novelty, selection; artefacts as the units; selection sorted into economic, military, social, cultural factors; no radical discontinuities — every artefact has antecedents. **C** (Semantic Scholar/Cambridge chapter listings; reviews). Meaning: descent-with-modification of artefacts; selection by human choice (directed, not blind); no inheritance mechanism specified beyond continuity.

**Ziman (ed.) 2000.** *Technological Innovation as an Evolutionary Process* (Cambridge UP), 22 papers from the Epistemology Group (question posed by Gerry Martin to Ziman in 1993). Blurb: artefacts and organisms "'evolve' by very similar processes of blind variation and selective retention"; the volume asks whether technological "memes" play the role of genes, in what sense inventions are "blind", and whether design makes them "Lamarckian rather than Darwinian"; Jablonka contributes "Lamarckian inheritance systems in biology" as a source of models; Vincenti contributes historical variation–selection cases (bridges, aircraft). **C** (LoC sample; JASSS review). A review titled it "an unusual and non-biological evolutionary process" (Stud. Hist. Phil. Mod. Phys. 2002). **C**.

**Mokyr.** *The Lever of Riches* (1990): macroinventions vs microinventions; "Punctuated Equilibria and Technological Progress", *AER* 80(2), 1990; "Evolutionary Biology, Technological Change and Economic History", *Bulletin of Economic Research* 43(2), 127–49, 1991; *The Gifts of Athena* (2002, useful knowledge and access to it). **C**. In his Ziman-volume chapter Mokyr states his unit: "the technique" ("a set of instructions on how to do something that involves production"), with the set of useful knowledge Ω : feasible techniques λ "somewhat reminiscent of the relation between the genotype and the phenotype"; but "the biological paradigm is too limiting for an explanation of cultural phenomena such as technology: in nature there is no feedback from the manifest entity to the underlying structure" — i.e. technology has Lamarckian feedback; he rejects the demand that "each element in that other discipline must find a counterpart"; artefacts are "awkward units of selection" (against Basalla). **C** (primary PDF).

**Arthur 2009.** *The Nature of Technology*: "the base mechanism is not Darwinian. Radically novel technologies do not come into existence by the cumulation of small changes in earlier technologies. They spring from combining or integrating earlier technologies ... a mechanism for evolution different from Darwin's: I call it Combinatorial Evolution." Footnote calls Basalla "the most complete theory to date". **C** (STIAS seminar page; Dix review). Meaning: descent (every technology has ancestors) with combination, not variation-and-selection, as the generative mechanism; Darwinian selection admitted only as a secondary sorting step **U** (the "secondary" reading is my paraphrase of the book, not fetched).

## 3. Science

**Popper 1972.** *Objective Knowledge: An Evolutionary Approach* (Clarendon; rev. 1979): "the growth of our knowledge is the result of a process closely resembling what Darwin called 'natural selection'; that is, the natural selection of hypotheses"; "From the amoeba to Einstein, the growth of knowledge is always the same"; we "may succeed in surviving some of our mistaken theories ... while the amoeba will perish with its theory". **C** (IEP; Nanay "Popper's Darwinian Analogy"). The phrase "let our theories die in our stead" is widely attributed but not located verbatim here **U**.

**Campbell 1974.** "Evolutionary Epistemology", in Schilpp ed. *The Philosophy of Karl Popper*, 412–463; earlier "Blind variation and selective retention in creative thought as in other knowledge processes", *Psychological Review* 1960. Variation must be "blind" (not foresighted) for the process to count as selection; SEP distinguishes EEM (evolution of cognitive mechanisms) from EET (evolutionary account of theories), which "do not stand or fall together". **C** (SEP entry). Campbell coined the term "evolutionary epistemology" **U** (commonly stated; not verified).

**Toulmin 1972.** *Human Understanding*, vol. 1 (Clarendon/Princeton): "populational" account of concepts within disciplines, "intellectual ecology"; Hull reviewed it in *Science* as "A populational approach to scientific change". **C**. Rationality local and itself evolving. **C** (SEP).

**Kuhn 1962.** Final chapter "Progress through Revolutions": scientific development "is a process of evolution from primitive beginnings" but "not a process of evolution toward anything"; "the analogy that relates the evolution of organisms to the evolution of scientific ideas can easily be pushed too far" (p. 172); resolution of revolutions is "the selection by conflict within the scientific community of the fittest way to practice future science". **C** (Wikipedia; Lawrence lecture summary; SEP Kuhn). SEP: Kuhn "retreated" from the analogy when criticised **C** (SEP EE entry); his late lectures (1990s) returned to a speciation analogy **U**.

**Hull 1988.** Site already carries. Godfrey-Smith counts Hull among those who "acknowledged as preliminary" the selectionist model. **C** (SEP).

## 4. Lewontin/Godfrey-Smith placement

Godfrey-Smith 2009 *Darwinian Populations and Natural Selection* (OUP): paradigm vs marginal Darwinian populations on a continuum; parameters H (heritability), C (continuity/smoothness of fitness landscape), S (dependence of fitness on intrinsic character); ch. 8 "Cultural Evolution": "Darwinism is not likely to unify and transform the social sciences" (pp. 147–8); memes low-S (success depends on access to distribution) and low-H ("once people combine too many sources of information and manipulate that information too intelligently, the phenomenon will vanish", p. 160). **C** (NDPR review; OUP page). He does not treat firms/economics specifically in what was fetched **U**.

Field-by-field self-placement (each field's own answer, not mine):
- Alchian: selection yes; replicator/inheritance none; variation unspecified → marginal (selection as filter only).
- Nelson & Winter: replicator = routine, interactor = firm, inheritance by growth/replication of firm capacity, variation deliberate + blind, Lamarckian by their own word; they refuse the higher-level abstraction → self-declared "evolutionary", not Darwinian.
- Hodgson & Knudsen: paradigm Darwinian at the abstract level (habits/routines as replicators; individuals/firms as interactors); mechanisms Lamarckian-compatible ("Darwinism can accommodate Lamarckism" is their line) **U** for the phrasing.
- Witt/Cordes/Buenstorf: reject the replicator–interactor framing; process is sui generis, continuous with but not modelled on biology.
- Hayek: group-level selection by imitation; explicitly not genetic; variation not blind.
- Basalla/Ziman group: variation–selection with directed variation; "unusual, non-biological".
- Mokyr: techniques as units, knowledge as genotype-like, Lamarckian feedback admitted; shoehorning refused.
- Arthur: not Darwinian at base; combinatorial.
- Popper/Campbell: blind variation + selective retention — the most Darwinian-apparatus claim in the science field; Campbell's "blindness" is the load-bearing requirement.
- Toulmin/Hull: populational and selectionist; Hull adds replicator/interactor; Kuhn: analogy admitted for the non-teleology only, then pushed back.
- Schumpeter, Marshall, Veblen: "evolution" as development/cumulative change; no Darwinian apparatus (Veblen wanted one; Schumpeter refused one; Marshall wished for one and did not build it).

## 1. The lifecycle / organism sense (all (a) unfolding)

**Vico**, *Scienza nuova* (1725/1730/1744). "Ideal eternal history traversed in time by the histories of all nations"; ages of gods, heroes, men "by a constant and uninterrupted order of causes and effects present in every nation"; *ricorso* = "break or return to a relatively more primitive condition" from a "second barbarism" (the late age of men's "civil disease") to "primitive simplicity", resuming "at a higher point than before" [C, SEP]. Word: *corso/ricorso*, "storia ideale eterna"; no "evolution". A single nation runs the course; there is no population of nations varying and being sorted.

**Spengler**, *Der Untergang des Abendlandes* vol. I 1918, vol. II 1922 (definitive ed. 1923) [C]. Eight high Cultures (Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese, Indian, Mesoamerican, Classical/Apollonian, Magian, Faustian) [C]; each an organism with seasons; "Civilization is what a Culture becomes once its creative impulses wane" — Culture = "becoming", Civilization = "the thing-become" [C]. Lifespan: "about a thousand years of flourishing, and a thousand years of decline" per Wikipedia's gloss [C]; the crisp "~1000 years" figure is the usual secondary summary [U as Spengler's own number]. Method: "Morphology", from Goethe's "living nature"; he explicitly distanced it from Darwin — "the century of Darwin is as remote from such a vision as it is possible to be" [C, secondary]. Word: *Morphologie*, *Schicksal* (destiny), organic growth; he attacked "indefinite evolution" in the linear-progress sense [C, secondary]. The purest case of (a): the unit is one Culture, its course is internally fixed, comparison across Cultures is homology of life-stages ("contemporaneous" epochs), not selection among them.

**Toynbee**, *A Study of History*, 12 vols 1934–61 (I–III 1934, IV–VI 1939, VII–X 1954, XI–XII 1959–61) [C]. Counts: "19 or 21" civilisations as primary focus, 23 in the vol. VII table, 27–29 with arrested and abortive ones [C]. Stages: "genesis, growth, time of troubles, universal state, and disintegration" [C]; growth continues "only when they meet one challenge only to be met by another" ("Challenge and Response") via a "Creative Minority" whom the majority follows by "mimesis" [C]. He rejected Spengler's biological determinism: civilisations "are not … organisms with a fixed lifespan; they rise and fall according to the creative — or failed — responses of their leading minorities" [C, secondary]. Word: "genesis/growth/breakdown/disintegration"; "evolution" not his term [U]. Still (a): lifecycle with the fatalism removed and free response inserted; there is a plurality of cases but no heredity between them (his "affiliation" of civilisations — parent/child via a universal church — is genealogical succession, not variation-and-selection) [U on affiliation detail].

**Sorokin**, *Social and Cultural Dynamics*, 4 vols 1937–41 [C]. Cultural supersystems ideational / sensate with idealistic (integrated mixture) between; later "integral" [C, mixed sources]. Change is "immanent": "immanent self-regulation and direction" rather than external causes, with a "Principle of Limits" — a system "can go only so far in any particular direction … before it starts to transform" [C]. Word: *dynamics*, *fluctuation*, *rhythm*; explicitly anti-lifecycle and anti-linear (no birth-death, recurrent oscillation) [U on his explicit anti-Spengler statement]. (a): directional change reversing at limits; one supersystem at a time.

**Quigley**, *The Evolution of Civilizations: An Introduction to Historical Analysis* (Macmillan 1961) [C]. Seven stages: Mixture, Gestation, Expansion, Age of Conflict, Universal Empire, Decay, Invasion [C]. Civilisation = "a producing society that has writing, city life, and an economic instrument of expansion"; six levels of culture (intellectual, religious, social, political, economic, military) [C]. Mechanism: an "instrument" of expansion ossifies into an "institution" serving its own members; "civilizations can survive crisis by reforming or circumventing institutions", otherwise decline [C]. Tested on Mesopotamian, Canaanite, Minoan, Classical, Western [C]. **Note the title**: his "evolution" is stage-sequence of a single civilisation — the word means (a) unfolding; no selection among civilisations is invoked [C on stages; U on absence of selection language in the text].

**Tainter**, *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988) [C]. Complexity is a problem-solving strategy; investment in complexity meets "diminishing marginal returns"; collapse = rapid loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity; cases Western Rome, Maya, Chaco [C]. "The ultimate cause is an economic one, inherent in the structure of society rather than in external shocks"; he rejects "mystical" and organism-lifecycle explanations [C]. Word: "complexity", "collapse", "marginal return"; not "evolution" as his frame [U]. (a): a directional cost-curve within one society; no population, no heredity.

**Diamond**, *Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed* (2005) [C]. Five-factor framework: environmental damage, climate change, hostile neighbours, loss of trading partners, the society's response [C]; cases Norse Greenland, Easter Island, Anasazi, Maya; successes Tikopia, Tokugawa Japan [C]. Framed as choice, not lifecycle: outcomes are "products of decision-making rather than predetermined historical trajectories" [C]. (a), and weakly so — comparative case method, no stage law.

## 2. Social evolutionism / the progress line

**Comte**, *Cours de philosophie positive* (1830–42): "humanity passes through three successive stages: the theological, the metaphysical, and the positive"; the law is stated at the opening and organises vols 5–6 [C, SEP]. Word: "progress", "development"; motto "Love as principle, order as basis, progress as end" [C]. (a) unfolding of the species' mind.

**Spencer** — on site. Militant→industrial and the differentiation-integration law: (a) directional law, applied to societies as organisms; his "survival of the fittest" is a separate mechanism claim the sociology does not run on [U as summary of the site page].

**Tylor**, *Primitive Culture* (1871): "savagery is the lower stage of civilization"; survivals; comparative method [C, thin]. Stage ladder = (a).

**Morgan**, *Ancient Society* (1877) [C]. "Ethnical periods" defined by "arts of subsistence": Savagery (fruit/roots → fish + fire → bow and arrow), Barbarism (pottery → domestication/cereals/irrigation → iron smelting), Civilization (phonetic alphabet, writing) [C]. Each stage is keyed to a technological marker: the earliest explicit technology-indexed stage theory. Engels, *Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884) "directly built upon Morgan's" scheme [C]. (a): a single ladder; no population sorting. (Site person page "morgan" is Thomas Hunt Morgan — do not confuse.)

**Childe**, *Man Makes Himself* (1936), *What Happened in History* (1942) [C]. "Human society has progressed through a series of technological, economic, and social revolutions"; Neolithic Revolution and Urban Revolution (the Urban Revolution folded Bronze/Iron Age revolutions and "corresponded largely to … Morgan's concept of 'civilization'") [C]. He chose "revolution" deliberately (1935 presidential address to the Prehistoric Society) [C]; Marxist "because and in so far as it *works*" [C]. Word: revolution, progress. (a) with punctuation.

**White**, "Energy and the Evolution of Culture" (*American Anthropologist* 1943), *The Science of Culture* (1949), *The Evolution of Culture* (1959) [C]. C = E × T (culture = energy harnessed × efficiency of technology); "culture as a whole, is dependent upon the material, mechanical means of adjustment to the natural environment" [C]; the canonical sentence "culture evolves as the amount of energy harnessed per capita per year is increased, or as the efficiency … is increased" is the standard quotation [U exact wording, C substance]. Anti-Boas polemic (Lowie's "farrago of immature metaphysical notions") [C]. Word: "evolution" — meaning a directional energy law: (a). Wikipedia's gloss "societies that capture more energy … have an advantage over other societies" [C] introduces a selection reading that is *not* White's stated mechanism [U].

**Steward**, *Theory of Culture Change: The Methodology of Multilinear Evolution* (Illinois 1955) [C]. "Multilinear" against 19th-c. unilinear and White's "universal" evolution [C]; culture core = "the sectors of society such as politics and religion that interact directly with the techno-economic base" [C]; regularities from parallel adaptation to similar environments; he "called attention to the Darwinian notion of 'adaptation'" and rejected progress [C, secondary]. Word: evolution = adaptation-produced regularities. Sits at the (a)/(b) border: adaptation language, no heredity or differential survival of societies stated.

**Sahlins & Service** (eds), *Evolution and Culture* (1960) [C]: *general* evolution (rising complexity/energy across culture as a whole) vs *specific* evolution (adaptive divergence of particular cultures); "Law of Evolutionary Potential": "the more specialized and adapted a form in a given evolutionary stage, the smaller its potential for passing on to the next stage" [C]. General = (a); specific = adaptation, (a)/(b) border as Steward.

**Service**, *Primitive Social Organization* (1962): band, tribe, chiefdom, state [C]; *Origins of the State and Civilization* (1975); "managerial benefits" theory of chiefdoms; integration rather than class-conflict origin of states [C]. (a) typological sequence.

**Lenski**, *Power and Privilege* (1966), *Human Societies* (1970), *Ecological-Evolutionary Theory* (2006) [C]. Societal types by subsistence technology (hunting-gathering, horticultural, agrarian, industrial, plus fishing, herding, maritime) [C]; "the cumulation of information, especially technological information" is "the most basic and most powerful factor in the evolution of human societies" [C]. Lenski also frames intersocietal selection (societies with more advanced technology displacing others) [U — from memory of *Human Societies*, not fetched]. (a) with a possible (b)-marginal component.

**Boas** — on site (1896). The neo-evolutionist revival = White 1943→Steward 1955→Sahlins/Service 1960→Service 1962→Lenski 1966 [C dates].

## 3. The quantitative / Darwinian return

**Turchin.** Coined "cliodynamics" 2003; *Historical Dynamics: Why States Rise and Fall* (2003) with Ibn Khaldun's asabiya as a model input and demographic-structural (secular-cycle) models [C]. *Ultrasociety* (Beresta, dated 2015/2016 in sources) [C on existence; U on year]: warfare drives cooperation at scale through "cultural multilevel selection" [C]. Seshat: Global History Databank founded 2011 (Evolution Institute; board Turchin, Whitehouse, François, Currie, Feeney), Neolithic to 1900 CE [C]. Turchin et al., *PNAS* 115(2): E144–51 (2018): 51 variables aggregated into nine complexity characteristics across 30 natural geographic areas; first principal component explains 77% of variance — "a single dimension of complexity" [C]. Placement in own terms: secular cycles = (a) (oscillation within one polity); Ultrasociety = (b) explicit — groups vary in institutions, institutions are transmitted, war sorts groups: all three Lewontin conditions asserted at the group level; heritability of institutions is the weak parameter (Godfrey-Smith's H) [U as my placement].

**Morris.** *Why the West Rules — For Now* (2010); *The Measure of Civilization* (2013) as the methods companion [C]. Social development index = energy capture, social organisation (city size), war-making capacity, information technology [C]. "Maps, not chaps"; the "Morris theorem": change is made by "lazy, greedy, frightened people looking for easier ways" [C]. Own word: "social development" (explicitly Lenski/White-descended [U]); the index is (a) — a directional measure — while causal story is geography. Critics: index arbitrariness, omission of institutions (Bagge), reliance on Pomeranz claims contested elsewhere [C, Reviews in History].

**Henrich.** *Secret of Our Success* (2015/16) on site. *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020) [C]: Western Church's marriage and family programme dissolved intensive kinship → individualism, impersonal prosociality; Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp & Henrich, "The Church, intensive kinship, and global psychological variation", *Science* 2019 (kinship intensity index; longest Church exposure → weakest kin institutions) [C]. Mechanism explicitly cultural group selection: "if some groups come to have sets of rituals and beliefs that allow them to out-compete other groups, then those beliefs are going to spread" [C, Henrich interview]. (b): variation (institutions), heredity (cultural transmission), differential success (intergroup competition) — all three claimed; Richerson & Boyd's cultural group selection is the frame (review: Richerson et al. / Handley & Mathew, *Evolutionary Human Sciences* 2023 "Cultural group selection and human cooperation") [C on existence].

**Diamond**, *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997): domesticable plants, domesticable large mammals (13 of 14 in Eurasia), continental axis orientation [C]. Geography, not selection: (a)/none — a causal endowment explanation with no evolutionary claim about societies.

**Kardashev**, "Transmission of Information by Extraterrestrial Civilizations", *Astronomicheskii Zhurnal* March–April 1964 (English in *Soviet Astronomy* Sept–Oct 1964) [C]. Types I/II/III = planetary, stellar, galactic energy; a classification "based on the axiom of exponential growth" [C]. Pure (a): one civilisation's energy trajectory; kin to White's energy law.

**Bostrom / Ord** (brief). Bostrom's existential risk = outcome that would "annihilate Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically curtail its potential"; Ord, *The Precipice* (2020): the Precipice from 1945, "Long Reflection" as a later stage [C]. "Civilisational trajectory" here = (a) single-lineage unfolding with branch points.

## 4. Technology at civilisation scale

**Mumford**, *Technics and Civilization* (1934) [C]: eotechnic (wood/wind/water, c. 1000–1800; the clock "the key-machine of the modern industrial age"), paleotechnic (coal/iron, 1700–1900), neotechnic (electricity/alloys, 1900–) [C]; technics shaped by "moral and political choices — not machines themselves" [C]. *The Myth of the Machine* I *Technics and Human Development* (1967), II *The Pentagon of Power* (1970) [C]: the megamachine = "a social organization that consisted exclusively of living parts" — the pyramid-age labour machine of "specialized, subdivided, interlocking parts" [C]. Own word: phases; not "evolution"; (a), and a human-directed (a).

**Ellul**, *La Technique ou l'enjeu du siècle* (1954; Eng. *The Technological Society* 1964) [C]. Technique = "the totality of methods rationally arrived at and having absolute efficiency (for a given stage of development) in every field of human activity" [C]. Characteristics — automatism, self-augmentation, monism, universalism, autonomy — are his chapter heads [U, standard summary; fetch confirmed only "autonomy" as the general thesis]. Technique grows on its own logic (self-augmentation): (a) unfolding, autonomous of human intention.

**Gille** (ed.), *Histoire des techniques* (Pléiade 1978) [C]. "All techniques are, to varying degrees, dependent on one another and there must necessarily be a certain coherence between them: this set of coherences … comprises what can be called a technical system"; history of Western technology as "a succession of general technical systems" [C]. (a): systems replace systems; no population of variants selected.

**Kelly**, *What Technology Wants* (2010) [C]. Technium = "the greater, global, massively interconnected system of technology vibrating around us" [C]; "an extension of biological evolution"; the technium as "the seventh kingdom of life" (Edge talk; book); long-term trends of biological evolution — "increased ubiquity, diversity, specialization, complexity, and socialization" — "are the exact same long-term trends of technological evolution" [C, secondary/Edge]. "Exotropy" and the three forces (structural/historical/intentional) [U — not confirmed this session]. Own word: evolution, meant as *directional* trends shared with life; criticised as teleological [C]. Placement: (a) by mechanism actually argued (trend/directionality), (b) by declared kinship with life — Kelly claims continuity, not Lewontin's conditions.

**Haff**, "Humans and technology in the Anthropocene: Six rules", *The Anthropocene Review* 1(2) 2014 [C]: technosphere as a planetary system with "quasi-autonomous dynamics"; rules of inaccessibility, impotence, control, reciprocity, performance, provision; a human creation that "gained relative independence from its creators" [C]. (a): a geosphere-like system with its own dynamics; no selection claim.

**Walker / Cronin**, "Assembly theory explains and quantifies selection and evolution", *Nature* Oct 2023 [C]: objects defined by formation histories; framework "allowing open-ended increases in complexity characteristic of life and technology" [C]. Walker, *Life as No One Knows It* (Aug 2024) [C]: "all creations as stacked lineages of causal information, subject to natural selection" [C, blurb]; site page already: technological constructs belong to the lineage process, extensions to technology "proposed framings". Placement in own terms: (b), and claimed paradigm at the level of *selection on lineages*, but with no specified heritability mechanism for technology — a large claim with marginal apparatus [U as placement].

## 5. Critics at this scale

**Popper**, *Poverty of Historicism* (1957) — on site; trend ≠ law, no prediction of knowledge growth.

**Nisbet**, *Social Change and History: Aspects of the Western Theory of Development* (OUP 1969; reissued as *Metaphor and History* 2009) [C]. Thesis: "developmentalism" — the "organic metaphor of growth and decay" used "to assert the inevitability and directionality of social change" — runs from the Greeks through Christians and moderns into "natural history" and "social evolution"; the metaphor lent the modern state the appearance of a necessary outcome [C]. Nisbet's target is exactly sense (a) worn as if it were a mechanism: growth is a metaphor imported from the organism, not a finding.

**Fracchia & Lewontin** (1999), **Ingold** (1986) — on site (no analogues of reproduction/selection in culture; transmission models presuppose a nature/culture split).

**Historians on Turchin / Seshat / Morris**: "complex social formations of the past cannot and should not be reduced to quantifiable, analyzable points in phase space" [C, Wikipedia summary of critics]; Seshat "coding of historical data is not a wholly objective enterprise" [C]; fear that mathematical history returns to "19th century historians … inspired by social Darwinism" [C, The Week]; Morris: index arbitrariness, institutional and cultural factors downplayed (Bagge, Reviews in History) [C]. Specific AHR reviews not fetched [U].

## Figures who carry this home — one line on standing beyond it

- **Spengler** — the organism-lifecycle sense in its strongest form; beyond it, a figure of the German conservative revolution and a byword for pessimism; read now as document.
- **Toynbee** — the challenge-and-response revision; beyond it, mid-century public intellectual, Chatham House director, broad influence on comparative-civilisation talk (Huntington).
- **Vico** — the *ricorso*; beyond it, founder-figure for the human sciences (verum-factum), on Berlin's and Collingwood's lists.
- **Comte** — three stages; beyond it, founder of positivism and the word "sociology".
- **L. H. Morgan** — technology-keyed stages, kinship systems; beyond it, the source Engels and Childe drew on; anthropology's founding figure in the US.
- **Tylor** — first definition of "culture"; beyond it, founder of British anthropology.
- **Childe** — Neolithic/Urban Revolution; beyond it, the most-read prehistorian of the 20th century.
- **White** — energy law; beyond it, "culturology", the Michigan school.
- **Steward** — cultural ecology; beyond it, area-studies and the *Handbook of South American Indians*.
- **Sahlins** — general/specific; beyond it, *Stone Age Economics*, the Obeyesekere debate, culturalist critique of sociobiology — much larger standing outside this topic.
- **Service** — band/tribe/chiefdom/state; little beyond.
- **Lenski** — ecological-evolutionary sociology; beyond it, stratification theory (*Power and Privilege*).
- **Turchin** — cliodynamics, Seshat; beyond it, population ecology (his first field), *End Times* 2023 public profile.
- **Morris** — social development index; beyond it, classical archaeology, *War! What Is It Good For?*
- **Diamond** — GGS/Collapse; beyond it, physiology and ornithology, Pulitzer, widely contested by anthropologists.
- **Tainter** — marginal returns on complexity; beyond it, a reference point in sustainability/energy-return debates.
- **Mumford** — technics phases, megamachine; beyond it, architecture and urbanism criticism, *The City in History*.
- **Ellul** — autonomous technique; beyond it, Protestant theology, anarchism, propaganda studies.
- **Kevin Kelly** — technium; beyond it, *Wired* founding editor, *Out of Control*.
- **Quigley** — seven stages; beyond it, Georgetown teacher of Clinton, *Tragedy and Hope*.
- **Sorokin** — cultural dynamics; beyond it, founder of Harvard sociology, social mobility, altruism studies.
- **Nisbet** — developmentalism critique; beyond it, *The Quest for Community*, conservative sociology.
- **Henrich** — on site.
