# Scoping: evolution as a keyword

Working document, opened 2026-09-13. Provisional throughout — deliberately not boxed in. The research comes first and may reshape everything below; this document exists to record where the thought stands and what is already on the ground, not to fix a plan.

## 1. The observation this starts from

**Not a claim, an observation about usage (Jules, 2026-09-13).** In ordinary language "evolution" already covers the living, the non-living and language alike: stellar evolution, the evolution of a species, of a language, of a city, of a design. Nobody argues for that range — it is how the word is used.

What is unusual is the **narrowing**. Biology took the word and gave it a technical definition — change in the heritable characteristics of populations across generations; in population-genetic terms, change in allele frequencies — and from inside that definition the other uses read as loose talk. Stellar evolution has no reproduction, no heredity, no differential survival; it is simply the word for a star's lifecycle.

So the position is not unifying scattered things under a new thesis. It is declining a specialisation and working at the generality the word already has, with the biological case as one instance rather than the definition.

The etymology cuts against the modern sense and is worth carrying: Latin *evolvere*, to unroll — an *ēvolūtiō* was the unrolling of a scroll, so the word originally implied that what unfolds was already written. Eighteenth-century biology used it exactly that way, for preformationism. Darwin largely avoided it, preferring "descent with modification"; it became the standard term afterwards and kept some of the teleological colouring it arrived with.

## 4. Homes the word has — first sketch, not a facet structure

Deliberately loose. The research decides what the facets are, and whether "facets by discipline" is even the right cut.

- **Biology** — the narrow technical sense; well backed.
- **Cosmology and stellar evolution** — change with no selection, reproduction or heredity at all. The clearest case of the word doing non-Darwinian work in a hard science.
- **Chemical evolution and the origin of life** — where the biological machinery is being assembled and the definition does not yet apply to its own subject matter.
- **Mineral evolution** — built as a subject; the word applied to a non-living, non-reproducing domain by geologists.
- **Assembly theory** — built; construction history and selection without the population-genetic apparatus.
- **Cultural evolution** — Boyd, Richerson, Henrich; a live dispute about how much of the biological apparatus transfers.
- **Language change / historical linguistics** — the comparative method, sound change, language families. One of the oldest uses of the word outside biology and possibly the most instructive, since descent-with-modification was borrowed *into* biology from philology as much as the other way round. **Backing state unknown — check.**
- **Technological, institutional and economic change** — evolutionary economics, the design literature. **Backing state unknown.**
- **Evolutionary computation** — Holland built; genetic algorithms as the word applied to a deliberately engineered process.

## 6. Open, and staying open

- Is "evolution" one concept across these homes, or a family sharing a word? Biology's answer is that only its own case is evolution proper. The neutral material is what any other answer would have to meet — and the research may well find the question is not the interesting one.
- Does the doorway sort by discipline, or by something the material itself suggests (with-selection vs without? with-heredity vs without? the direction question)? Undecided, and better decided from the material.
- Does the word's teleological inheritance deserve its own page, as the emergence bundle gave the definitional confusion its own pages?
- Where does the existing Darwinism bundle sit relative to a doorway — beneath it as the biology facet's backing, or does the doorway largely point at it?
- Naming: "evolution" is almost certainly right, being the keyword and the field's word both, but the territory-naming test should still be applied rather than assumed.
