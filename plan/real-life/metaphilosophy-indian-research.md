# Metaphilosophy in India: research note (top-up)

Purpose: to fill the gaps for a Metaphilosophy page on India, beside the Greek and Latin and the Islamic pages. Each stage answers (1) What is philosophy? (2) Its point? (3) How is it done? Headlines only. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (see Sources). **[C as quoted]** = wording checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified. "My rendering" = mine from the fetched Sanskrit or Pāli. Items marked **[earlier round]** were checked in a previous round (*darśana* and Halbfass's word test; Kauṭilya *Arthaśāstra* 1.2.10–12; Jacobi, Hacker, Halbfass; *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.1–3; Vātsyāyana; Hegel against Matilal and Daya Krishna). They were not redone here.

## 1. Naming the page (word test)

- No Sanskrit word maps onto "philosophy". *Darśana* is a doxographic "view" that the philosophers rarely used for their own work. *Ānvīkṣikī* is a method, not a field. The schools' own words were *śāstra*, *vidyā*, *vāda*, *siddhānta* [earlier round]. The texts in this note add *vidyā* (knowledge, science), *jijñāsā* (desire to know, inquiry) and *pramāṇa* (means of knowing) as the working vocabulary (sections 2–5).
- "Indian philosophy" is itself the contested phrase: Jacobi's equation, Hacker's rejection and Halbfass's verdict [earlier round]. "Hindu" is wrong for the scope, since Buddhist, Jaina and Cārvāka are inside it. "Sanskrit philosophy" is also wrong, since Pāli and Prakrit texts belong too.
- **Options (Jules's call):**
  - (a) **"Philosophy in India"**, parallel to "Philosophy in the Islamic world". It is a place-designation that does not assert an Indian concept of philosophy, and the intro can carry the Hacker/Halbfass headline.
  - (b) **"The Indian tradition"**, parallel to "The Greek and Latin tradition". Shorter, but the singular hides the plurality: the Vedic schools against the Buddhist and Jaina renouncer traditions, with the Cārvāka outside both.
  - (c) **"Philosophy in classical and modern India"**. Accurate, but long.
- My lean is (a).

## 2. The Upaniṣads

**Q1.** Muṇḍaka 1.1.1 calls *brahmavidyā* "the foundation of all knowledge" (*sarvavidyāpratiṣṭhā*): "the knowledge of Brahman, the foundation of all knowledge" [C, Sanskrit S1; Müller S2, SBE 15 p. 27]. Śaunaka's question (1.1.3): "Sir, what is that through which, if it is known, everything else becomes known?" (*kasmin nu bhagavo vijñāte sarvam idaṃ vijñātaṃ bhavati*) [C, S1; S2 p. 27]. The answer is **"Two kinds of knowledge must be known … the higher and the lower knowledge" (*dve vidye veditavye … parā caivāparā ca*, 1.1.4)**. The lower is the four Vedas and the six auxiliary sciences (phonetics, ritual, grammar, etymology, metre, astronomy). "But the higher knowledge is that by which the Indestructible (Brahman) is apprehended" (*atha parā yayā tad akṣaram adhigamyate*, 1.1.5) [C, S1; S2 pp. 27–28]. Müller notes that "other MSS. add here *itihāsa-purāṇa-nyāya-mīmāṃsā-dharmaśāstrāṇi*" [C, S2 p. 27 n.]: in that reading, *nyāya* and *mīmāṃsā* stand among the lower knowledge. A detail, but a telling one.

**Q2.** Chāndogya 7.1.2–3: Nārada lists his learning, including *vākovākya* (Müller: "logic"), and then says: "with all this I know the Mantras only … I do not know the Self. I have heard … that he who knows the Self overcomes grief. I am in grief" (*mantravid evāsmi nātmavit … tarati śokam ātmavit*) [C, S3 SBE 1 pp. 109–110; Sanskrit C, S4]. This is the cleanest Upaniṣadic Q2 line: self-knowledge (*ātmavid*, "self-knower") against sorrow. The noun *ātmavidyā* for the discipline is Vātsyāyana's usage [earlier round]; I did not find it in these Upaniṣad passages.

**Q3.**
- **The teacher.** Muṇḍaka 1.2.12: having "examined all these worlds which are gained by works", and since "nothing that is eternal (not made) can be gained by what is not eternal (made)", "let him … take fuel in his hand and approach a Guru who is learned and dwells entirely in Brahman" [C, S1; S2 pp. 32–33].
- **Hearing, reflection, meditation.** Bṛhadāraṇyaka 2.4.5 (repeated at 4.5.6): "*ātmā vā are draṣṭavyaḥ śrotavyo mantavyo nididhyāsitavyo maitreyi*". Müller: "Verily, the Self is to be seen, to be heard, to be perceived, to be marked, O Maitreyī!" [C, Sanskrit S5; S2 pp. 109–110].
- **Word test.** The Upaniṣad has four gerundives, "to be seen" first. The three-step method as nouns (*śravaṇa–manana–nididhyāsana*) is Śaṅkara's gloss: "to be heard first from the teacher and from scripture; afterwards to be reflected on, by reasoning (*tarkataḥ*); then to be meditated on with certainty… seen through the means of hearing, reflection and meditation (*śravaṇa-manana-nididhyāsana-sādhanaiḥ*)" [C, S5 BUBh 2.4.5; my rendering]. On the page, attribute the triad to Śaṅkara and the verse to the Upaniṣad.
- *Neti neti* (BU 2.3.6 and elsewhere) is an *ādeśa*, a designation of Brahman [C, S5, as quoted in Śaṅkara's commentary]. It is not offered as a method of inquiry. I did not open a translation of 2.3.6. My recommendation is to leave it out (see Discard list).

## 3. The Buddha and the Jina

**Buddha, Q2/Q3: the unanswered questions.** Cūḷamālukya Sutta (MN 63), tr. Sujato [C, S6]:
- Māluṅkyaputta lists the "convictions that the Buddha has left undeclared" (*diṭṭhigatāni … abyākatāni*): the world eternal or not, finite or infinite; the soul and the body one or two; a realised one existing after death or not (all four options) (2.2–2.3).
- The arrow: "Suppose a man was struck by an arrow thickly smeared with poison" who refuses treatment until he knows who shot it, of what caste and clan, with what bow… "That man would still not have learned these things, and meanwhile they'd die" (5.6–5.30).
- Why undeclared: "Because they aren't beneficial or relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life. They don't lead to disillusionment, dispassion, cessation, peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment" (8.2). Declared instead: "'this is suffering,' 'this is the origin of suffering,' 'this is the cessation of suffering,' 'this is the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering'" (9.2–9.7).
- With any of these views "there is rebirth, there is old age, there is death, and there is sorrow … And it is the defeat of these things in this very life that I advocate" (6.3–6.4, 6.19–6.20).
- **Word test.** *Diṭṭhi* (Sanskrit *dṛṣṭi*, "view") is from the same root (*dṛś*, "see") as *darśana* [my observation, not a scholar's claim; do not place without a source]. Sujato renders *diṭṭhigata* "convictions"; other renderings ("speculative views") are [U].

**Buddha, Q3: testing.** Kesamutti (Kālāma) Sutta (AN 3.65), tr. Sujato: "don't go by oral transmission, don't go by lineage … don't go by canonical authority, don't rely on logic (*mā takkahetu*), don't rely on inference … But when you know for yourselves: 'These things are unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people, and when you undertake them, they lead to harm and suffering', then you should give them up" (4.1–4.3) [C, S7]. The scope is the skilful and the unskilful, i.e. conduct. It is not a general epistemology, so a page should not stretch it. Its modern reading as a "charter of free inquiry" is reception [U].

**Jina, Q1/Q2.** *Tattvārthasūtra* 1.1, *samyagdarśanajñānacāritrāṇi mokṣamārgaḥ*: "Right belief, (right) knowledge, (right) conduct, these (together constitute) the path to liberation" (tr. J. L. Jaini 1920) [C, S9]. SEP (Gorisse 2023): "The path to liberation is constituted by right view (*darśana*), right knowledge, and right conduct" [C, S10]. Jaini adds that "Right belief is not identical with faith … It is reasoned knowledge" [C, S9]. **Word test.** *Darśana* here is "belief" (Jaini), "view" (Gorisse); "enlightened world-view" (Tatia 1994) is [U]. SEP dates the text to "around 150–400 CE", "probably written by multiple authors", "the oldest extant Jaina treatise in Sanskrit" [C, S10]. Gorisse's gloss on the point: "not by modifying one's environment, but by modifying oneself" [C, S10].

**Jina, Q3.**
- **Standpoints.** TS 1.6, *pramāṇanayair adhigamaḥ*: knowledge is attained through *pramāṇa* and *naya*. Jaini: "*Naya*, a stand-point which gives partial knowledge of a thing in some particular aspect of it" [C, S9].
- **Many-sidedness.** *Anekāntavāda* is "the famous doctrine of the many-sidedness of things", and the seven modes of predication follow Samantabhadra's *Āptamīmāṃsā* 14: an entity is "somehow" (*syāt*) being, non-being, both, indescribable, "in accordance with the speaker's intention and not in an absolute fashion" [C as quoted, S10].
- **The metaphilosophical headline.** In the doctrine of perspectives (*nayavāda*), "Jaina philosophers came to explain the resisting divergences between the different philosophical systems as resulting from an initial choice of an epistemic perspective". Rival systems are "the erroneous radicalization of a perspective" (Gorisse) [C, S10]. This is a Jaina answer to "why are there many philosophies?".
- **Word test.** *Anekāntavāda* ("non-one-sidedness") is the tradition's own term (Haribhadra's *Anekāntajayapatākā*) [C, S10]. "Relativism" and "pluralism" are later readers' words.

## 4. Sāṃkhya and the Cārvāka

**Sāṃkhya, Q2.** *Sāṃkhyakārikā* 1: "*duḥkhatrayābhighātāj jijñāsā tadapaghātake hetau / dṛṣṭe sāpārthā cen naikāntātyantato 'bhāvāt*" [C, S11]. Colebrooke (1837): **"The inquiry is into the means of precluding the three sorts of pain; for pain is embarrassment: nor is the inquiry superfluous because obvious means of alleviation exist, for absolute and final relief is not thereby accomplished"** [C, S12 p. 1]. A plainer rendering: "from the blow of the threefold suffering, the desire to know (*jijñāsā*) the means of removing it". The opening word of the inquiry is again *jijñāsā* [my rendering].

**Sāṃkhya, Q3.** SK 2, Colebrooke: "The revealed mode is like the temporal one, ineffectual, for it is impure… A method different from both is preferable, consisting in a discriminative knowledge of perceptible principles, and of the imperceptible one, and of the thinking soul" (*vyaktāvyaktajñavijñāna*) [C, S11; S12]. Knowledge, not Vedic ritual, ends suffering.

**Word test.** Colebrooke's title is *Memorial Verses on the Sánkhya Philosophy*. "Philosophy" is the translator's word; the text says *sāṃkhya* [C, S12 title page]. Dating (c. 4th–5th c. CE) is [U].

**Cārvāka (the open check).** In Mādhava's 14th-century *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha*, ch. 1 (tr. Cowell and Gough):
- "the only Liberation is the dissolution of the body".
- A verse ascribed to Bṛhaspati: "There is no heaven, no final liberation, nor any soul in another world".
- On method: "this school holds that perception is the only source of knowledge and does not allow inference".
- On the point: "The only end of man is enjoyment produced by sensual pleasures" [C, S13 ch. 1].

**Answer to the check.** Yes, as their opponents report it: liberation is denied, or redefined as death. The caution is the headline: the account comes from an Advaitin doxographer, and no full Cārvāka work survives except Jayarāśi's *Tattvopaplavasiṃha* [U]. State it as "as reported by". Kauṭilya counts Lokāyata as *ānvīkṣikī* [earlier round], so the investigative science is not defined by liberation.

## 5. The inquiry into dharma and into Brahman (Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta)

**Q1: the opening words.** *Mīmāṃsāsūtra* 1.1.1, **"athāto dharmajijñāsā"** [C, S14]. *Brahmasūtra* 1.1.1, **"athāto brahmajijñāsā"** [C, S15]. Thibaut renders the latter "Then therefore the enquiry into Brahman", noting "(more literally, the desire of knowing Brahman)"; he renders the former, as Śaṅkara cites it, "then therefore the enquiry into duty" [C, S8 pp. 9, 25]. **Word test.** *Jijñāsā* is the desiderative of *jñā*, "desire to know"; "enquiry" is the translator's English. MS 1.1.2 gives *dharma*: "*codanālakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ*", the good defined by Vedic injunction [C, S14; my rendering].

**Q2.** Śaṅkara distinguishes the two inquiries by their fruit. Knowledge of dharma yields prosperity (*abhyudaya*) and depends on performance. Knowledge of Brahman yields the highest good (*niḥśreyasa*) and depends on no further act [C, S16 BSBh 1.1.1]. The study is begun "with a view to freeing one's self from that wrong notion which is the cause of all evil and attaining thereby the knowledge of the absolute unity of the Self" (end of the *adhyāsabhāṣya*, tr. Thibaut) [C, S8].

**Q3: who may inquire.** Śaṅkara (BSBh 1.1.1): "the antecedent conditions are the discrimination of what is eternal and what is non-eternal; the renunciation of all desire to enjoy the fruit (of one's actions) both here and hereafter; the acquirement of tranquillity, self-restraint, and the other means, and the desire of final release" (*nityānityavastuvivekaḥ, ihāmutrārthabhogavirāgaḥ, śamadamādisādhanasaṃpat, mumukṣutvaṃ ca*) [C, S8 p. 12; S16]. **Word test.** Śaṅkara calls these *sādhana* ("means"). *Adhikāra* (eligibility) is the general Mīmāṃsā term [U as the tradition's label here]. The later name *sādhana-catuṣṭaya* is [U].

**Q3: reasoning.**
- *Brahmasūtra* 2.1.11, *tarkāpratiṣṭhānāt*. Śaṅkara: "In matters to be known from Scripture mere reasoning is not to be relied on … As the thoughts of man are altogether unfettered, reasoning which disregards the holy texts and rests on individual opinion only has no proper foundation. We see how arguments, which some clever men had excogitated with great pains, are shown, by people still more ingenious, to be fallacious … even men of the most undoubted mental eminence, such as Kapila, Kaṇāda, and other founders of philosophical schools, have contradicted one another" (tr. Thibaut) [C, S8 pp. 314–315; Sanskrit S16].
- He lets the objector reply that "'want of foundation' … really constitutes the beauty of reasoning". He answers that in the matter of release reasoning still fails [C, S8 p. 315].
- Reasoning has a place, as "a subordinate auxiliary of intuitional knowledge" (BSBh 2.1.6; Sanskrit *śrutyanugṛhīta eva hy atra tarko 'nubhavāṅgatvenāśrīyate*, "reasoning favoured by scripture is resorted to here as an auxiliary of *anubhava*") [C, S8 p. 308; S16].
- In BSBh 1.1.2 "intuition is the final result of the enquiry into Brahman" (*anubhavāvasānatvāt*) [C, S8 p. 18; S16].
- **Word test, important.** Thibaut's "intuition" renders *anubhava*, "experience". The English "intuition" feeds the "Indian thought is intuitive" reading that Halbfass ties to Neo-Hinduism (*darśana* as "vision") [earlier round]. Render *anubhava* as "experience" or keep the Sanskrit. Halbfass's chapter on the concept of experience is an open check.

## 6. The pramāṇa debate (Nyāya, Buddhist epistemology, Nāgārjuna)

**Buddhist epistemology.**
- Dharmakīrti, *Nyāyabindu* 1.1: "*samyagjñānapūrvikā sarvapuruṣārthasiddhir iti tad vyutpādyate*". Stcherbatsky: **"All successful human action is preceded by right knowledge. Therefore this (knowledge will be here) investigated"** [C, S17 ch. 1 §1; Sanskrit S18].
- 1.2–3: "*dvividhaṃ samyagjñānam / pratyakṣam anumānaṃ ca*", "Right knowledge is twofold": perception and inference [C, S17; S18].
- **Word test.** *Puruṣārtha* is "human aim". "Successful human action" is Stcherbatsky's.
- Dharmottara's commentary gives the point negatively: no reasonable man would "undertake an enquiry about the teeth of the crow, because this would serve no purpose" [C, S17].
- Dharmakīrti's definition: "*pramāṇam avisaṃvādi jñānam arthakriyāsthitiḥ*". A means of knowledge is non-deceptive cognition, non-deception being the reliability of its practical efficacy (*Pramāṇavārttika*, *pramāṇasiddhi* 1; ch. 2 in the GRETIL numbering) [C, S19; my rendering].
- Dignāga's prior "*pratyakṣam anumānaṃ ca pramāṇe*" (*Pramāṇasamuccaya* 1.2) is [U].

**Nāgārjuna, Q3 in his own words.**
- *Vigrahavyāvartanī* 29: "*yadi kā cana pratijñā syān me tata eṣa me bhaved doṣaḥ / nāsti ca mama pratijñā tasmān naivāsti me doṣaḥ*", "If I had any thesis, this fault would be mine; but I have no thesis, so the fault is not mine" [C, S20; my rendering].
- **Word test.** *Pratijñā* is the technical "thesis", the first member of the Nyāya inference. The work is a reply to a Nyāya-style critic of emptiness on the *pramāṇa*s [U as to the opponent's identity].
- **Q2.** MMK 13.8: "*śūnyatā sarvadṛṣṭīnāṃ proktā niḥsaraṇaṃ jinaiḥ / yeṣāṃ tu śūnyatādṛṣṭis tān asādhyān babhāṣire*", "The victors have said that emptiness is the relinquishing of all views; those for whom emptiness is a view, they called incurable" [C, S21; my rendering].
- **Q2, the closing verse.** MMK 27.30: "*sarvadṛṣṭiprahāṇāya yaḥ saddharmam adeśayat*", "who taught the true dharma for the abandoning of all views… to him, Gautama, I bow" [C, S21; my rendering].
- Garfield (1995) and Siderits–Katsura (2013) translations [U, not opened]. *Prasaṅga* against *svātantra* is already on `madhyamaka`. Link it; don't restate it.

## 7. Commentary and the new reason (early modern)

- **Ganeri, *The Lost Age of Reason: Philosophy in Early Modern India 1450–1700* (OUP 2011).** Headline, via Thom Brooks's NDPR review (2013.03.03) [C as quoted, S22]. Ganeri reads the layered commentary (sūtra, *bhāṣya*, subcommentary, *nibandha*) as a philosophical method that continues "the process of revision and adjustment until a state of reflective equilibrium is reached" (p. 103): "reading philosophically is a way of thinking philosophically" (p. 115). The review gives the book's theme as a modernity "where the modern represents a break from and rejection of the past" being absent: "Indian philosophers view the past as something to engage and develop further". Its parts include "Text and Method" and "The Possibility of Inquiry" (on Gaṅgeśa).
- Navya-Nyāya's "precise technical language" [earlier round]. I found no quotable primary line on method short enough for a headline. Gaṅgeśa's *Tattvacintāmaṇi* opening is a candidate [U, not opened; GRETIL has only the *śabda* section]. My recommendation is to let Ganeri carry the stage.

## 8. The modern question

- **Radhakrishnan, *Indian Philosophy* vol. 1 (1923), Introduction §II "General Characteristics of Indian Thought": "Philosophy in India is essentially spiritual"** (p. 24). Also: "The spiritual motive dominates life in India. Indian philosophy has its interest in the haunts of men" (p. 25) [C, S23]. This is the view Matilal and Daya Krishna oppose [earlier round].
- **K. C. Bhattacharyya, "Svaraj in Ideas"** [C, S24, IPQ reprint, pp. 383–393]:
  - **Dating.** The IPQ footnote: "This discourse was given at a meeting of the students of the Hooghly College of which the writer was Principal, during 1928-30 … Subsequently it was published in the *Visva Bharati Journal*, Vol. XX, 1954; pp. 103-114." Reprinted *Indian Philosophical Quarterly* XI.4 (Oct. 1984). The "October 1931, Chandernagore" date in other accounts is [U]; on the page say "given c. 1928–31, published 1954". The heading spells the name "Bhattacharya"; Bhattacharyya is the usual form.
  - **§1:** "There is however a subtler domination exercised in the sphere of ideas by one culture on another". "There is cultural subjection only when one's traditional cast of ideas and sentiments is superseded without comparison or competition by a new cast representing an alien culture which possesses one like a ghost. This subjection is slavery of the spirit". Freed of it one "experiences a rebirth, and that is what I call Svaraj in Ideas" (p. 383).
  - **§9 (Q1/Q3):** "There is nothing like a judgment on western systems from the standpoint of Indian philosophy … there appears to be no recognition yet that a criticism of the fundamental notions of either philosophy is necessary before there can be any useful comparative estimate". "If the Modern Indian Mind is to philosophise at all to any purpose, it has to confront Eastern thought and Western thought with one another and attempt a synthesis or a reasoned rejection of either" (p. 386).
  - **Q2:** "it is through philosophy that we can methodically attempt to discover it", namely "the soul of India" (p. 387).
  - **§12 (a word-test line in its own right):** "Thought or reason may be universal, but ideas are carved out of it differently by different cultures … No idea of one cultural language can exactly be translated in another cultural language" (p. 388).
  - His "The Concept of Philosophy" (1936) is more directly metaphilosophical [U, not opened].
- **Daya Krishna, "Three Myths about Indian Philosophy".** Still not opened. It is collected in *Indian Philosophy: A Counter Perspective* (1991; rev. 2006) [C as listed, S25 obituary by Raveh]. First publication and wording are still [U]. A related "Three Conceptions of Indian Philosophy" may be a separate essay [U].

## 9. Draft outline (proposal)

Intro: the words (no Sanskrit "philosophy"; *darśana*, *ānvīkṣikī*, *vidyā*, *jijñāsā*), the Jacobi–Hacker–Halbfass headline, and the naming choice.

| Stage | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Upaniṣads | *brahmavidyā*, "foundation of all knowledge"; the higher and the lower knowledge (Muṇḍaka 1.1.4–5) | "he who knows the Self overcomes grief" (Chāndogya 7.1.3) | approach a teacher (Muṇḍaka 1.2.12); "to be seen, to be heard…" (BU 2.4.5), Śaṅkara's triad |
| The Buddha and the Jina | none on Q1 in MN 63; the Jina's path of view, knowledge and conduct (TS 1.1) | the end of suffering "in this very life"; speculation set aside (MN 63) | the arrow; test for yourselves (AN 3.65, conduct); *pramāṇa* and *naya*, rival systems as radicalised perspectives |

| Inquiry into dharma and Brahman: Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta | "athāto dharmajijñāsā" / "athāto brahmajijñāsā" | prosperity against the highest good; freedom from "the cause of all evil" | qualified inquirers (four means); reasoning "has no proper foundation" apart from scripture, but serves *anubhava* |

| The modern question | "essentially spiritual" (Radhakrishnan) against the corrective (Matilal, Daya Krishna) | "Svaraj in Ideas": freedom from "slavery of the spirit" | confront the two traditions, "a synthesis or a reasoned rejection of either" |

Sequence options: chronological as above; or put the *pramāṇa* debate before Mīmāṃsā/Vedānta, since Śaṅkara (8th c.) postdates Dignāga and Dharmakīrti [dates U]. The stages overlap in time far more than on the Islamic page. A sentence in the intro should say that the schools ran side by side in debate rather than in succession.

## 13. Discard list (do not place)

- *Neti neti* as a "method of defining through differentiation" (a SPLectrum-side gloss on the seed page; the text gives it as a designation of Brahman).
- Thibaut's "intuition" for *anubhava* without the word test.
- "India anticipated Hume/Wittgenstein/Kant" or any priority framing, including the Kālāma Sutta as "the first charter of free inquiry".
- The Kālāma Sutta stretched to a general epistemology (its scope is conduct).
- The Cārvāka position stated as their own words (it comes through hostile doxography).
- Jaina *anekānta* called "relativism" as if that were the tradition's term.
- "Indian philosophy is spiritual" as fact (Radhakrishnan's thesis, one side of the dispute).
- My *dṛṣṭi*/*darśana* root observation unless a scholar makes it.
- Scholarly details: Muṇḍaka manuscript variants beyond a clause; the Prāsaṅgika/Svātantrika history (on `madhyamaka`); the authorship layers of the *Tattvārthasūtra*; PV chapter-order disputes.
