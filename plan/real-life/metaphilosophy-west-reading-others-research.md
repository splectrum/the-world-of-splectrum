# Metaphilosophy, the West reading the other traditions: research note

Purpose: to source a group of pages in the Western section on how Western thinkers and scholars judged whether, and in what sense, other traditions had philosophy. The group sits outside the Western line, with a timeline of its own. The other direction, each tradition receiving the West, is out of scope. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked this round against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary itself was not opened. **[C as reported]** = a secondary's statement of fact or paraphrase, checked in that secondary. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a source I could not open). Translations marked "my rendering" are mine from the fetched original.

Voice for the pages: the Western thinkers are the subject, so their own words carry the lines; the scholars (Halbfass, Park, Defoort and others) supply facts and the shape of the disputes.

## 1. Open interest (1650s–1750s)

**The origin question inherited from antiquity.** Diogenes Laertius opens: "There are some who say that the study of philosophy had its beginning among the barbarians." The list runs Persian Magi, Chaldaeans, Indian Gymnosophists, Druids. His answer: "These authors forget that the achievements which they attribute to the barbarians belong to the Greeks, with whom not merely philosophy but the human race itself began" (I.1–3, tr. Hicks) [C, W7]. Halbfass: the rediscovery of Diogenes, "from Walter de Burleigh (fourteenth century) to J. Brucker (1696-1770) and beyond", renewed "the question of the potential Oriental roots of Western thought" [C, W9 ch. 9].

**Early modern histories split on the origin** [all C as reported, W9 ch. 9, pp. 147–148]:
- Thomas Stanley "was content to begin his history of philosophy with Thales" and set Oriental philosophy at the end. Its closing part, on "barbarian" philosophy, appeared in Le Clerc's Latin as *Historia philosophiae orientalis* (Amsterdam 1690). The English volumes are dated 1655–60, and *The History of the Chaldaick Philosophy* 1662 [C, W8, archive.org records].
- G. Hornius, *Historiae philosophicae libri VII* (Leiden 1655), "portrayed Adam as the first philosopher".
- G. J. Vossius, *De philosophia et philosophorum sectis* (1657): philosophy "derived from the Orient".
- Two early opinions against including Oriental thought (A. Gravius, P. S. Regis) came "from the Cartesian camp".

**The Jesuits' *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* (Paris 1687).**
- **Title page:** *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus, sive Scientia Sinensis Latine exposita*, "Studio & Opera Prosperi Intorcetta, Christiani Herdtrich, Francisci Rougemont, Philippi Couplet, Patrum Societatis Jesu", "Jussu Ludovici Magni … e Bibliotheca Regia in lucem prodit", Paris, Daniel Horthemels, 1687, with Couplet's *Tabula chronologica* [C, W1, title page, Getty copy].
- **Contents:** it translates three of the Four Books, in the order Daxue (*Ta hio*), Zhongyong (*Chum yum*) and Lunyu (*Lun yu*) [C, W1, "Finis Libri Ta Hio", "Finis Libri tertii Lun yu"]. The archive.org catalogue note lists them in another order; the book's own explicits decide it.
- **The dedication to Louis XIV** calls Confucius "Moralis Philosophiae pariter ac Politicae Magister & Oraculum", master and oracle of moral and political philosophy alike [C, W1; my rendering].
- **Word test.** "Philosophus" is the Jesuits' word. The Latin name "Confucius" is itself a Jesuit forging from "Kong Fuzi" (Granado's abstract) [C as reported, W5].
- **Not uniform.** Four years earlier, Andrea Lubelli SJ wrote to the General in Rome (15 December 1683) that "in China there is no philosophy in whatever form, unless one takes into account some philosophical sayings" (tr. Eager, from Zhang) [C as quoted, W19]. The Jesuit "philosophus" names a moral master. The denial measures by the Aristotelian sciences the Jesuits were translating into Chinese.

**Leibniz.** Preface to *Novissima Sinica* (1697; 2nd ed. 1699, Latin) [C, W2, 1699 text; the 1697 first edition not opened]:
- Fate has gathered "maximus generis humani cultus ornatusque" in the two extremes of the continent, "Europa et Tschina".
- Europe excels in logic, metaphysics, knowledge of incorporeal things and mathematics. The Chinese "seem to have been ignorant of that great light of the mind, the art of demonstrating" ("ignorasse magnum illud mentis lumen, artem demonstrandi").
- "certe practica philosophia (quod propemodum fateri pudet) victi sumus, id est Ethicae et Politicae praeceptis": in practical philosophy, "which it is almost shameful to admit", we are beaten.
- It "seems almost necessary" that Chinese missionaries be sent to us "to teach us the use and practice of natural theology, as we send them those who teach revealed theology" ("qui nos Theologiae naturalis usum praxinque doceant, quemadmodum nos illis mittimus qui Theologiam eos doceant revelatam"). (My renderings throughout; the 1699 OCR is poor but the sentences are legible.)

His late *Discours sur la théologie naturelle des Chinois* is an unfinished letter to Nicolas de Rémond, dated 1716 in the record. It answers Longobardi's *Traités* (1701) and Antonio de Santa Maria Caballero's *Traité* (1668) [C, W3]. English: Rosemont and Cook (1977); Cook and Rosemont, *Writings on China* (1994) [C as listed, W3 search results; U for contents].

**Christian Wolff's Halle oration (1721).**
- **The oration:** delivered on 12 July 1721 as outgoing prorector, it "upheld the compatibility of Confucian moral philosophy with the acquisition of virtue", and so denied that revelation was indispensable for morality [C, W4, SEP].
- **The escalation:** Wolff refused to submit the text to the theology faculty.
- **The expulsion:** on 8 November 1723 the king removed him and ordered him "to leave the lands of Electoral Brandenburg within 48 hours on pain of hanging". The edict reached Halle on 12 November [C, W4].
- **The return:** Wolff returned on 6 December 1740 under Friedrich II [C, W4].
- **Printing:** an annotated Latin edition appeared in 1726 [C as reported, W5]. The Latin title is *Oratio de Sinarum philosophia practica* [C, W9 ch. 4, W5].
- **His claim, as Granado renders it from Albrecht's Latin–German edition (Meiner 1985):** the Chinese, lacking any concept of God, could practise only "philosophical virtue", governed "by the guide of reason" alone and attributable "only to the powers of nature" ("fuerzas de la naturaleza", *vires naturae*). Providence placed Confucius among them "como un maestro y un profeta", as a teacher and a prophet [C as quoted, W5, Spanish rendering of Wolff, Oratio p. 26]. The English of these lines is mine, from Granado's Spanish. A page should quote Albrecht's edition or an English translation instead.
- **Headline:** the Chinese case was argued as philosophy, practical philosophy, and the dispute was over what reason can do without revelation, not over whether the Chinese thought.

**Brucker, *Historia critica philosophiae* (Leipzig 1742–44).**
- **The frame.** Brucker divides the first period into barbarian and Greek philosophy ("in philosophiae barbaricae et graecae partes duas") [C, W6 vol. 1]. Barbarian philosophy was "simplex admodum", "very simple", held to the first rules of wisdom, and handed down "traditione magis, quam demonstratione", more by tradition than by demonstration, until it reached the Greeks. Those "qui primos homines magnos philosophos … somniant", who dream that the first men were great philosophers, are "wonderfully deceived" [C, W6 vol. 1; my rendering]. He opens with "De philosophia antediluviana" [C, W6]. He reports that Epicurus held "solos Graecos posse philosophari", that only Greeks can philosophise [C, W6 vol. 1].
- **The last volume.** Tome IV, part 2 (1744) ends with a "Liber tertius de philosophia exotica": the Asian peoples in general, the "gentis Malabaricae", the Chinese, the Japanese, and "De philosophia Canadensium" [C, W6 tom. IV.2].
- **The Chinese chapter** sets the admirers against the deniers. The admirers are the Jesuits, Vossius, Spizel, "incomparabilis Leibnizius", Wolff and Bülfinger. The deniers are Renaudot, the friars collected by Arnauld, and the Jesuit Le Comte. Brucker favours Renaudot and Minorelli as freer "a praeiudicio peregrinae admirationis et credulitatis", from the prejudice of admiring the foreign and of credulity [C, W6 tom. IV.2, ch. 3 §1].
- **Halbfass's summary:** Brucker "ultimately finds philosophy in its complete sense and with the 'correct manner of philosophizing' only among the Greeks and the traditions which followed" [C as reported, W9 p. 149].
- **Word test:** *barbarica* (after the Greeks' word) and *exotica* are Brucker's categories.

**Reception of an Arabic text as philosophy of nature.** Ibn Ṭufayl's *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* reached Europe as Pococke's Latin *Philosophus Autodidactus* (1671). English versions followed from the Latin (Keith 1674, Ashwell 1686) and from the Arabic (Ockley 1708, subtitled "by the meer Light of Nature", with an appendix against "Enthusiasts") [U this round: carried from an earlier check against the headnote in Lerner and Mahdi (eds.), *Medieval Political Philosophy* (1963); not re-opened].

## 2. The narrowing, c. 1780–1830, and the Romantic counter-current

**The scholarship that frames the period.**
- **Park.** Peter K. J. Park, *Africa, Asia, and the History of Philosophy: Racism in the Formation of the Philosophical Canon, 1780–1830* (SUNY series Philosophy and Race; hardcover June 2013, paperback January 2014) [C, W10].
  - **Publisher's summary:** Kant-influenced historians recast the history as a "march of progress from the Greeks to Kant", supplanting accounts that began in Egypt or Western Asia. The other traditions were "relegated to the study of religion" [C, W10].
  - **Chapters:** the Kantian school; de Gérando's *Histoire comparée des systèmes de philosophie* as "The Birth of Comparative History of Philosophy"; India in Schlegel; the formation of the Kantian position; Ast and Rixner's "Systematic Inclusion"; Hegel "Reverts to the Kantian Position"; Tholuck's polemic against Hegel [C, W10].
  - **His questions:** "When and how did the history of philosophy become the story of Europe, of the West, of Greeks and Germans?" (pp. 5–6). And "By recounting philosophy's past (what philosophy was), the history of philosophy teaches what philosophy is (the concept of philosophy)" (p. 1) [C as quoted, W11].
  - **The reviewer's summary:** Park names Christoph Meiners as founder of the reordering, with race-based arguments passed on after Kant to Tiedemann and Tennemann. Hegel is the high point of the exclusion, not its start, against Halbfass (pp. 149–150) [C as reported, W11].
- **The two scholars disagree on Hegel's place.**
  - Halbfass: Hegel's segregation of philosophy from religion "contributed to a restrictive use of the concept of philosophy". The academic historians then dropped Hegel's world-historical horizon and pursued philosophy in its "true," "actual" sense [C, W9 p. 146].
  - Halbfass also notes "an attitude which Hegel occasionally gave utterance to during his later years": "the willingness to concede India an 'actual' philosophy" [C, W9 p. 146].
  - **Headline:** Halbfass sees Hegel as the turn; Park sees him returning to a Kantian position already formed. This changes the headline, so a page can name both readings in one sentence.

**Meiners, *Grundriß der Geschichte der Weltweisheit* (Lemgo 1786)** [C, W15, Fraktur OCR, early pages; page numbers not legible]:
- "kein alter Griechischer Schriftsteller die Wissenschaften seines Volkes [den Bar]baren zuschreibt", no ancient Greek writer ascribes his people's sciences to the barbarians. On the contrary, several give "ausdrückliche Zeugnisse über die Kindheit und Mittelmässigkeit der Kenntnisse Asiatischer und Afrikanischer Völker", express testimony to the infancy and mediocrity of the knowledge of Asian and African peoples.
- Egypt's priestly sciences stayed "in einer beständigen Kindheit", in a permanent childhood. "Weder das Klima, noch die Regierungsform, noch endlich die Religion" favoured the arts and sciences there (my renderings).
- His racial classification is [U]: "Celtic" against "Mongolian" in the *Grundriß der Geschichte der Menschheit* (1785). Park's account of it was not opened.

**Kant** [C, W12, W13]:
- ***Logik*, ed. Jäsche (1800), Introduction IV, "Kurzer Abriß einer Geschichte der Philosophie"** (AA IX 27). Philosophical knowledge is "speculative Erkenntniß der Vernunft", the knowledge of the universal "in abstracto". Hence "Unter allen Völkern haben also die Griechen erst angefangen zu philosophiren": of all peoples the Greeks first began to philosophise. The others made concepts intelligible only "durch Bilder in concreto", "such as the Chinese and some Indians" today, who treat God and the soul's immortality "but do not seek to investigate the nature of these objects according to concepts and rules in abstracto". The Persians and Arabs had some speculative use of reason, but borrowed its rules "vom Aristoteles". In Zoroaster's Zendavesta "not the slightest trace of philosophy". Egyptian wisdom was, compared with Greek philosophy, "ein bloßes Kinderspiel" (my renderings).
- **Word test:** the *Logik* is Jäsche's compilation from Kant's lecture notes.
- ***Physische Geographie*, ed. Rink (1802), on China** (AA IX 382): "Man verehrt auch den Confucius oder Con=fu=tse, den chinesischen Sokrates." Rink's edition is also compiled from notes.
- **The Orient sentence** [C as quoted, W14, two secondaries quoting Glasenapp, *Kant und die Religionen des Osten*, 1954, pp. 105–106]: "Philosophy is not to be found in the whole Orient … Their teacher Confucius teaches in his writings nothing outside a moral doctrine designed for the princes … But a concept of virtue and morality never entered the heads of the Chinese" (tr. Julia Ching). The German as quoted: "Philosophie ist im ganzen Orient nicht anzutreffen, die wenigen Araber ausgenommen …". Which lecture transcript Glasenapp quotes is [U].

**Tiedemann.** He excluded the Orient on a concept of "eigentliche Philosophie", philosophy proper [C as reported, W9 p. 149]. Tennemann names him as the one historian who "would exclude the Orientals" [C, W16]. *Geist der spekulativen Philosophie* (1791–97) itself was not opened [U].

**Tennemann.** *Grundriß der Geschichte der Philosophie*, in Arthur Johnson's translation, *A Manual of the History of Philosophy* (Oxford: Talboys, 1832), General Introduction §§16–20 [C, W16]:
- "all nations have not an equal claim to a place in the history of this science" (§17).
- "The Greeks are the nation whose originality of genius has created an era in the history of this science" (§18).
- "The Orientals … never attained to the same eminence". "Their doctrines were constantly invested with the character of Revelation". "Their climate, their political constitution, and despotic governments, with the institution of castes, were often obstacles to the free development of the mind" (§19).
- "The true commencement, therefore, of the history of philosophy must be sought among the Greeks … the epoch of Thales" (§20).
- He still prefaces Greek philosophy with "a brief review" of the Oriental nations (§20 Observation; the Particular Introduction covers Hindus, Persians, Chaldaeans, Egyptians, Phoenicians and Chinese) [C, W16].
- §16 Observation: the Egyptians as the primitive people "have recently (since the writings of Fred. Schlegel), been displaced by the Hindoos" [C, W16].
- Halbfass gives the German sense, which agrees: Oriental wisdom "still conveyed the character of divine revelation"; "the philosophizing which we cannot deny them" had not led them "to any philosophy" [C as quoted, W9 pp. 149–150].

**Hegel, *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*.** The text is Michelet's edition of the lecture notes, in E. S. Haldane's translation (1892) [C for the wording, W17; the edition basis U]. Halbfass's renderings from the German sometimes differ.
- **The beginning:** "Philosophy proper commences in the West … Thus in the West we are upon the soil of a veritable Philosophy" (Introduction B.3.c) [C, W17].
- **The Orient:** "no philosophic knowledge can be found here … The Eastern form must therefore be excluded from the History of Philosophy, but still, upon the whole, I will take some notice of it" (B.3.b) [C, W17].
- **Oriental philosophy** "does not enter into the substance or range of our subject … Its position is preliminary". What is called Eastern philosophy "is more properly the religious mode of thought and the conception of the world belonging generally to the Orientals" [C, W17, "Oriental Philosophy"].
- **Confucius:** "only a man who has a certain amount of practical and worldly wisdom — one with whom there is no speculative philosophy … for their reputation it would have been better had they never been translated". Cicero's *De Officiis* is "more comprehensive and better than all the books of Confucius" [C, W17].
- **India:** "in modern times men have learned to recognize real philosophic writings", through Colebrooke. The Nyāya "may be compared to the Logic of Aristotle" [C, W17]. Of Schlegel: what he "says about the wisdom of the Indians is taken from their religious ideas only" [C, W17].
- **Arabian philosophy** "flourished to an extraordinary degree, in spite of its here not displaying any specially characteristic features. … What we have to say, however, chiefly concerns the external preservation and propagation of Philosophy" [C, W17, "Arabian Philosophy"].
- **As Halbfass quotes Hegel:** "The real philosophy begins only in the Occident. … Philosophy can exist only here; and hence we have free constitutions only in the Occident". "abstract and negative liberation" is **Halbfass's paraphrase**, not a quotation: "What the Indians call moksa is, according to Hegel, only an abstract and negative liberation" [C, W9 pp. 92–93]. For Halbfass, Hegel's Orient "is essentially beginning, introduction, preparation" [C, W9 p. 88].
- **Lectures on the Philosophy of World History**, vol. 1 (1822–3; tr. R. F. Brown and P. C. Hodgson, Clarendon 2011, pp. 240–241): Confucius "was a moralist as such, not actually a philosopher; for in his case we do not find theory that occupies itself in thought as such … He is not to be compared to Plato, Aristotle, or Socrates" [C as quoted, W19]. **This is the source of the "moralist" line, not the History of Philosophy and not Halbfass** (section 7).
- **Word test:** Haldane's "Philosophy proper" and Halbfass's "real philosophy" both render *eigentliche Philosophie* [U for Hegel's German at this place]. Tiedemann's term is the same.

**The Romantic counter-current.**
- **Friedrich Schlegel, *Ueber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier* (1808)** [C, W20; publisher U]. Book II, "Von der Philosophie", has chapters on transmigration and emanation, astrological superstition and wild nature worship, the two principles, and pantheism. He asks the reader to take its parts "nicht als philosophische Systeme", not as philosophical systems, but as epochs of "orientalischen Denkart", since "they were all originally more than mere philosophy" ("ursprünglich alle mehr als bloß Philosophie") [C, W20; my rendering]. Park reads Schlegel's as the first history to treat Asian and European philosophy on a common basis, "his defence of Indian philosophy as genuine philosophy" [C as reported, W11].
- **The Oupnek'hat.** Anquetil-Duperron's Latin *Oupnek'hat*, 2 vols (Strasbourg/Paris, 1801–1802) [C for the volumes and dates, W23; place U]. It was made from Dārā Shikōh's Persian [U].
- **Schopenhauer, Preface to the first edition of *The World as Will and Representation* (1818), tr. Haldane and Kemp (1883)** [C, W21]:
  - "the access to which, opened to us through the Upanishads, is in my eyes the greatest advantage which this still young century enjoys over previous ones".
  - Each of the Upanishads' "individual and disconnected aphorisms … may be deduced as a consequence from the thought I am going to impart, though the converse, that my thought is to be found in the Upanishads, is by no means the case".
- ***Parerga* II §185, in Max Müller's English** [C as quoted, W22, SBE 1, 1879]: "In the whole world there is no study, except that of the originals, so beneficial and so elevating as that of the Oupnekhat. It has been the solace of my life, it will be the solace of my death!"
- **Word test:** Schopenhauer's text is the Oupnek'hat, a Latin made from a Persian version, not the Sanskrit.

## 3. The colonial frame (1830s–1950s)

**Hegel, *Philosophy of History*, tr. Sibree** [C, W18]:
- **Africa:** "At this point we leave Africa, not to mention it again. For it is no historical part of the World; it has no movement or development to exhibit." Egypt "does not belong to the African Spirit". "What we properly understand by Africa, is the Unhistorical, Undeveloped Spirit, still involved in the conditions of mere nature".
- **America:** "What has taken place in the New World up to the present time is only an echo of the Old World—the expression of a foreign Life; and as a Land of the Future, it has no interest for us here."
- On a page, Africa can be stated in Hegel's words, not only in Diagne's paraphrase, once `hegel` carries them.

**Renan on "Arab" philosophy (Sorbonne lecture, 29 March 1883; *L'Islamisme et la science*, Calmann Lévy 1883).** "Ce grand ensemble philosophique, que l'on a coutume d'appeler arabe, parce qu'il est écrit en arabe, mais qui est en réalité gréco-sassanide. Il serait plus exact de dire grec": the great philosophical whole usually called Arab "is in reality Graeco-Sasanian. It would be more exact to say Greek" [C, W42, Wikisource text via search; my rendering]. The lecture date and publisher, and the Averroes/Albertus sentence, are [U this round; carried from an earlier check against the same Wikisource text]. The decline-after-Ghazālī reading, and Griffel's refutation, are already on `islamic-world` [C, site].

**Lévy-Bruhl, *Les fonctions mentales dans les sociétés inférieures* (Paris: Alcan, 1910), ch. 2** [C, W24, p. 79 and the following pages]:
- The mentality of "primitives" "peut être dite prélogique à aussi juste titre que mystique".
- "la mentalité des sociétés de type inférieur, que j'appelle prélogique, faute d'un nom meilleur … Elle n'est pas antilogique; elle n'est pas non plus alogique. En l'appelant prélogique, je veux seulement dire qu'elle ne s'astreint pas avant tout, comme notre pensée, à s'abstenir de la contradiction. Elle obéit d'abord à la loi de participation": prelogical "for want of a better name"; neither antilogical nor alogical; it does not bind itself, as our thought does, to avoid contradiction, and obeys first the law of participation.
- **Word test:** "prélogique" is his own coinage, offered "faute d'un nom meilleur". English: *How Natives Think* (1926) [C, archive.org record].
- **The notebooks** (*Carnets*, posthumous, 1949). Encyclopedia entries differ on how far he went [C as reported, W25]:
  - Cazeneuve: he "was prepared to give up the term 'prelogical'" and "even questioned the specificity" of the characteristics.
  - Rivière: the *Carnets* "considerably tempers the difference".
  - Fischoff: he "retracted this idea".
  - His own wording is [U]. The 1975 English, *The Notebooks on Primitive Mentality* (tr. Rivière), is lending-only.

**Tempels, *La philosophie bantoue*, as read in Europe** [C as reported, W26, Hountondji 1983 extract]:
- Tempels set out "to correct a certain image of the black man disseminated by Levy-Bruhl and his school".
- The book "is not addressed to Africans but to Europeans, and particularly to two categories of Europeans: colonials and missionaries". Its last chapter is "Bantu philosophy and our mission to civilize".
- The Paris edition was Présence Africaine, 1949, with Alioune Diop's preface [C as listed, W26 bibliography].
- The 1945 first edition, the Dutch 1946 and the English of 1959 (tr. Colin King) are [U].
- Tempels's own sentence that the Bantu cannot formulate their philosophy and that "it is our job" to do it is [U]: the 1959 English is lending-only on archive.org.
- **Word test:** "philosophie" is the missionary's word.

**The twentieth-century claim that philosophy is Greek in essence.**
- **Husserl, Vienna lecture (1935).** In Hayes's paraphrase, Husserl granted Indian and Chinese thinkers a search for universal truths, vocational communities and transmission, but held that philosophy is "a purely 'theoretical' attitude", and that Indian and Chinese thought stayed with practical and mythical ends [C as reported, W32]. His own wording ("it is a mistake…") is [U]: Hua VI and the Carr translation not opened.
- **Heidegger, *Was ist das – die Philosophie?* (Cerisy 1955; Neske 1956).** "the often-heard phrase 'Western-European philosophy' is in truth a tautology" [U; text lending-only]. The Derrida remark below aligns with this line as Zheng Jiadong reports it [C as reported, on `derrida`, site].
- **Jaspers, against the single origin.** *Vom Ursprung und Ziel der Geschichte* (1949), tr. Michael Bullock, *The Origin and Goal of History* (1953). For the "Axial Period" "between 800 and 200 B.C.", "Confucius and Lao-tse were living in China, all the schools of Chinese philosophy came into being … India produced the Upanishads and Buddha and, like China, ran the whole gamut of philosophical possibilities down to scepticism, to materialism, sophism and nihilism" [C, W31, from a private upload of the Routledge reprint; cite the printed book]. That he set the Axial Period against Hegel's Christian axis is [U for wording].

## 4. Comparative philosophy (1923–)

**Masson-Oursel, *La philosophie comparée* (Paris, 1923, thesis); English *Comparative Philosophy* (Kegan Paul, 1926; Part II tr. V. C. C. Collum), dedicated "to my master M. Lévy Bruhl"** [C, W27]:
- Only three branches of humanity developed "en constant parallélisme au cours d'environ trois millénaires": "l'Europe, l'Inde et la Chine".
- "L'Europe s'impose comme point de repère, attendu que nous ne saurions comprendre autrui que relativement à nous, même si nous apprenons à ne pas juger autrui par nous-mêmes": Europe is the reference point, since we can understand others only relative to ourselves, even if we learn not to judge others by ourselves (my rendering).
- The method is "analogy", "a likeness of relations" that "in no sense implies assimilation of different terms".
- He refuses to apply the Western ancient/medieval/modern division to China and India, which "never cut up into sections the continuous thread of their traditions".
- **Precedent:** Park calls de Gérando's *Histoire comparée des systèmes de philosophie* the "birth of comparative history of philosophy" [C, W10]. Its date (1804) is [U].

**The East–West Philosophers' Conferences, Honolulu.**
- The first, in 1939, was initiated by Charles A. Moore, Wing-tsit Chan and Gregg Sinclair. "Its aim was to explore the significance of Eastern ways of thinking as a complement to Western thought" [C, W28].
- Later conferences: 1949, 1959, 1964, 1969, 1989, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2011, 2016 and 2024 [C, W28].
- 1939 papers by Chan, Conger, Hocking, Moore, Northrop, Sakamaki, Suzuki and Takakusu [C, W29].
- **Moore's preface to the proceedings** (*Philosophy—East and West*, Princeton UP, copyright 1944; second printing 1946) [C, W30]:
  - "The underlying purpose was to determine the possibility of a world philosophy through a synthesis of the ideas and ideals of East and West".
  - A "constant theme": "neither Orient nor Occident is philosophically self-sufficient, each lacking that total perspective which is characteristic of philosophy".
  - "the 'East' consists primarily of India, China, and Japan". "no Indian scholar was available". Hocking and Suzuki did not attend.
- *Philosophy East and West* "was founded in 1951" [C, W28]. That Moore founded and edited it is [U, tertiary only].
- **Word test:** "synthesis" and "world philosophy" are Moore's terms.

## 5. The present disputes (1950s–)

**India.**
- **Jacobi, Hacker, Halbfass** [C as quoted, W9 pp. 273–286]:
  - Hermann Jacobi rendered *ānvīkṣikī* as "philosophy". Hacker (1958): Jacobi "was obviously so impressed with the Occidental emancipation of philosophy from theology that he projected this conflict into his own area of research, India".
  - Hacker's conclusion: Hindu culture "knew the concept of philosophy, but no word which expressed this concept fully and exclusively".
  - Halbfass: neither *darśana* nor *ānvīkṣikī* "has played a role in India which would be historically comparable to the role of 'philosophy' in Europe". "Today, it is no longer necessary to argue that there was philosophy in India." India had "constructive metaphysics as well as critical epistemology, ontology as well as linguistic analysis", with "a level of reflexivity and conceptual articulation which is fully commensurable with what we find in Europe" [C, W9 p. 286].
  - Hacker's article title and year and Jacobi's year (c. 1911) are [U]; Halbfass gives the references.
- **Halbfass's own frame:** chapter 9, "On the Exclusion of India from the History of Philosophy". Paul Deussen's *Allgemeine Geschichte der Philosophie*, which included India, is "unique amid the literature of this field" [C, W9 p. 145]. Its volume dates are [U].

**China.**
- **Defoort**, "Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Philosophy? Arguments of an Implicit Debate", *Philosophy East and West* 51.3 (2001) 393–413 [C, W33]:
  - Universities, bookshops, journals and conferences "all confirm a vision that, in fact, they have seldom explicitly discussed" (p. 393).
  - First position (it does not exist): "primarily, though not exclusively, implicit and Western". Second (it exists): "best and most explicitly represented in contemporary China. But it also exists, although more implicitly, in the West". "even Hegel spoke of Chinese philosophy without attaching any positive significance to it".
  - Third position (redefine the content): "held above all by sinologists with philosophical training and, analogously, by philosophers with sinological training".
  - Fourth position (question the project, "why should China even have had a philosophy?"): "predominantly Chinese in its representation".
  - The paradox: "'Chinese philosophy' seems to have died of its own birth". The case "acts as a case study for a wider problematic".
  - **Correction:** the wording "seldom explicitly stated", used in earlier drafts, is not hers.
- **Derrida, 2001.** In conversation with Wang Yuanhua during the China visit (organised by the French General Consulate in Shanghai): "中国没有哲学，但/只有思想". The French original "has not been preserved", and Zhang gives the "but/only" readings [C, W19]. Already on `derrida` [C, site].

**Japan.**
- **Kasulis.** The closing section of the SEP entry (Kasulis and Bouso, revised 11 January 2026), "Metaphilosophy: Philosophy as *Wissenschaft* or Way?": "When Japanese philosophy considers itself a Way rather than a *Wissenschaft*, it stresses engagement over detachment" [C, W35 §5.1].
- **Maraldo** (Prologue, *Japanese Philosophy in the Making 1*, 2017) [C, W34]:
  - The first of his four senses: "philosophy of a Greco-European vintage distilled by people who happen to be Japanese". The distinctive-originality sense has been criticised as "inverted orientalism".
  - "The name Indian philosophy meets almost no resistance today, but whether Chinese thought should be called philosophy is a matter of controversy. Even more controversial is the question whether pre-modern Japanese thought counts as philosophy".
  - "Philosophy in Japan not only avoids ethnocentric connotations; it is also more balanced and inclusive." "If there is a defining characteristic to philosophy through the ages and across cultures, surely it is the habit of interrogating given definitions."

**Africa.**
- **Lefkowitz and Bernal.** Mary Lefkowitz, *Not Out of Africa* (New Republic and Basic Books, 1996) [C, W36 header].
  - Tritle's review: "Aristotle died before the Library at Alexandria was even built", and "As Lefkowitz acutely notes, philosophy and related ideas can not really be 'stolen'" [C, W37, BMCR 1996.05.07].
  - Bernal's review (BMCR 1996.04.05): "there is no doubt that the Afrocentrists have been wrong on many particulars". "The term 'philosophy' is extraordinarily slippery but taken in the Socratic sense of 'wonder, or speculation on truth and reality,' there is every reason to suppose that it was present in Ancient Egypt. Indeed, it was conventional wisdom among Greeks and Romans that philosophy had derived from Egypt." He places the Afrocentrists in what *Black Athena* called "the Ancient Model" [C, W36].
- **Headline:** Bernal's "Ancient Model" and Park's "accounts that began in Egypt or Western Asia" name the same pre-1780 history from two sides. No priority claim follows from either.

**Latin America.**
- **SEP (Gracia and Vargas):** of pre-Columbian thought, "Whether this body of work is rightly characterized as philosophy or something else is a disputed matter". The debate's four approaches: "universalist, culturalist, critical, and ethnic" [C, W38].
- **SEP (Nuccetelli, revised 27 October 2025):** "Nuccetelli (2011, 2020) argues that pre-Columbian works of philosophical import are proto-philosophy of the non-academic variety. Gracia (2010) defers taking a position" [C, W39].
- **Maffie, *Aztec Philosophy* (2014)**, introduction p. 6: the idea that philosophy is exclusively Western "plays a vital role in the West's conception of itself" [C as quoted, W43]. A reviewer says he "respectfully unbinds" León-Portilla's reading from "the trappings of Western discourses" (the reviewer's words) [C, W44].
- **Placement note:** Gracia, Vargas and Nuccetelli are US-based scholars of Latin American philosophy who take part in the Latin American debate. Whether they count as "the West reading" or as voices inside the tradition is a placement call (section 6).

**The Islamic world.** Adamson's "philosophy in the Islamic world", "a neutral designation", against "Islamic" and "Arabic" [U this round: the Five Books interview URL now returns 404; already on `islamic-world`, C on the site]. Griffel's revision and Sabra's "naturalization" (1987), the Gutas–Corbin/Nasr dispute over "Eastern" or "Oriental" philosophy, Strauss's esoteric-writing reading, and Rizvi applying Hadot's "way of life" to Mullā Ṣadrā are Western framings. All are [U this round: carried, not re-opened].

**The profession.**
- **Garfield and Van Norden, "If Philosophy Won't Diversify, Let's Call It What It Really Is"** (*The Stone*, *NYT*, 11 May 2016) [C, W40, via the Wayback copy]:
  - "of the 118 doctoral programs in philosophy in the United States and Canada, only 10 percent have a specialist in Chinese philosophy". Of "the top 50 philosophy doctoral programs in the English-speaking world, only 15 percent have any regular faculty members who teach any non-Western philosophy".
  - "any department that regularly offers courses only on Western philosophy should rename itself 'Department of European and American Philosophy'".
- **Van Norden, *Taking Back Philosophy: A Multicultural Manifesto*** (Columbia UP, December 2017; foreword by Jay L. Garfield). The description says most departments "stubbornly insist that only Western philosophy is real philosophy". Chapters include "Trump's Philosophers" and "The Way of Confucius and Socrates" [C, W41].
- Not pursued: the Philosophical Gourmet Report and APA debates (see Discard).

## 6. Placement proposal (options, not decisions)

The group sits on the Western landing page outside the line, with its own dated timeline. The material falls into five periods. Three ways to cut it:

**Option A: five pages, one per period.**
1. *Open interest* (1655–1744): Diogenes's question, Stanley, Hornius and Vossius; the Jesuits' *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus* and Lubelli; Leibniz; Wolff; Brucker's barbarian and exotic philosophy; Pococke's *Philosophus Autodidactus*.
2. *Philosophy proper* (1786–1830s): Meiners, Kant, Tiedemann, Tennemann, Hegel; Park and Halbfass on the turn; Schlegel, the Oupnek'hat and Schopenhauer as the counter-current.
3. *The colonial frame* (1830s–1950s): Hegel on Africa and America; Renan; Lévy-Bruhl and the notebooks; Tempels in Paris; Husserl, Heidegger and, against them, Jaspers.
4. *Comparative philosophy* (1923–): Masson-Oursel; Honolulu 1939 and Moore's "world philosophy"; *Philosophy East and West*.
5. *The present disputes* (1950s–): Hacker and Halbfass; Defoort's four positions; Derrida; Kasulis and Maraldo; Lefkowitz and Bernal; Maffie, Nuccetelli, Gracia and Vargas; the Islamic naming and decline debates; Garfield and Van Norden.

For: matches the timeline. Each page has a clear date span and a primary voice (Brucker, Hegel, Lévy-Bruhl, Moore, Defoort). Against: page 4 is thin unless Northrop or later Honolulu material is added.

**Option B: four pages.** As A, but fold comparative philosophy into the present disputes ("Comparison and its critics", 1923–), since Defoort's third position and the Honolulu "synthesis" are one strand. For: no thin page. Against: Masson-Oursel and 1939 sit beside 2016 with no break.

**Option C: three pages on the arc of the word.**
1. *Barbarian and exotic philosophy* (to 1780).
2. *Philosophy proper* (1786–1950s): the narrowing and the colonial frame together, ending with Husserl and Heidegger.
3. *After philosophy proper* (1923–): Jaspers, comparison, the present disputes.

For: follows the term that does the work (*eigentliche Philosophie*). Against: page 2 becomes long, and the 1830–1950 colonial material loses its own frame.

**Landing timeline.** Dates checked in this note: 1655, 1687, 1699 (Leibniz's second edition), 1716, 1721, 1723, 1742–44, 1786, 1801–02, 1808, 1818, 1832, 1910, 1923, 1939, 1944, 1949 (Tempels in Paris), 1951, 1953 (Jaspers in English), 1988, 2001 (two), 2013, 2016, 2017.
