# The history of natural philosophy: research note

Purpose: to gather, period by period, what the thinkers themselves said about how knowledge of nature is gained, with the medieval arts faculty, the word histories, McGrath, the Cunningham dispute and the Whitehead and Smolin backing alongside. This is for a set of pieces that tell natural philosophy's history through the changing emphasis in how knowledge is acquired. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked, but in a secondary source that quotes or summarises it, not in the text itself. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). "My rendering" = my English from the original. OCR scans are quoted with obvious OCR errors corrected and nothing else changed.

## 1. The thread: how knowledge of nature was gained, period by period

**Headline.** The emphasis does move: from seeing and inquiring (the Greek *historia*, *theōria*, *aisthēsis*), to reading and commenting on authoritative texts (the arts faculty), to experiment and mathematical method (the seventeenth century), to the question of what makes knowledge "proper science" (Kant), and then to division of labour (the nineteenth century). The material does not support a clean sequence, though. Each period has its counter-voice: Aristotle reads his predecessors; Grosseteste and Roger Bacon insist on experience inside the reading culture; Bacon still builds on "natural history"; Galileo's mathematics is a kind of reading ("the book of nature"). A page should say "emphasis", not "stage". Each period also names its method in its own words, which is what makes the thread workable.

### 1a. Greece and Rome: inquiry, seeing, argument

- **Herodotus: inquiry as sight, judgement and inquiry.** The proem: "This is the Showing forth of the Inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassos, to the end that neither the deeds of men may be forgotten by lapse of time…" (Macaulay trans.) [C, S1]. The Greek is *historiēs apodexis*; LSJ cites "Hdt.1 Praef." under *historia* in the sense "knowledge so obtained, information" [C, S30]. At 2.99: "Hitherto my own observation and judgment and inquiry are the vouchers for that which I have said; but from this point onwards I am about to tell the history of Egypt according to that which I heard" (Macaulay) [C, S1]. LSJ gives the Greek: *opsis emē kai gnōmē kai historiē*, sight, judgement and inquiry [C, S30]. Hearing is set against seeing in the same sentence.
- **Aristotle: from perception, through memory and experience, to principles.** *Posterior Analytics* II.19: "out of sense-perception comes to be what we call memory, and out of frequently repeated memories of the same thing develops experience … From experience … originate the skill of the craftsman and the knowledge of the man of science"; "we must get to know the primary premisses by induction; for the method by which even sense-perception implants the universal is inductive" (Mure trans.) [C, S2].
- **Aristotle: experience supplies the principles.** *Prior Analytics* I.30: "it is the business of experience to give the principles which belong to each subject. I mean for example that astronomical experience supplies the principles of astronomical science: for once the phenomena were adequately apprehended, the demonstrations of astronomy were discovered … if none of the true attributes of things had been omitted in the historical survey, we should be able to discover the proof" (Jenkinson trans.) [C, S3]. Word test: Jenkinson's "historical survey" presumably renders *historia* [U, the Greek not opened]. If so, *historia* here is the collected survey of facts that precedes demonstration.
- **Aristotle: start from what is clearer to us.** *Physics* I.1: "The natural way of doing this is to start from the things which are more knowable and obvious to us and proceed towards those which are clearer and more knowable by nature … it is a whole that is best known to sense-perception" (Hardie and Gaye trans.) [C, S4].
- **Aristotle: the senses are the final test.** *De caelo* III.7, against those who defend their principles at any cost: principles must "be judged from their results, and particularly from their final issue! And that issue, which in the case of productive knowledge is the product, in the knowledge of nature is the unimpeachable evidence of the senses as to each fact" (Stocks trans.) [C, S5]. *Generation of Animals* III.10, on bees: "the facts, however, have not yet been sufficiently grasped; if ever they are, then credit must be given rather to observation than to theories, and to theories only if what they affirm agrees with the observed facts" (Platt trans.) [C, S6]. Platt's own footnote is the word test: "*aisthēsis*, lit. sense-perception, or the evidence of the senses. Plato had maintained that *logos* was more trustworthy than *aisthēsis*" [C, S6]. "Observation" is the translator's word for perception.
- **Epicurus: the canon of sensation.** "in *The Canon* Epicurus affirms that our sensations and preconceptions and our feelings are the standards of truth"; "nor again can reason refute them, for reason is wholly dependent on sensation"; "Hence it is from plain facts that we must start when we draw inferences about the unknown" (Diogenes Laertius 10.31–32, Hicks trans.) [C, S7]. For the sky, he gives more than one explanation: "we must take into account the variety of ways in which analogous occurrences happen within our experience" (10.80), and "one must not be so much in love with the explanation by a single way as wrongly to reject all the others" (10.94–95, "the method of plural explanation") [C, S7]. LSJ lists Epicurus for *historia* as "systematic or scientific observation" [C, S30].
- **Cicero: the natural philosopher as watcher and hunter.** "physicum, id est speculatorem venatoremque naturae", the natural philosopher, that is, the watcher and hunter of nature (*De natura deorum* 1.83; my rendering) [C, S8]. The speaker is Cotta, Cicero's Academic, mocking the Epicurean appeal to habit [U as to the speaker; the dialogue's book 1 reply is Cotta's from memory].
- *Already on the site and usable:* Parmenides "judge by argument" against "eye" and "ear"; Pythagoras the "observer of nature"; *theōria* as spectating; Aristotle reading his predecessors (*Metaphysics* A.3).

### 1b. The Arabic and Latin Middle Ages: reading, commentary, and a counter-voice of experience

- **Ibn Rushd: Aristotle as the text to read.** The focus of his commentaries was Aristotle because "no one who has come after him to this our time—and this is close to 1500 years later—has been able to add a word worthy of attention to what he said" (Long Commentary on the *Physics*, proem, in Harvey's translation) [C as quoted, S9]. He wrote three kinds of commentary (epitome, paraphrase, long commentary), a classification SEP notes recent scholars doubt [C, S9]. Demonstration is "the most perfect kind of reflection (*naẓar*), using the most perfect kind of inference (*qiyās*)" (*Decisive Treatise* 3) [C as quoted, S9].
- **Ibn Sīnā: natural philosophy and its special sciences.** For him "the proper subject of natural philosophy, in its broadest or most general sense, is body insofar as it is subject to motion", and the special natural sciences are divided by kind of motion or kind of body, as the books of *The Cure* (*al-Shifāʾ*) are divided: the heavens, generation and corruption, meteorology, the soul, plants and animals [C as SEP's summary, S10]. His own statements of method in the *Physics* of *The Cure* (on proceeding from what is better known to us) I could not open [U]. The site already has his reading of the *Metaphysics* forty times and his call for "addition, correction, or revision" of the ancients.
- **Aquinas: natural philosophy as teaching heard.** "ens mobile sit subiectum naturalis philosophiae", mobile being is the subject of natural philosophy. The *Physics* "etiam dicitur de physico sive naturali auditu, quia per modum doctrinae ad audientes traditus fuit", is also called *On Natural Hearing*, because it was handed down by way of teaching to listeners (*In Physic.* I lect. 1 nn. 3–4; my rendering) [C, S11]. Against mere doxography: "studium philosophiae non est ad hoc quod sciatur quid homines senserint, sed qualiter se habeat veritas rerum", the study of philosophy is not for knowing what men have thought, but how the truth of things stands (*In De caelo* I lect. 22 n. 8; my rendering) [C, S11]. Both lines bear on reading: the course is a listening to texts, and its aim is still the things.
- **Grosseteste: experience and geometry inside the commentary.** In his *Posterior Analytics* commentary, repeated sensory experience stirs reason to form universals; he takes Avicenna's example of scammony and red bile and arrives "from sense at an observational (*experimentale*) universal principle" (Rossi 1981: 215) [C as quoted, S13]. On geometry: "Utilitas considerationis linearum, angulorum et figurarum est maxima, quoniam impossibile est sciri naturalem philosophiam sine illis", the study of lines, angles and figures is of the greatest use, since natural philosophy cannot be known without them (*De lineis, angulis et figuris*; my rendering) [C, S14]. With these rules "ex potestate geometriae, diligens inspector in rebus naturalibus potest dare causas omnium effectuum naturalium", from the power of geometry the careful observer of natural things can give the causes of all natural effects (*De natura locorum*, opening; my rendering) [C, S14].
  - *Reception, not priority:* Crombie (1953/1955) made Grosseteste the first in the Latin West to state an experimental method. SEP's verdict is that the scammony passage is "the sole reference to controlled experiments in his writings", that he relies on "authority and everyday observation (*experimentum*)", and that the mathematical claim "stands on firmer ground" [C, S13]. This changes the headline, so a page should carry it: medieval *experimentum* is experience, not the controlled experiment.
- **Roger Bacon: two ways of knowing.** "without experience nothing can be sufficiently known. For there are two modes of acquiring knowledge, namely, by reasoning and experience. Reasoning draws a conclusion and makes us grant the conclusion, but does not make the conclusion certain, nor does it remove doubt so that the mind may rest on the intuition of truth, unless the mind discovers it by the path of experience" (*Opus maius* VI.1, Burke trans. 1928) [C, S15]. The Latin: "sine experientia nihil sufficienter sciri potest. Duo enim sunt modi cognoscendi, scilicet per argumentum et experimentum" (Bridges ed.) [C, S16]. The title of Part VI is *De scientia experimentali* [C, S16]. Against reading without trying: "authors write many statements, and people believe them through reasoning which they formulate without experience … therefore all things must be verified by experience" (the diamond and goat's blood, hot water freezing) [C, S15]. Experience is "of two kinds": through the external senses (with instruments for the heavens, and other people's reports for distant places), and an inner illumination [C, S15].
  - *Word test:* *experimentum* and *experientia* alternate in his Latin, and Burke renders both "experience". "Experimental science" is a translator's rendering of *scientia experimentalis*, not the modern sense [C as to the Latin, S16]. *Opus maius* is usually dated 1267 [U].
- **Buridan: what is evident, and the common course of nature.** SEP (Zupko) reads him as making philosophy "a secular enterprise based on what is evident to the senses and intellect", with natural science "about what happens for the most part, i.e., assuming the common course of nature" [C, S17]. In his words, as SEP quotes them:
  - against theological additions: "one might assume that there are many more separate substances than there are celestial spheres … viz., great legions of angels [*magnae legiones angelorum*], but this cannot be proved by demonstrative arguments originating from sense perception" (*Questions on the Metaphysics* II.9) [C as quoted, S17];
  - on impetus: the projectile "would continue to be moved as long as the impetus remains stronger than the resistance, and would be of infinite duration were it not diminished and corrupted by some contrary force" [C as quoted, S17].
  - *Reception, not priority:* "It is too much to claim, as Duhem did, that he was a forerunner of Galileo" [C, S17]. I could not open a Buridan text directly [U]; see the fetch list.
- **Oresme: the senses cannot decide it.** In *Le livre du ciel et du monde* (c. 1377, a French translation of and commentary on *De caelo*) he argues that no observation proves the heavens turn rather than the earth, then concludes that "everyone maintains, and I think myself, that the heavens do move and not the Earth" [C as quoted by Wikipedia, citing Menut and Denomy's edition, p. 537, S18]. SEP: he "finished by affirming his belief in a stationary Earth" [C, S19]. The manuscript incipit is "La science naturelle presque tout est des corps et des magnitudes", natural science is almost wholly about bodies and magnitudes, Aristotle's opening of *De caelo* in French [C as catalogued, S20]. The Menut–Denomy text itself was not available [U].

### 1c. The seventeenth century: experiment and method

- **Bacon: from the senses upward, and natural philosophy as the root.**
  - "Man, as the minister and interpreter of nature, does and understands as much as his observations on the order of nature … permit him" (I.1); "Knowledge and human power are synonymous … nature is only subdued by submission" (I.3) (*Novum Organum*, Devey ed.) [C, S21].
  - The two ways: one "hurries on rapidly from the senses and particulars to the most general axioms"; the other "constructs its axioms from the senses and particulars, by ascending continually and gradually … which is the true but unattempted way" (I.19) [C, S21].
  - Experience and experiment: "There remains but mere experience, which, when it offers itself, is called chance; when it is sought after, experiment"; "the real order of experience begins by setting up a light" (I.82) [C, S21]. Against reading: the usual way of discovery "first inquires and obtains a full account of all that has been said on the subject by others" (I.82); natural history built on "mere reports of experience, traditions as it were of dreams" is like a state run on "the gossip of city and street politicians" (I.98) [C, S21].
  - Natural philosophy as "the great mother of the sciences", reduced in the Christian era to "a handmaid". And: "let no one expect any great progress in the sciences … unless natural philosophy be applied to particular sciences, and particular sciences again referred back to natural philosophy … these sciences, when they have been once partitioned out and established, are no longer nourished by natural philosophy … we can little wonder that the sciences grow not when separated from their roots" (I.79–80) [C, S21]. The Latin wording is not checked [U].
  - The bee (I.95) is already on the site.
- **Galileo: the book of nature against the book of a man.** The famous line answers a critic who treats philosophy as a matter of following an author: "forse stima che la filosofia sia un libro e una fantasia d'un uomo, come l'*Iliade* e l'*Orlando furioso*, libri ne' quali la meno importante cosa è che quello che vi è scritto sia vero. Signor Sarsi, la cosa non istà così. La filosofia è scritta in questo grandissimo libro che continuamente ci sta aperto innanzi a gli occhi (io dico l'universo), ma non si può intendere se prima non s'impara a intender la lingua, e conoscer i caratteri, ne' quali è scritto. Egli è scritto in lingua matematica, e i caratteri son triangoli, cerchi, ed altre figure geometriche" (*Il Saggiatore*, 1623, §6) [C, S22]. My rendering: "perhaps he thinks philosophy is a book and a fancy of some man, like the *Iliad* or *Orlando furioso*, books in which the least important thing is whether what is written there is true. Signor Sarsi, that is not how it is. Philosophy is written in this grand book that stands continually open before our eyes (I mean the universe), but it cannot be understood unless one first learns to understand the language and know the characters in which it is written. It is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles and other geometric figures." The passage is the hinge of the thread: reading is not given up but moved from the author's book to the universe, and the language changes to mathematics.
- **Galileo: sense-experiences and demonstrations before Scripture.** "nelle dispute di problemi naturali non si dovrebbe cominciare dalle autorità di luoghi delle Scritture, ma dalle sensate esperienze e dalle dimostrazioni necessarie", in disputes about natural questions one should begin not from the authority of passages of Scripture but from sense-experiences and necessary demonstrations, for Scripture and nature both proceed from the divine Word (letter to Christina of Lorraine; my rendering) [C, S23]. Dated 1615 [U].
- **Boyle: experiments rather than syllogisms, and witnessed reports.** "you are resolved on this occasion to insist rather on Experiments then Syllogismes", since the Schoolmen's "Dialectical subtleties … are wont much more to declare the wit of him that uses them, then increase the knowledge" (*The Sceptical Chymist*, London, 1661) [C, S24]. On reports: experiments should be set down "not of Prescriptions, … but of Relations", naming the author "upon whose credit they relate it" [C, S24].
- **Newton: phenomena, forces, rules.**
  - Author's preface: "all the difficulty of philosophy seems to consist in this—from the phænomena of motions to investigate the forces of nature, and then from these forces to demonstrate the other phænomena" (*Principia*, Motte trans., 1846 ed.) [C, S25].
  - Rules of Reasoning, Book III. "We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances" (I); "the qualities of bodies are only known to us by experiments … We are certainly not to relinquish the evidence of experiments for the sake of dreams and vain fictions of our own devising" (III); "In experimental philosophy we are to look upon propositions collected by general induction from phænomena as accurately or very nearly true … till such time as other phænomena occur" (IV) [C, S25]. Rules III and IV were added in later editions (1713, 1726) [U]. A page should not date all four to 1687.
  - General Scholium: "I frame no hypotheses … In this philosophy particular propositions are inferred from the phenomena, and afterwards rendered general by induction" [C, S25]. This is already on the site.
- **The Royal Society.** Motto *Nullius in verba*, from Horace, *Epistles* I.1.14: "nullius addictus iurare in verba magistri", not bound to swear by any master's words [C, S26]. The founding meeting of 28 November 1660 proposed a "Colledge for the Promoting of Physico-Mathematicall Experimentall Learning"; the motto was chosen soon after (Hunter 1995) [C that Wikipedia says so, S27]. The Society's own history page was blocked [U on its wording].

### 1d. The eighteenth century: what counts as proper science

- **Kant: "historical" natural doctrine and natural science proper** (*Metaphysische Anfangsgründe der Naturwissenschaft*, preface, AA IV 467–471) [C, S28]. The work is dated 1786 [U]. Renderings are mine.
  - The division matters for the word history: natural doctrine (*Naturlehre*) divides into "historische Naturlehre, welche nichts als systematisch geordnete Facta der Naturdinge enthält (und wiederum aus Naturbeschreibung, als einem Classensystem derselben nach Ähnlichkeiten, und Naturgeschichte, als einer systematischen Darstellung derselben in verschiedenen Zeiten und Örtern, bestehen würde)" and natural science (*Naturwissenschaft*) (IV 468). That is: historical natural doctrine contains only systematically ordered facts, and is made up of the *description* of nature (a classification by likeness) and the *history* of nature (a presentation of things across different times and places).
  - "Eigentliche Wissenschaft kann nur diejenige genannt werden, deren Gewißheit apodiktisch ist", only that whose certainty is apodictic can be called science proper (IV 468).
  - "Ich behaupte aber, daß in jeder besonderen Naturlehre nur so viel eigentliche Wissenschaft angetroffen werden könne, als darin Mathematik anzutreffen ist", I maintain that in any particular natural doctrine there is only as much science proper as there is mathematics in it (IV 470).
  - Chemistry can be no more than "systematische Kunst oder Experimentallehre, niemals aber eigentliche Wissenschaft", a systematic art or experimental doctrine, never science proper (IV 471). Empirical psychology is further still, at most "eine Naturbeschreibung der Seele", a description of the soul, partly because observation alters what is observed (IV 471).
- **Schelling: a philosophy of nature that derives experience.** "eine Philosophie der Natur solle die Möglichkeit einer Natur, d. h. der gesammten Erfahrungswelt, aus Principien ableiten", a philosophy of nature is to derive the possibility of a nature, that is, of the whole world of experience, from principles; and "Die Natur soll der sichtbare Geist, der Geist die unsichtbare Natur seyn", nature should be visible spirit, spirit invisible nature (*Ideen zu einer Philosophie der Natur*, 1797, introduction; my rendering) [C, S29]. The OCR also shows a line pairing physics with theoretical philosophy and history with practical philosophy; the scan is too garbled to quote [U]. Word test: this is *Naturphilosophie*, a philosophy of nature built from principles, not the English "natural philosophy" of Newton. Helmholtz's 1862 attack (§1e) is the sciences' reply to it.
- **Goethe: the experiment as mediator.** From "Der Versuch als Vermittler von Objekt und Subjekt" (written probably April 1792; my renderings) [C, S31]:
  - the inquirer is to observe "die Gegenstände der Natur an sich selbst und in ihren Verhältnissen untereinander", the objects of nature in themselves and in their relations to one another, and take the measure "nicht aus sich, sondern aus dem Kreise der Dinge … die er beobachtet", not from himself but from the circle of things he observes;
  - "Da alles in der Natur … in einer ewigen Wirkung und Gegenwirkung sind, so kann man von einem jeden Phänomene sagen, daß es mit unzähligen andern in Verbindung stehe", since everything in nature is in an eternal action and counteraction, every phenomenon stands in connection with countless others. Hence "Die Vermannigfaltigung eines jeden einzelnen Versuches ist also die eigentliche Pflicht eines Naturforschers", varying each single experiment is the natural inquirer's real duty;
  - knowledge is shared work: only "das Interesse mehrerer auf einen Punkt gerichtet", the interest of many directed at one point, produces anything excellent; science needs "Mitteilung, Beihülfe, Erinnerung und Widerspruch", communication, help, reminder and contradiction; and "eine Wissenschaft … ist … eine so große Masse, daß sie viele Menschen trägt, wenn sie gleich kein Mensch tragen kann", a science is so great a mass that it carries many people, though no one person can carry it.

### 1e. The nineteenth century: division, and the term narrows

- **Whewell: induction as a conception superinduced, and consilience.** "in every inference by Induction there is some Conception superinduced upon the Facts" (*The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences*, 1840, vol. 2, c. p. 215) [C, S32]. On consilience: "the evidence in favour of our induction is of a much higher and more forcible character when it enables us to explain and determine cases of a kind different from those which were contemplated in the formation of our hypothesis … That rules springing from remote and unconnected quarters should thus leap to the same point, can only arise from that being the point where truth resides … I will term it the Consilience of Inductions" (vol. 2, pp. 230–231) [C, S32]. His 1834 and 1840 "scientist" lines are already on the site.
- **Helmholtz, Heidelberg, 22 November 1862: specialisation and the whole.** In Atkinson's translation (*Popular Lectures on Scientific Subjects*, 1873) [C, S33]:
  - the sciences once combined "under the name Universitas Litterarum have become looser than ever"; "every student is forced to choose a narrower and narrower field for his own studies, and can only keep up an imperfect acquaintance even with allied fields of research"; Kepler professor of "mathematics and moral philosophy", Boerhaave holding botany, chemistry and clinical medicine;
  - the schism between natural science and the philological-historical sciences grew up "mainly under the influence of the Hegelian philosophy", where the philosophy of identity tried to construct the sciences "a priori"; under Kant "such a schism had never been proclaimed";
  - "each one must be convinced that it is only in connection with others that he can further the great work"; "The annals of science abound in evidence how such mutual services have been exchanged, even between departments of science apparently most remote" (eclipses dating ancient chronology, physiology serving philology).
  - **Word test:** Atkinson has Helmholtz say "I am myself a student of natural philosophy". The German is "als ich selbst dem Kreise der Naturwissenschaften angehöre", as I myself belong to the circle of the natural sciences [C, S34]. Atkinson's note: "The German word *Naturwissenschaft* has no exact equivalent in modern English" [C, S33]. So in 1873 an English translator could still render *Naturwissenschaften* as "natural philosophy".
- **Thomson and Tait, *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (Oxford, Clarendon Press, vol. I, 1867; preface dated July 1867)** [C, S35]. Both authors are styled professors of natural philosophy, at Glasgow and at Edinburgh. The preface defines the term:
  - "The term Natural Philosophy was used by Newton, and is still used in British Universities, to denote the investigation of laws in the material world, and the deduction of results not directly observed. Observation, classification, and description of phenomena necessarily precede Natural Philosophy in every department of natural science. The earlier stage is, in some branches, commonly called Natural History; and it might with equal propriety be so called in all others." [C, S35]
  - The epigraph is from Fourier: "Les causes primordiales ne nous sont point connues; mais elles sont assujetties à des lois simples et constantes, que l'on peut découvrir par l'observation, et dont l'étude est l'objet de la philosophie naturelle." [C, S35]
  - Chapter III, "Experience", "treats of Observation and Experiment as the basis of Natural Philosophy". "When, as in astronomy, we endeavour to ascertain these causes by simply watching their effects, we observe; when, as in our laboratories, we interfere arbitrarily with the causes or circumstances of a phenomenon, we are said to experiment" (§369) [C, S35].
  - "we take the position of Restorers, and not of Innovators" [C, S35].
- **Chairs.** Zabarella at Padua, 1577, is given as the first appointment in natural philosophy as such [C that Wikipedia says so, citing SEP, S36]. By the mid-nineteenth century the term meant physics, and it survives in that sense in chair titles [C that Wikipedia says so, S36]. Thomson and Tait's title page is the primary evidence for Glasgow and Edinburgh [C, S35].
- **Windelband (1894)** is on the site already. du Bois-Reymond was not researched [U].

### 1f. Word tests that recur through the thread

- *historia*: inquiry, the knowledge so gained, and then a written account (§3). Jenkinson's "historical survey" and Macaulay's "Inquiry" are translators' choices [C, S1, S3].
- *aisthēsis* rendered "observation" (Platt) [C, S6]. *experimentum*/*experientia* rendered "experience" and "experimental science" (Burke) [C, S15, S16]. *sensate esperienze* are sense-experiences, not experiments [C, S23].
- "natural philosophy" in English can render *physikē* or *physica* (Lewis and Short gives "natural science, natural philosophy, physics" for *physica*), *philosophia naturalis*, or even *Naturwissenschaften* (Atkinson) [C, S30, S33, S34]. LSJ glosses *ho physikos* as "an inquirer into nature, natural philosopher" [C, S30]. That gloss is the lexicon's English, not the Greek's.
- *Naturphilosophie* (Schelling) is not "natural philosophy" (Newton, Thomson and Tait) [C, S29, S35].

## 2. Medieval natural philosophy in the arts faculty

**Headline.** At Paris, Aristotle's books on nature were first banned (1210, 1215), then admitted after a promised examination (1231), and by 1255 set texts of the arts course with fixed lecture times. The arts course was reading and lecturing on these books, with the disputed question alongside. The masters' own sense of what natural philosophy is comes from Aristotle: the study of mobile being.

- **1210, provincial council of Sens at Paris:** "nec libri Aristotelis de naturali philosophia nec commenta legantur Parisius publice vel secreto", neither Aristotle's books on natural philosophy nor commentaries are to be read at Paris, publicly or privately, on pain of excommunication (*Chartularium Universitatis Parisiensis* I no. 11, pp. 70–71) [C, S12]. Denifle's note says the council did not yet distinguish metaphysics from natural philosophy, as Courson did five years later [C, S12].
- **1215, Robert de Courson's statutes:** "Non legantur libri Aristotelis de methafisica et de naturali philosophia, nec summe de eisdem, aut de doctrina magistri David de Dinant, aut Amalrici heretici, aut Mauricii hyspani" (no. 20, pp. 78–79) [C, S12]. Fordham's English: "The books of Aristotle on Metaphysics or Natural Philosophy, or the abridgements of these works, are not to be read" [C, S37]. Aristotle's logic was to be read "in the regular and not in the extraordinary courses" [C, S37].
- **1229, the masters of Toulouse advertise:** "Libros naturales, qui fuerant Parisius prohibiti, poterunt illic audire qui volunt nature sinum medullitus perscrutari", those who want to search nature's bosom to the marrow can hear there the natural books that had been prohibited at Paris (Chartularium I p. 131; my rendering) [C, S12]. Again the verb is *audire*, to hear.
- **1231, Gregory IX, *Parens scientiarum*:** the masters of arts are not to use "libris illis naturalibus, qui in Concilio provinciali ex certa causa prohibiti fuere … quousque examinati fuerint et ab omni errorum suspitione purgati", those natural books prohibited for a certain reason by the provincial council, until they have been examined and purged of all suspicion of error. Theology students should not "philosophos se ostentent, sed satagant fieri theodocti", show themselves off as philosophers, but strive to become taught by God (Chartularium I no. 79) [C, S12].
- **1255, 19 March, arts faculty statute:** set because some masters were rushing their lectures, it fixes the dates by which the listed books may be finished (no. 246, pp. 277–278; dated in the text "Anno Domini MCCLIV", Old Style) [C, S12]. The natural books listed: the *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De animalibus*, *De caelo et mundo*, *Meteora* I and IV, *De anima*, *De generatione*, *Liber de causis*, *De sensu et sensato*, *De sompno et vigilia*, *De plantis*, *De memoria et reminiscentia*, *De differentia spiritus et anime*, and *De morte et vita* [C, S12]. Three of these are not Aristotle's: the *Liber de causis*, *De plantis* and Costa ben Luca's *De differentia* [U as to the attributions; standard].
- **What natural philosophy covered.** It covered the books on nature: the *Physics*, *De caelo*, *On Generation and Corruption*, *Meteorology*, *De anima*, the *Parva naturalia* and the animal books. SEP's list for later medieval natural philosophy is the same [C, S17], and the 1255 list matches (above). Its subject: "ens mobile" (Aquinas) [C, S11]; for Ibn Sīnā, "body insofar as it is subject to motion" [C as SEP's summary, S10].
- **How it proceeded.** By lecture on the text (*lectio*) and the disputed question, already on the site. Buridan and Oresme wrote commentaries and books of *Questions* on these texts [C, S17, S19]. Their own statements are in §1b.
- **Edward Grant's thesis** is relevant here: natural philosophy and the exact sciences were distinct in the Middle Ages and fused in the seventeenth century into a "mathematized" natural philosophy (*A History of Natural Philosophy*, 2007, p. 319) [C as quoted by Carolino, S38].

## 3. Word histories

**Headline.** *historia* meant inquiry, then the knowledge gained, then the written account, and only later the past itself. Its old sense survives in "natural history", the descriptive stage that Kant and Thomson and Tait both set beneath "science proper" or "natural philosophy". *physis* ("origin", "growth", "nature") gave *physikē*, the study of nature, which narrowed to physics. The dictionary record supports the drift. Because Etymonline and the OED were not available, the English dating is weak.

- **historia (Greek).** LSJ [C, S30]:
  - I. "inquiry": Hdt. 2.118; *hē peri physeōs historia*, Plato *Phaedo* 96a; Aristotle's inquiries "about animals"; *peri phytōn historia*, the title of Theophrastus's work; "systematic or scientific observation", Epicurus; "of science generally".
  - I.2. "knowledge so obtained, information": Hdt. 1 proem; *opsis emē kai gnōmē kai historiē*, Hdt. 2.99; *hē tēs psychēs historia*, Aristotle *De anima* 402a4.
  - II. "written account of one's inquiries, narrative, history": Aristotle *Rhetoric* and *Poetics*, Polybius; "restricted by some to contemporary history".
  - Root: *histōr* (*ístōr*), "one who knows law and right, judge" (Homer), "witnesses" (Boeotian inscriptions), and as adjective "knowing, learned"; "From ϝιδ-τωρ, cf. οἶδα" [C, S30]. The verb *historeō*: "inquire into", "examine, observe" [C, S30].
  - On the site, the `aristotle` page renders *Historia Animalium* as "Inquiry into Animals".
- **historia (Latin).** Lewis and Short: "a narrative of past events, history"; Cicero's "historia testis temporum, lux veritatis, vita memoriae, magistra vitae" (*De oratore* 2.36); Herodotus "patrem historiae" (*De legibus* 1.5) [C, S39]. By Cicero the Latin word's primary sense is the narrative of the past.
- **historia → "natural history" → description.** Pliny's title *Naturalis historia* [U, not opened]. Bacon's "natural history" is the store of observed facts beneath natural philosophy (I.98) [C, S21]. Kant's *historische Naturlehre* is ordered facts, split into *Naturbeschreibung* and *Naturgeschichte* across "times and places" [C, S28]. Thomson and Tait call the observational stage "Natural History" [C, S35]. This is the line along which *historia* comes apart from *physis*, before Windelband's 1894 split of sciences of laws from sciences of events (on the site).
- **English "history".** Middle English *historie*, from Old French and Latin *historia*. Wiktionary's root note, citing the OED, gives Gower's *Confessio Amantis* (1393) for a first attestation [C that Wiktionary says so, S40]. When the sense narrowed to "the past" is not checked [U].
- **physis.** LSJ [C, S30]:
  - I. "origin": Empedocles 8.1; Plato *Laws* 892c, "*physis* they mean the generation of the first things"; Aristotle *Physics* 193b12 and *Metaphysics* 1014b16, "*physis* is said of the genesis of growing things". 2. "growth".
  - II. "the natural form or constitution of a person or thing as the result of growth", then character.
  - III. "the regular order of nature"; *kata physin* against *para physin*, *physei* against *nomōi*.
- **physikos, physikē.** LSJ: II. "of or concerning the order of external nature"; *hē physikē epistēmē* (Aristotle *PA* 640a2); *physikē philosophia* (*PA* 653a9); *hē physikē*, opposed to *mathēmatikē* and *theologikē* (*Metaphysics* 1026a, 1064b); *ho physikos*, "an inquirer into nature, natural philosopher", "esp. of the Ionic and other pre-Socratic philosophers"; *hē physikē akroasis*, the title of Aristotle's treatise; *ta physika*, his physical treatises [C, S30]. *physiologia*: "inquiring into natural causes and phenomena" [C, S30].
- **Latin.** *physica, physicē*, "= φυσική, natural science, natural philosophy, physics" (Cicero *Academica* 1.25 and others); *physicus*, "a natural philosopher, naturalist" [C, S39]. *natura*, from *nascor*, "birth"; then "the nature … the natural constitution … of a thing"; *rerum natura* (Cicero) [C, S39]. Aquinas's own definition chains the words: "naturalia autem sunt quorum principium est natura; natura autem est principium motus et quietis" [C, S11]. Lucretius's *De rerum natura* as a rendering of Epicurus's *Peri physeōs* [U].
- **English "physics".** Wiktionary: from *physic*, Middle English *phisik*, Old French *fisike* "natural science, art of healing", Latin *physica*, Greek *physikē*; the form "physics" from the 1580s [C that Wiktionary says so, S40]. The narrowing to the present discipline is shown in Thomson and Tait's preface (1867) and in "physicist" (Whewell 1840, on the site).
- **"natural philosophy" in English.** Wiktionary: "from 14th c."; Middle English "(natural) philosophie" [C that Wiktionary says so, S40]. A first attestation needs the OED or MED [U]. Both were blocked or not tried.
- **"science" and "philosophy".** Latin *scientia*, knowledge, from *scire* [C that Wiktionary says so, S40]. The split in English usage is dated by Whewell's 1833/34 and 1840 coinages (on the site). Helmholtz's translator (1873) and Thomson and Tait (1867) show that "natural philosophy" still named the natural sciences, or physics, in British usage into the 1860s–70s [C, S33, S35].

## 4. McGrath in his own words

- **Imprint: 2023.** *Natural Philosophy: On Retrieving a Lost Disciplinary Imaginary*, first edition, Oxford and New York, Oxford University Press, 2023, viii + 248 pp., ISBN 978-0-19-286573-1 (hardback); LC control number 2022936280 (catalogued 2022) [C, S41]. An Oxford alumni interview dated 1 November 2022 calls it his "latest work" [U, not re-opened for this note: https://www.alumni.ox.ac.uk/node/2813981]; the online edition probably appeared in late 2022 [U]. **Use 2023.**
- **Publisher's description, verbatim** (as given in the library record) [C as quoted, S41]: "In the seventeenth century, natural philosophy was seen as an integrated enterprise, embracing what are now seen as separate disciplines, such as philosophy, the natural sciences, mathematics, and theology. Although often portrayed as a now redundant precursor of the natural sciences, natural philosophy was far more than this, enfolding the two quite different notions of learning about and learning from nature. This book argues for the retrieval of the 'disciplinary imaginary' of natural philosophy." Part one traces the idea from Aristotle to its peak in the seventeenth century (Kepler, Boyle, Newton) and identifies "the factors causing the decline of the movement in the nineteenth century". Part two argues that "a retrieved natural philosophy can hold together both the objective and subjective aspects of the human engagement with the natural world", using "Mary Midgley's approach to safeguarding the complexity of nature and Karl Popper's model of the 'three worlds' of human knowledge—objective, subjective, and theoretical".
- **Contents** [C, S41]: "Introduction: on retrieving natural philosophy"; Part I, "A critical conversation with the tradition": Aristotle; the Middle Ages; "Skywatching: the natural philosophy of Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo"; "English natural philosophy: Bacon, Boyle, and Newton"; "The parting of the ways: from natural philosophy to natural science"; Part II, "A reconceived natural philosophy: exploring a disciplinary imaginary": foundations; "Theory: the contemplation of nature"; "Objectivity"; "Subjectivity: an affective engagement with nature"; "Natural philosophy: recasting a vision".
- **Event listing** (Oxford Literary Festival, 29 March 2023): McGrath "argues for the return of 'natural philosophy' and its emphasis on both learning about and learning from nature, a concept that has been lost since the establishment of individual scientific disciplines"; natural philosophy "encompassed natural sciences, mathematics, music, philosophy and theology. It faded into obscurity during the 19th century" [C, S42]. This is the festival's wording, not McGrath's.

## 5. Cunningham's dispute

**Headline.** Andrew Cunningham argued that natural philosophy was not early science: it was "about God" and "God's universe", and science was invented later, around 1760–1848. Edward Grant held that natural philosophy gave natural explanations of natural things and grew into the sciences. Reviewers locate the disagreement in method: actors' categories against analytic categories. This changes the headline of any "natural philosophy became science" sentence, so a page needs one honest clause, but not the whole debate.

- **Cunningham 1988:** "Getting the game right: Some plain words on the identity and invention of science", *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* 19(3) (1988) 365–389 [C, metadata, S43].
- **Cunningham 1991:** "How the *Principia* got its name; or, taking natural philosophy seriously", *History of Science* 29(4) (1991) 377–392 [C, metadata, S43]. Catalogue abstract: it "Explores the meaning of the title of Newton's 'Principia' … and argues that … 'natural philosophy' is different from our modern science in the fact that 'natural philosophy was "about God" and about "God's universe"'" [C as catalogued, S44]. Reprinted in his collection *The Identity of the History of Science and Medicine* (Routledge, 2018) [C, metadata, S43].
- **Cunningham and Williams 1993:** "De-centring the 'big picture': *The Origins of Modern Science* and the modern origins of science", *British Journal for the History of Science* 26(4) 407–432 [C, metadata, S43]. Thesis, as Orthia summarises it: science is "a distinctly modern, western phenomenon rather than a human universal, … it was invented in the Age of Revolutions 1760-1848, and … science be de-centred … treated as just one of many forms of human knowledge-seeking activity" [C as summarised, S45]. Orthia's review of 1994–2014 literature finds "the old big picture is disintegrating and lacks active defenders, while many scholars support aspects of the new big picture" [C, S45].
- **The Grant–Cunningham exchange:** *Early Science and Medicine* 5(3) (2000): Cunningham, "The Identity of Natural Philosophy. A Response to Edward Grant", 259–278; Grant, "God and Natural Philosophy: The Late Middle Ages and Sir Isaac Newton", 279–298 [C, metadata, S43].
  - Grant's summary of Cunningham's claim: "natural philosophy was always about God, even when God is not discussed or mentioned; and, consequently, (...) natural philosophy could not be science, because the latter was never about God" (*A History of Natural Philosophy*, 2007, p. xi) [C as quoted by Carolino, S38].
  - Grant's own view, as reviewers summarise it: "the scope of natural philosophy is to provide natural phenomena with natural explanations", and natural philosophy, theology and mathematics were three distinct theoretical sciences [C as summarised, S38]. Actors' terms are "mere names, or labels" (p. 319) [C as quoted, S46].
  - The method point: Cunningham "favors using actor's terms and categories over analytic terms and categories"; "This methodological difference … appears to lie at the heart of their disagreement" (Lao, BMCR 2010.11.14) [C, S46]. Lao also judges Grant's book "not current" in method [C, S46].
- **Dear and reply:** Peter Dear, "Religion, science and natural philosophy: thoughts on Cunningham's thesis", *SHPS* 32(2) (2001) 377–386; Cunningham, "A reply to Peter Dear's …", *SHPS* 32(2) 387–391 [C, metadata, S43]. Content not opened [U].
- **What bears on the pieces:** Newton's own line that discourse of God "from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy" (General Scholium, Motte) is the kind of text Cunningham leans on [the quotation C, S25; the use U]. Thomson and Tait (1867) define the term without God, as "the investigation of laws in the material world" [C, S35]. So the term itself moved, which is a fact both sides can accept.
