## 1. Isaac Newton

### 1a. Life (brief)

- Born at Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, "on 25 December 1642 (old calendar)", into a Puritan family; his father died two months before (SEP) [C, N1]. Keynes: "a posthumous child born with no father on Christmas Day, 1642" [C, N11]. The New Style date (4 January 1643) is not in N1 [U]. **Use 1642, the date the sources give, and note the calendar if needed.**
- Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1661. During the plague years 1665–67 at Woolsthorpe (his "so-called *annus mirabilis*") came early optical discoveries, the inverse-square relation in circular motion, and the calculus (tract of October 1666). Fellow 1667; first reflecting telescope 1669; Lucasian Professor of Mathematics from October 1669 [C, N1].
- Optical paper read to the Royal Society and printed in 1672. The disputes with Hooke and Huygens that followed made him withdraw "from further public exchanges in natural philosophy" [C, N1].
- From 1669, private work in alchemy and theology. His study of the Trinity "opened the way to his questioning the validity of a good deal more doctrine central to the Roman and Anglican Churches" [C, N1].
- Halley's visit in 1684 led to *De motu corporum in gyrum* (November 1684), and that to the *Principia*, printed summer 1687 (about 300 copies) [C, N1].
- MP for Cambridge University 1689–90; friendships with Locke and Fatio de Duillier; a breakdown in 1693 "by his own testimony" [C, N1].
- London: Warden of the Mint 1696, Master from the end of 1699; resigned the Lucasian chair in 1701; President of the Royal Society 1703; knighted by Queen Anne 1705 [C, N1].
- Disputes: with Flamsteed; with Leibniz over the calculus (Keill's charge of plagiarism in 1710, the Royal Society's report of 1712, Newton's anonymous review of it in 1715) [C, N1].
- Died 20 March 1727 (SEP gives no calendar note) [C, N1]. Burial in Westminster Abbey [U] (the `darwin` page says so).
- His papers: the non-scientific part was sold at auction in 1936; serious work on them began in the 1970s [C, N1]. Keynes bought back "about half of them" [C, N11].

### 1b. Key concepts, in his own words

**The laws of motion** (*Principia*, "Axioms, or Laws of Motion", tr. Motte) [C, N3]:
- "Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed thereon."
- "The alteration of motion is ever proportional to the motive force impressed; and is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed."
- "To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction."
- **Word test:** *vis inertiae* is Newton's own term (Def. III: the *vis insita* "may, by a most significant name, be called vis inertiæ, or force of inactivity") [C, N3]. "Newtonian mechanics" is a later name. SEP: it "consists mostly of results achieved on the continent between 1740 and 1800" [C, N1].
- **Galileo, credited by Newton:** "By the first two Laws and the first two Corollaries, Galileo discovered that the descent of bodies observed the duplicate ratio of the time, and that the motion of projectiles was in the curve of a parabola" (Scholium to the Laws) [C, N3]. This is Newton's attribution. Galileo did not state the laws.

**Universal gravitation.** "That there is a power of gravity tending to all bodies, proportional to the several quantities of matter which they contain" (Book III, Prop. VII) [C, N3]. Rule III's comment: "we must, in consequence of this rule, universally allow that all bodies whatsoever are endowed with a principle of mutual gravitation … Not that I affirm gravity to be essential to bodies" [C, N3]. On the cause, to Bentley: "You sometimes speak of gravity as essential & inherent to matter: pray do not ascribe that notion to me, for the cause of gravity is what I do not pretend to know" (17 January 1692/3) [C, N9]. And: "That gravity should be innate inherent & essential to matter so that one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum without the mediation of any thing else … is to me so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters any competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it. Gravity must be caused by an agent acting constantly according to certain laws, but whether this agent be material or immaterial is a question I have left to the consideration of my readers" (25 February 1692/3; date from the Newton Project catalogue) [C, N9].
- *Reception headline:* Huygens and Leibniz saw the theory "as invoking an occult power of action at a distance"; it won on the Continent from the late 1730s to the 1750s (SEP) [C, N1].

**The Rules of Reasoning** (Book III, tr. Motte) [C, N3]:
- I: "We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances."
- II: "to the same natural effects we must, as far as possible, assign the same causes."
- III: qualities "found to belong to all bodies within the reach of our experiments, are to be esteemed the universal qualities of all bodies whatsoever"; "We are certainly not to relinquish the evidence of experiments for the sake of dreams and vain fictions of our own devising."
- IV: "In experimental philosophy we are to look upon propositions collected by general induction from phænomena as accurately or very nearly true … till such time as other phænomena occur, by which they may either be made more accurate, or liable to exceptions."
- **Dates (this settles the earlier open check):** Rules I and II were in the first edition, there labelled "hypotheses"; Rule III was added in the second edition (1713) and Rule IV in the third (1726) (SEP) [C, N2]. A page should not date all four to 1687.

**"Hypotheses non fingo."** General Scholium, added in the second edition (1713) [C, N2]. The Latin is the same in 1713 and 1726: "Rationem vero harum Gravitatis proprietatum ex Phaenomenis nondum potui deducere, & Hypotheses non fingo. Quicquid enim ex Phaenomenis non deducitur, Hypothesis vocanda est; & Hypotheses seu Metaphysicae, seu Physicae, seu Qualitatum occultarum, seu Mechanicae, in Philosophia Experimentali locum non habent" [C, N5, N6].
- Motte (1729): "I frame no hypotheses" [C, N4]. Cohen and Whitman (1999), as SEP quotes them: "I do not feign hypotheses" [C as quoted, N2].
- **Word test:** Newton's own English, in Opticks Q28, is "feigning Hypotheses" [C, N7]. So "feign" has his own usage behind it, and "frame" is Motte's choice. I. B. Cohen's article on the first English version (*Isis* 1962) was not opened [U].

**Experimental philosophy.** "In this philosophy particular propositions are inferr'd from the phænomena, and afterwards render'd general by induction" (Motte 1729) [C, N4]. Opticks Q31: "As in Mathematicks, so in Natural Philosophy, the Investigation of difficult Things by the Method of Analysis, ought ever to precede the Method of Composition. This Analysis consists in making Experiments and Observations, and in drawing general Conclusions from them by Induction … For Hypotheses are not to be regarded in experimental Philosophy. And although the arguing from Experiments and Observations by Induction be no Demonstration of general Conclusions; yet it is the best way of arguing which the Nature of Things admits of" [C, N7]. The *Opticks* opens: "My Design in this Book is not to explain the Properties of Light by Hypotheses, but to propose and prove them by Reason and Experiments" [C, N7].

**Absolute space and time** (Scholium after the Definitions, tr. Motte) [C, N3]:
- "Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external, and by another name is called duration: relative, apparent, and common time, is some sensible and external … measure of duration by the means of motion."
- "Absolute space, in its own nature, without regard to anything external, remains always similar and immovable. Relative space is some movable dimension or measure of the absolute spaces; which our senses determine by its position to bodies."
- The bucket: the water rises to the sides "(as I have experienced)", and "the true and absolute circular motion of the water … discovers itself, and may be measured by this endeavour."
- *Reading (SEP, Smith):* the primary distinction is "absolute, true, mathematical" against "relative, apparent, common", an old astronomical one; absolute space and time "cannot enter directly into empirical reasoning", and "Newton never presupposes absolute time and space in his empirical reasoning". The bucket illustrates "how forces can be distinguished" [C, N2]. This is SEP's reading against a long philosophical habit; a page can carry it as SEP's.
- Corollary V: "The motions of bodies included in a given space are the same among themselves, whether that space is at rest, or moves uniformly forwards in a right line without any circular motion" [C, N3]. This is Galileo's ship in Newton's terms. SEP: it "put him in a position to introduce the notion of an inertial frame, but he did not do so" [C, N2].
- God and space: God "is not duration or space, but he endures and is present. He endures for ever, and is every where present; and by existing always and every where, he constitutes duration and space" (General Scholium) [C, N3]. In Opticks Q28, God "in infinite Space, as it were in his Sensory, sees the things themselves intimately" [C, N7]. Leibniz attacked the "Sensorium" wording; Clarke replied that it is "by way of Similitude only" (1717 English) [C, garbled OCR, N10].

**The relational reply (Leibniz–Clarke, 1715–16).** Leibniz, Third Paper §4 (1717 English): "I hold Space to be something merely relative, as Time is; … an Order of Coexistences, as Time is an Order of Successions" [C, wording read through a badly garbled OCR, N10; confirm against a clean text]. Leibniz's First Paper mocks "these Gentlemen" whose God must "clean" his watch "now and then by an extraordinary Concourse, and even to mend it, as a Clock-maker mends his Work" [C, garbled OCR, N10]; his footnoted target is the *Opticks* passage on the system that "wants a Reformation" [C, N7]. On the Newton page this is one clause pointing to `leibniz`. Newton's own share in Clarke's replies is not checked [U].

**Optics.** 1672: in 1666 he "procured me a Triangular glass-Prisme, to try therewith the celebrated Phænomena of Colours" and saw the spectrum "in an oblong form"; "The gradual removal of these suspitions, at length led me to the Experimentum Crucis"; "Light it self is a Heterogeneous mixture of differently refrangible Rays"; colours are "Original and Connate properties" of rays, not "Qualifications of Light" (*Phil. Trans.* 80, 19 Feb. 1671/2) [C, N8]. *Opticks* (1704): "the Rays to speak properly are not coloured. In them there is nothing else than a certain Power and Disposition to stir up a Sensation of this or that Colour … in the Sensorium they are Sensations of those Motions under the Forms of Colours" [C, N7]. That is the qualities distinction in Newton's own words. Light as particles is posed as a question ("Are not the Rays of Light very small Bodies …?", Q29) [C, N7]. SEP: he thought particles "far more probable" but left it undecided [C, N1].

**The calculus priority dispute (one clause).** Calculus in 1665–66; "most of a decade before Leibniz did so independently, and ultimately more influentially" (SEP); the 1712 Royal Society report, with Newton "a dominant figure" in it, and his anonymous 1715 review [C, N1]. In that review: "The one proceeds upon the Evidence arising from Experiments and Phenomena, and stops where such Evidence is wanting; the other is taken up with Hypotheses" [C as quoted, N1].

**Natural philosophy including God.**
- General Scholium: "This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being"; "a god without dominion, providence, and final causes, is nothing else but Fate and Nature"; "And thus much concerning God; to discourse of whom from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy" (Motte) [C, N3].
- **Word test, edition by edition:** 1713 reads "de quo utique ex Phaenomenis disserere, ad Philosophiam Experimentalem pertinet"; 1726 reads "ad philosophiam naturalem pertinet" [C, N5, N6]. Newton changed "experimental" to "natural" in the God sentence. Motte translates the 1726 text.
- Opticks Q28: "the main Business of natural Philosophy is to argue from Phænomena without feigning Hypotheses, and to deduce Causes from Effects, till we come to the very first Cause, which certainly is not mechanical; and not only to unfold the Mechanism of the World, but chiefly to resolve these and such like Questions … How came the Bodies of Animals to be contrived with so much Art, and for what ends were their several Parts?" [C, N7].
- Opticks Q31: "if natural Philosophy in all its Parts, by pursuing this Method, shall at length be perfected, the Bounds of Moral Philosophy will be also enlarged. For so far as we can know by natural Philosophy what is the first Cause … so far our Duty towards him, as well as that towards one another, will appear to us by the Light of Nature" [C, N7].
- To Bentley: "When I wrote my treatise about our Systeme I had an eye upon such Principles as might work with considering men for the belief of a Deity" (10 December 1692) [C, N9].

### 1c. What natural philosophy is and how it is done, in his words

- The title: *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. SEP: Newton changed the title in 1686 "in allusion to Descartes's most prominent work at the time, *Principia Philosophiae*", and the first edition set the first and third words in larger type [C, N2].
- The task (Author's Preface, 1686): "all the difficulty of philosophy seems to consist in this—from the phænomena of motions to investigate the forces of nature, and then from these forces to demonstrate the other phænomena" (Motte) [C, earlier round].
- Mathematics before physics (Book I, end of Section XI, as SEP quotes Cohen–Whitman): "considering in this treatise not the species of forces and their physical qualities but their quantities and mathematical proportions … Then, coming down to physics, these proportions must be compared with the phenomena" [C as quoted, N2].
- Method: analysis before composition; induction from experiments is "no Demonstration" but "the best way of arguing which the Nature of Things admits of" (Q31); propositions held "till such time as other phænomena occur" (Rule IV) [C, N3, N7].
- Scope: the first cause, final causes and God belong to it (Q28, General Scholium 1726), and its completion would enlarge moral philosophy (Q31) [C, N3, N7].
- SEP's headline: Newton's "deep commitment to having the empirical world serve not only as the ultimate arbiter, but also as the sole basis for adopting provisional theory"; leaving the cause of gravity and the nature of light open was what drove "a wedge between natural philosophy and empirical science" [C, N1].

## 2. Galileo Galilei

### 2a. Life (brief)

- Born in Pisa on 15 February 1564. Died on 8 January 1642, a date SEP flags ("but for problems with the date, see Machamer 1998b") [C, G1].
- Father Vincenzo, a court musician and music theorist. Enrolled in medicine at Pisa in 1580, did not finish, and studied mathematics with Ostilio Ricci; "left the University of Pisa without a degree" [C, G1].
- Lecturer in mathematics at Pisa 1589; chair of mathematics at Padua 1592. Instrument workshop and the compass (1606). Children with Marina Gamba: Virginia (1600), Livia (1601), Vincenzo (1606) [C, G1].
- 1609: heard of the spyglass and built an improved telescope. *Sidereus Nuncius*, March 1610. In 1610 he moved to Florence as "Chief Mathematician and Philosopher to the Grand Duke". Accademia dei Lincei 1611 ("Lincean Academician" for life) [C, G1].
- To Belisario Vinta, 7 May 1610: "io desidererei, oltre al nome di Matematico, che S. A. ci aggiungesse quello di Filosofo, professando io di avere studiato più anni in filosofia, che mesi in matematica pura" [C, G3]. My rendering: I would wish His Highness to add to the name of Mathematician that of Philosopher, since I profess to have studied more years in philosophy than months in pure mathematics.
- *Discourse on Floating Bodies* (1612); *Letters on Sunspots* (1613), his first open Copernicanism; Letter to Castelli (1613), expanded into the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615) [C, G1].
- March 1616: Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* "suspended" pending correction. Bellarmine admonished Galileo; whether a stricter "formal injunction" followed is "far from straightforward" and disputed [C, G1].
- *Il Saggiatore* (1623). Urban VIII (Maffeo Barberini) elected 1623. *Dialogo* printed in Florence by February 1632; sale banned; trial [C, G1].
- 22 June 1633: sentenced as "vehementemente sospetto d'heresia"; the book prohibited; "carcere formale"; the seven penitential psalms weekly for three years (Sentence) [C, G7]. Abjuration at the Minerva, the same day, "con cuor sincero e fede non finta abiuro, maledico e detesto li sudetti errori et heresie", "dell'età mia d'anni 70" [C, G7]. Imprisonment commuted to house arrest, at Siena and from December 1633 at Arcetri [C, G1].
- *Two New Sciences*, smuggled out and published by the Elzeviers in Holland in 1638. Re-interred in 1737 [C, G1].

### 2b. Key concepts, in his own words

**The book of nature, written in mathematics.** *Il Saggiatore* (1623) §6, against Sarsi, who treats philosophy as "un libro e una fantasia d'un uomo, come l'*Iliade* e l'*Orlando furioso*": "La filosofia è scritta in questo grandissimo libro che continuamente ci sta aperto innanzi a gli occhi (io dico l'universo) … Egli è scritto in lingua matematica, e i caratteri son triangoli, cerchi, ed altre figure geometriche, senza i quali mezi è impossibile a intenderne umanamente parola; senza questi è un aggirarsi vanamente per un oscuro laberinto" [C, G4]. The last clause: without these means it is humanly impossible to understand a word of it, and one wanders in vain through a dark labyrinth. SEP quotes Drake's English (Drake and O'Malley 1960, p. 184) [C as quoted, G1]. Sarsi is the pseudonym of the Jesuit Orazio Grassi [U]; SEP's bibliography names Grassi as co-author of the comet controversy volume [C, G1].
- **Mathematics as knowledge equal to God's in certainty** (*Dialogo*, Day One): understanding taken *extensive* is "come nullo" against the infinity of intelligibles, but taken *intensive*, "l'intelletto umano ne intende alcune così perfettamente, e ne ha così assoluta certezza, quanto se n'abbia l'istessa natura; e tali sono le scienze matematiche pure, cioè la geometria e l'aritmetica … credo che la cognizione agguagli la divina nella certezza obiettiva" [C, G2]. My rendering: the human intellect understands some propositions as perfectly, and with as absolute a certainty, as nature itself; such are the pure mathematical sciences; and of those few I believe its knowledge equals the divine in objective certainty.
- **The geometer-philosopher and the impediments of matter** (*Dialogo*, Day Two): just as the accountant deducts boxes and wrappings, "così, quando il filosofo geometra vuol riconoscere in concreto gli effetti dimostrati in astratto, bisogna che difalchi gli impedimenti della materia … Gli errori dunque non consistono nè nell'astratto nè nel concreto, nè nella geometria o nella fisica, ma nel calcolatore, che non sa fare i conti giusti" [C, G2]. **Word test:** *filosofo geometra* (geometer-philosopher) is rendered "mathematical scientist" in Drake's English as SEP quotes it [C as quoted, G1].

**Primary and secondary qualities.** *Il Saggiatore* §48: tastes, odours and colours "non sieno altro che puri nomi" on the side of the object and "tengano solamente lor residenza nel corpo sensitivo, sì che rimosso l'animale, sieno levate ed annichilate tutte queste qualità"; what body cannot lack is shape, size, place, time, motion or rest, contact, number, "primi e reali accidenti" [C, earlier round]. SEP: "generally recognized as the first clear argument for the primary and secondary quality distinction", but "out of character for Galileo, who is generally skeptical about our ability to discern ultimate constitutions" (*Sunspot Letters*: investigating essences "no less impossible" for near substances than for far) [C, G1]. **Word tests:** Drake's "consciousness" and SEP's "sensorium" both render *corpo sensitivo*; the pair "primary and secondary" is Locke's [C, earlier round, G1].

**Telescopic observations** (*Sidereus Nuncius*, Venice, 1610, tr. Carlos 1880) [C, G6]:
- The title page addresses "every one, but especially philosophers and astronomers": the Moon's surface, innumerable fixed stars, the Milky Way, nebulous stars, "but especially … Four Planets which revolve round the Planet Jupiter", named "the Medicean Stars"; it is styled "Professor of Mathematics in the University of Padua".
- The Moon: "not perfectly smooth, free from inequalities and exactly spherical, as a large school of philosophers considers … but … full of inequalities, uneven, full of hollows and protuberances, just like the surface of the Earth itself".
- Jupiter's moons as an argument for Copernicus: "our sense of sight presents to us four satellites circling about Jupiter, like the Moon about the Earth, while the whole system travels over a mighty orbit about the Sun".
- Later in 1610, the phases of Venus and Saturn's odd shape (SEP) [C, G1].

**The law of fall and the inertia precursor** (*Two New Sciences*, 1638, Day Three and Day Four, tr. Crew and de Salvio 1914) [C, G5]:
- "My purpose is to set forth a very new science dealing with a very ancient subject … I have discovered by experiment some properties of it which are worth knowing"; "no one has yet pointed out that the distances traversed, during equal intervals of time, by a body falling from rest, stand to one another in the same ratio as the odd numbers beginning with unity".
- Theorem II, Prop. II: "The spaces described by a body falling from rest with a uniformly accelerated motion are to each other as the squares of the time-intervals employed in traversing these distances."
- The cause set aside: "The present does not seem to be the proper time to investigate the cause of the acceleration of natural motion … At present it is the purpose of our Author merely to investigate and to demonstrate some of the properties of accelerated motion (whatever the cause of this acceleration may be)".
- Horizontal motion: "any velocity once imparted to a moving body will be rigidly maintained as long as the external causes of acceleration or retardation are removed, a condition which is found only on horizontal planes … motion along a horizontal plane is perpetual"; projection is compounded "of one which is uniform and horizontal and of another which is vertical and naturally accelerated", hence the parabola.
- **Word test:** "inertia" is not Galileo's word. Newton's *vis inertiae* is the term [C, N3]. SEP says Galileo "conceived of an inertial principle" [C, G1]. Crew and de Salvio render *naturalem motum* as "free motion" and say so in a note [C, G5]. Galileo's perpetual motion is along a horizontal plane, and on the Earth the horizontal follows its surface. Koyré's "circular inertia" reading is [U] and stays out unless the page needs it.

**The relativity of motion: the ship** (*Dialogo*, Day Two, Salviati) [C, G2]: "Riserratevi con qualche amico nella maggiore stanza che sia sotto coverta di alcun gran navilio" (shut yourself up with a friend in the largest cabin below decks of some great ship) with flies, butterflies, fish in a bowl and dripping water; "fate muover la nave con quanta si voglia velocità; chè (pur che il moto sia uniforme e non fluttuante in qua e in là) voi non riconoscerete una minima mutazione in tutti li nominati effetti, nè da alcuno di quelli potrete comprender se la nave cammina o pure sta ferma". The cause: "l'esser il moto della nave comune a tutte le cose contenute in essa ed all'aria ancora". My rendering: make the ship move at any speed you like; as long as the motion is uniform and not rocking, you will not see the least change in any of these effects, nor be able to tell from any of them whether the ship is moving or standing still … because the ship's motion is common to everything in it, and to the air as well.
- On the stone dropped from the mast, Simplicio asks whether he has made "cento … prove": "Io senza esperienza son sicuro che l'effetto seguirà come vi dico, perchè così è necessario che segua" (I am sure without experiment that the effect will follow as I tell you, because it must follow so) [C, G2]. SEP cites this line against reading Galileo as a pure experimentalist [C, G1].

**Scripture and nature: the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615)** [C, G3]:
- "nelle dispute di problemi naturali non si dovrebbe cominciare dalle autorità di luoghi delle Scritture, ma dalle sensate esperienze e dalle dimostrazioni necessarie: perché, procedendo di pari dal Verbo divino la Scrittura Sacra e la natura, quella come dettatura dello Spirito Santo, e questa come osservantissima essecutrice de gli ordini di Dio" (in natural disputes begin not from passages of Scripture but from sense-experiences and necessary demonstrations, since Scripture and nature both proceed from the divine Word, the one as dictated by the Holy Spirit, the other as the most obedient executor of God's orders).
- "due verità non possono contrariarsi" (two truths cannot contradict each other), so wise interpreters must find senses of Scripture that agree with what "il senso manifesto e le dimostrazioni necessarie" have made certain. Where human reasoning cannot reach, one must conform "col puro senso della Scrittura".
- "Ma che quell'istesso Dio che ci ha dotati di sensi, di discorso e d'intelletto, abbia voluto, posponendo l'uso di questi, darci con altro mezo le notizie che per quelli possiamo conseguire … non credo che sia necessario il crederlo" (I do not think it necessary to believe that the same God who gave us senses, reason and intellect wished us to set them aside).
- "l'intenzione dello Spirito Santo essere d'insegnarci come si vadia al cielo, e non come vadia il cielo" (the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes). Galileo credits it to an unnamed "persona ecclesiastica costituita in eminentissimo grado". John Paul II (1992) calls it "le mot célèbre attribué à Baronius" [C, G8]. **The attribution to Baronius is a later identification**; the letter does not name him.
- He quotes Augustine (*De Genesi ad litteram*) and Pereira for the principle [C, G3; the citations as Galileo gives them].
- SEP: Galileo argues for "two truths" that cannot contradict, Scripture "accommodated" to common understanding; Bellarmine would reinterpret Scripture only if the earth's motion "could be proven or demonstrated", and he took the planetary models as "merely mathematical conceits" [C, G1]. Date: 1615 [C, G1], which settles the earlier open check. Printing in 1636 (Strasbourg) is [U].

**The Copernican trial (1633).** The sentence and abjuration as in §2a [C, G7]. The *Dialogo*'s preface ("Al discreto lettore") says he took the Copernican side "procedendo in pura ipotesi matematica" (proceeding as a pure mathematical hypothesis) and calls the 1616 decree "un salutifero editto" [C, G2]. The sentence finds that the book transgressed the 1616 precept, and that its arguments for the false side seemed "più tosto per la loro efficacia … potenti a stringer che facili ad esser sciolti" [C, G7]. SEP's headlines: Copernicanism was first publicly called heresy in Galileo's own condemnation; *De revolutionibus* stayed on the Index until 1835; the 1616 records made him look a recidivist; Redondi's atomism plea-bargain thesis "has not found much support" [C, G1].
- *Reception:* John Paul II, 31 October 1992, to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences: Galileo, "croyant sincère, s'est montré plus perspicace sur ce point que ses adversaires théologiens"; "L'erreur des théologiens d'alors … fut de penser que notre connaissance de la structure du monde physique était, d'une certaine manière, imposée par le sens littéral de l'Écriture Sainte"; and from the Enlightenment on "le cas Galilée a constitué une sorte de mythe" [C, G8]. The address is in French on the Vatican site; the English page carries no text [C, G8]. It also says Galileo would not present Copernicus "comme une hypothèse" [C, G8].

### 2c. What natural philosophy is and how it is done, in his words

- Philosophy is read in the universe, not in an author's book, and its language is mathematics (*Saggiatore* §6) [C, G4].
- He wanted the title Philosopher as well as Mathematician (1610), and the court title joined them [C, G3, G1]. SEP: he was a "novel hybrid", "licensed to draw conclusions about fundamental natures from mathematical demonstrations", against the old split of *mathematicus* and philosophy professor (the *Quaestio de certitudine mathematicarum*) [C, G1].
- The method's two terms are "sensate esperienze" and "dimostrazioni necessarie" (Christina) [C, G3]. **Word test:** *sensate esperienze* are sense-experiences, not experiments (earlier round) [C].
- Mathematics: pure mathematics equals the divine in certainty (Day One), and the geometer-philosopher "deducts" the impediments of matter (Day Two) [C, G2].
- Causes may be set aside while properties are demonstrated (*Two New Sciences*, Day Three) [C, G5].
- Experiment's role is disputed (§2d). His own texts point both ways: "I have discovered by experiment" [C, G5] against "senza esperienza son sicuro" [C, G2].

### 2d. Readings (attributed)

- **SEP's frame:** "Galileo has been used to exemplify many different themes, usually as a personification of whatever the writer wished to make the hallmark of the Scientific Revolution or of good science"; the readings run from mathematical Platonist (Koyré) and Archimedean, through experimentalist (Settle, Drake), to an heir of Scholastic method (Wallace), or "no method" (Feyerabend 1975) [C, G1]. That list can carry the headline in one sentence.
- **Koyré against Drake:** Koyré placed Galileo among the Platonists, and some read his method as "entirely a priori" (Koyré 1966); manuscript studies of his experiments "have cast doubt on this view" (Drake 1978, 1990; Settle) [C as SEP reports, G1]. Koyré's own wording ("Galileo and Plato", 1943) was not opened [U].
- **Burtt (1925), Collingwood (1945), Husserl (1936):** already on the site (`collingwood`, `husserl`, *Philosophy and the special sciences*) [C, earlier round].
- **Feyerabend (1975):** already on the site.
- **Duhem's forerunners:** SEP on Buridan: "It is too much to claim, as Duhem did, that he was a forerunner of Galileo" [C, earlier round]. This is a reception note only.
- **The "warfare" myth:** SEP: the condemnation made him "a purported 'martyr' to the cause of rationality … in the subsequent history of a supposed 'warfare' between science and religion" [C, G1]; John Paul II's "une sorte de mythe" [C, G8].

### 2e. Key works

- *Sidereus Nuncius* (Venice, March 1610); English by E. S. Carlos (1880) [C, G1, G6]; Albert Van Helden's translation (1989) [U]
- *Discourse on Floating Bodies* (1612); *Letters on Sunspots* (1613) [C, G1]

## 3. Proposed outlines (the site's person-page shape)

The shape follows `descartes` and `leibniz`: front matter (layout, lastmod, title with dates, description), breadcrumb, H1, intro paragraph, biography paragraph with an SEP link, **Key concepts** (bold label, then text), **Where X stops**, **Key works**, **See also**. ### Newton: `/positioning/persons/n/newton/`, "Isaac Newton (1642–1727)"

- **Intro (headline options):** the mathematical natural philosophy that named itself; laws, gravity and method ("hypotheses non fingo", the Rules) beside a natural philosophy that still took in God and final causes; absolute space held against Leibniz.
- **Biography:** Woolsthorpe, Trinity, plague years, Lucasian chair 1669, 1672 optics and the quarrels, Halley and the *Principia* 1687, Mint and Royal Society, the Leibniz dispute in a clause, the private alchemy and theology, the papers' history.
- **Key concepts:**
  1. *The laws of motion* (the three laws in Motte; *vis inertiae*; his credit to Galileo)
  2. *Universal gravitation* (Prop. VII; "Not that I affirm gravity to be essential"; the Bentley "absurdity" letter)
  3. *The Rules of Reasoning* (four rules, with edition dates)
  4. *"Hypotheses non fingo" and experimental philosophy* (Latin, Motte against Cohen–Whitman, his own "feigning"; Q31 analysis and synthesis)
  5. *Absolute space and time* (the Scholium; the bucket; Corollary V; God "constitutes duration and space"; Leibniz's relational reply in a clause, linking `leibniz`; Mach's critique in a clause, linking `mach`)
  6. *Optics* (1672 prism and *experimentum crucis*; rays "not coloured"; Goethe's opposition in a clause, linking `goethe`)
  7. *Natural philosophy including God* (General Scholium with the 1713/1726 word change; Q28; Q31 on moral philosophy; Cunningham's title reading attributed)
  - The calculus dispute is a clause in the biography, not its own concept.
- **Material for "Where Newton stops" (his own boundaries):** the cause of gravity "what I do not pretend to know"; the nature of light left a question; absolute space not reachable by the senses ("make no impression on the senses", as SEP quotes) [C as quoted, N2]; the readings that put Newtonian determinism on him while SEP gives it to Laplace.
- **Key works:** the 1672 paper; *Principia* (1687, 1713, 1726; Motte 1729, Cajori 1934, Cohen–Whitman 1999); *Opticks* (1704 to 1730); the Bentley letters; the theological and chronological works.
- **See also (options):** `leibniz` · `descartes` · `mach` · `locke` (friendship, under-labourer) · `goethe`. Subject: metaphilosophy *The New Philosophy*.

### Galileo: `/positioning/persons/g/galileo/`, "Galileo Galilei (1564–1642)"

- **Intro (headline options):** the mathematician who claimed the title of philosopher; the book of nature in mathematical characters; the telescope; the condemnation and its afterlife as a symbol.
- **Biography:** Pisa, medicine dropped, Padua, the telescope and *Sidereus* (1610), the court title, Lincei, sunspots and the Castelli and Christina letters, 1616, the *Assayer*, Urban VIII, the *Dialogo*, the 1633 sentence and abjuration, Arcetri, *Two New Sciences* (1638), re-interment 1737; the 1992 address in a clause.
- **Key concepts:**
  1. *The book of nature in mathematics* (§6; Day One on intensive certainty; the *filosofo geometra*)
  2. *Primary and secondary qualities* (§48 with the *corpo sensitivo* word test; SEP's "out of character")
  3. *Telescopic observations* (Moon, stars, Medicean planets, Venus, Saturn; the Copernican argument from Jupiter)
  4. *The law of fall and horizontal motion* (odd numbers, Theorem II, the cause set aside; perpetual horizontal motion, with the inertia word test; Newton's later credit)
  5. *The relativity of motion: the ship* (Day Two in Italian with English; "senza esperienza son sicuro"; the later name noted)
  6. *Scripture and nature* (the Christina letter: sense-experiences and necessary demonstrations, two truths, "come si vadia al cielo" with the Baronius attribution as later)
  7. *The Copernican trial* (1616 and 1633 in his and the court's words; "pura ipotesi matematica"; SEP's disputed points in one clause each)
- **Material for "Where Galileo stops" (his own boundaries):** essences declared unknowable (*Sunspot Letters*, as SEP quotes); causes of acceleration set aside; the tides argument for the earth's motion, which SEP calls "a mistake to modern eyes" and which did not persuade Bellarmine [C, G1]; the readings' disagreement over whether experiment or a priori demonstration carried his method.
- **Key works:** as in §2e.
- **See also (options):** `descartes` · `feyerabend` · `husserl` · `collingwood` · `hobbes` (met him; only if the page mentions it). Subject: metaphilosophy *The New Philosophy*.
