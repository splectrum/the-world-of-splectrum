# How nature narrowed: research note

Purpose: to check the narrowing of nature step by step, with the method of knowing as the driver, ending at philosophy as residue. Two targets: (1) Descartes in his own words on thinking and extended substance, the step where the knower leaves nature, with Galileo's and Locke's qualities beside it; (2) the authors who themselves draw the link between the narrowed nature and philosophy as remainder, so that the reading can be attributed rather than asserted. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked, but in a secondary source that quotes it, not in the text itself. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a paraphrase I could not open). Keys marked "not re-opened" in the Sources list were checked in an earlier round and are carried over unchanged. "My rendering" = my English from the original. OCR scans are quoted with the long s and obvious OCR errors corrected and nothing else changed.

## 1. Descartes in his own words

**Headline.** Descartes makes the cut in two moves. In the *Meditations* the self is a thinking thing, not extended, really distinct from the body. In the *Principles* each substance has one principal attribute, so body is extension alone, and physics admits nothing but what the geometers call quantity. His own sentence is that "toute ma Physique n'est autre chose que Géométrie". The knower is placed outside the object of physics by definition. One structural point needs no source beyond the books' own order: the soul, which the Paris arts course read in *De anima* among the books on nature, is treated by Descartes in Part I with God and knowledge (metaphysics, the root of his tree), and Part II on material things begins after it. That observation is my reading, not a source's claim.

- **Meditation II: "a thing that thinks."** "sum igitur praecise tantum res cogitans, id est, mens, sive animus, sive intellectus, sive ratio, voces mihi prius significationis ignotae" (AT VII 27) [C, S1]. Haldane–Ross: "to speak accurately I am not more than a thing which thinks, that is to say a mind or a soul, or an understanding, or a reason, which are terms whose significance was formerly unknown to me" [C, S2]. Then: "Sed quid igitur sum? Res cogitans. Quid est hoc? Nempe dubitans, intelligens, affirmans, negans, volens, nolens, imaginans quoque, & sentiens" (AT VII 28) [C, S1]; H–R: "But what then am I? A thing which thinks. What is a thing which thinks? It is a thing which doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels" [C, S2]. Note that *sentiens*, sensing, sits on the thinking side.
- **Meditation VI: the real distinction.** "quia tamen ex una parte claram & distinctam habeo ideam mei ipsius, quatenus sum tantum res cogitans, non extensa, & ex alia parte distinctam ideam corporis, quatenus est tantum res extensa, non cogitans, certum est me a corpore meo revera esse distinctum, & absque illo posse existere" (AT VII 78) [C, S1]. H–R: "because, on the one side, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself inasmuch as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and as, on the other, I possess a distinct idea of body, inasmuch as it is only an extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that this I [that is to say, my soul by which I am what I am], is entirely and absolutely distinct from my body, and can exist without it" [C, S2]. The brackets are H–R's additions from the French version.
- **Word test.** *res cogitans* and *res extensa* are Descartes's own Latin (Med. II, Med. VI) [C, S1]. "Cartesian dualism" is the readers' label (Burtt uses it, §3) [C, S11]. "Mind–body problem" is later still.
- **Principles I.8: the order of knowing.** "neither extension, nor figure, nor local motion, nor anything similar that can be attributed to body, pertains to our nature, and nothing save thought alone; and, consequently, that the notion we have of our mind precedes that of any corporeal thing, and is more certain" (Veitch) [C, S3].
- **Principles I.53: one principal attribute.** "una tamen est cujusque substantiae praecipua proprietas, quae ipsius naturam essentiamque constituit, & ad quam aliae omnes referuntur. Nempe extensio in longum, latum & profundum, substantiae corporeae naturam constituit; & cogitatio constituit naturam substantiae cogitantis" (AT VIII-1 25; article title "Cujusque substantiae unum esse praecipuum attributum, ut mentis cogitatio, corporis extensio") [C, S4]. Veitch: "there is, however, one principal property of every substance, which constitutes its nature or essence, and upon which all the others depend. Thus, extension in length, breadth, and depth, constitutes the nature of corporeal substance; and thought the nature of thinking substance" [C, S3].
- **Principles II.4: body is extension alone.** Title: "Naturam corporis non in pondere, duritie, colore, aut similibus; sed in sola extensione consistere" (AT VIII-1 42) [C, S4]. Veitch: "the nature of matter or body, considered in general, does not consist in its being hard, or ponderous, or coloured, or that which affects our senses in any other way, but simply in its being a substance extended in length, breadth, and depth" [C, S3]. II.3 just before: the senses "do not present to us these objects as they are in themselves", so "we will … have recourse to our understanding alone" [C, S3].
- **Principles II.64: physics admits only the geometers' quantity.** Title: "Non alia principia in Physica, quam in Geometria, vel in Mathesi abstracta, a me admitti, nec optari, quia sic omnia naturae phaenomena explicantur, & certae de iis demonstrationes dari possunt." Text: "Nam plane profiteor me nullam aliam rerum corporearum materiam agnoscere, quam illam omnimode divisibilem, figurabilem & mobilem, quam Geometrae quantitatem vocant, & pro objecto suarum demonstrationum assumunt; ac nihil plane in ipsa considerare, praeter istas divisiones, figuras & motus … Et quia sic omnia Naturae Phaenomena possunt explicari, ut in sequentibus apparebit, nulla alia Physicae principia puto esse admittenda, nec alia etiam optanda" (AT VIII-1 78–79) [C, S4].
  - My rendering: "For I frankly declare that I recognise no matter of corporeal things other than that which is divisible, shapeable and movable in every way, which the geometers call quantity and take as the object of their demonstrations; and that I consider nothing in it except those divisions, shapes and motions … And because all the phenomena of nature can be explained in this way, as will appear in what follows, I think no other principles of physics should be admitted, nor even wished for."
  - H–R give only the title in English, not the article text [C, S2]. Veitch omits II.64 [C, S3]. The brief's English ("I recognise no matter in corporeal things apart from that which the geometers call quantity") is presumably Cottingham–Stoothoff–Murdoch, CSM I 247 [U, not opened].
- **Principles IV.198–199: the qualities are motions.** "we in no way likewise apprehend that in external objects, which we call light, colour, smell, taste, sound, heat or cold … unless as the various dispositions of these objects which have the power of moving our nerves"; "beyond what is perceived by the senses, there is nothing that can be considered a phenomenon of nature", and the sensible qualities are "nothing more, at least so far as they are known to us, than certain dispositions of the objects, consisting in magnitude, figure, and motion" (Veitch) [C, S3].
- **The letters: "my physics is nothing but geometry."** To Mersenne, 27 July 1638: he has given up only abstract geometry "affin d'auoir d'autant plus de loysir de cultiuer vne autre sorte de Géométrie, qui se propose pour questions l'explication des phainomenes de la nature … il connoistra bien que toute ma Physique n'est autre chose que Géométrie" (AT II 268, letter CXXXI) [C, S5]. My rendering: "so as to have all the more leisure to cultivate another sort of geometry, which takes as its questions the explanation of the phenomena of nature … he will see that my whole physics is nothing other than geometry." And on 30 April 1639 (letter CLXI): "encore que toute ma Physique ne soit autre chose que Mechanique", although my whole physics is nothing other than mechanics (AT II c. 542) [C, S5; the page number is read off the OCR headers, U].
- **Dates.** The *Meditationes* were published in 1641 [U, standard]. The *Principia* appeared in 1644, and the French *Principes* (Picot) in 1647 [U, standard; the prior Veitch letter-preface is dated by search only]. The French wording of II.64 (AT IX-2) is not checked [U].

## 2. The companion steps: Galileo, Bacon, Locke

- **Galileo, *Il Saggiatore* (1623) §48: the qualities go to the sensitive body.** "Per lo che vo io pensando che questi sapori, odori, colori, etc., per la parte del suggetto nel quale ci par che riseggano, non sieno altro che puri nomi, ma tengano solamente lor residenza nel corpo sensitivo, sì che rimosso l'animale, sieno levate ed annichilate tutte queste qualità" [C, S6]. And: "tolti via gli orecchi le lingue e i nasi, restino bene le figure i numeri e i moti, ma non già gli odori né i sapori né i suoni, li quali fuor dell'animal vivente non credo che sieno altro che nomi" [C, S6]. What is inseparable from body: bounded, shaped, large or small, in place and time, moving or at rest, touching or not, one or many; these are "primi e reali accidenti", first and real accidents [C, S6].
  - Drake's English, as quoted by Hardin: "Hence I think that tastes, odors, colors, and so on are no more than mere names so far as the object in which we place them is concerned, and that they reside only in the consciousness. Hence if the living creature were removed, all these qualities would be wiped away and annihilated" [C as quoted, S7].
  - **Word test.** "Consciousness" is Drake's word. Galileo has *corpo sensitivo*, the sensitive body, and *l'animale*, *l'animal vivente* [C, S6]. In Galileo's own words the qualities move into the living, sensing animal, not into a mind. The move to a mind is Descartes's. A page that quotes Drake should say whose word "consciousness" is. "Primary" has a Galilean root (*primi e reali accidenti*); "primary and secondary qualities" as a pair is Locke's (below).
  - The same book (§6) puts the mathematical language of nature in the "grandissimo libro" of the universe, "scritto in lingua matematica" [C, S8].
- **Bacon, *The Advancement of Learning* (1605), Book II: man as the mind turned on itself.** "In philosophy the contemplations of man do either penetrate unto God, or are circumferred to nature, or are reflected or reverted upon himself. Out of which several inquiries there do arise three knowledges—divine philosophy, natural philosophy, and human philosophy or humanity" [C, S9]. The division is already on the site; what this adds is the image of knowledge "reflected or reverted upon himself" for the human part.
- **Locke, *Essay* II.viii: primary and secondary qualities.** "These, which I call ORIGINAL or PRIMARY qualities of body, are wholly inseparable from it" (solidity, extension, motion or rest, number, figure) (§9); "the ideas of primary qualities of bodies are resemblances of them … but the ideas produced in us by these secondary qualities have no resemblance of them at all" (§15); "Take away the sensation of them; let not the eyes see light or colours … and all colours, tastes, odours, and sounds, AS THEY ARE SUCH PARTICULAR IDEAS, vanish and cease, and are reduced to their causes, i.e. bulk, figure, and motion of parts" (§17) [C, S10].
- **Locke against the linear story: spirits are still inside natural philosophy.** In IV.xxi.2, *physikē*, or natural philosophy, is "The knowledge of things, as they are in their own proper beings … whereby I mean not only matter and body, but spirits also … whether it be God himself, angels, spirits, bodies" [C, S10]. The third science is *sēmeiōtikē*, "the doctrine of signs", whose business is "to consider the nature of signs, the mind makes use of for the understanding of things, or conveying its knowledge to others"; "IDEAS and WORDS as the great instruments of knowledge" (IV.xxi.4) [C, S10]. And in IV.iii.26, "how far soever human industry may advance useful and experimental philosophy in physical things, SCIENTIFICAL will still be out of our reach" [C, S10].
  - **What this changes.** In 1690, after Descartes, the *field* called natural philosophy still takes in God and spirits, as Newton's General Scholium does in 1713 [C, S16]. What narrowed first was *nature as the object of physics* (Galileo, Descartes); *the name* "natural philosophy" narrowed later (Thomson and Tait, 1867 [C, S16]). A page should keep the two apart.
  - The Gutenberg text is from a later edition; its §10 reads "[not in early editions]" [C, S10]. The *Essay* is dated 1690 on its title page [U].

## 3. Who draws the link, in their own words

The question for each author: do they draw the link *narrowed nature (or the method) → the knower or mind as philosophy's leftover*? Four answers are possible: **full** (both halves), **half** (nature narrowed and knower expelled, but no claim about philosophy's remainder), **residue only** (philosophy as remainder, but not tied to the narrowing of nature), **counter** (closes the remainder another way).

**Full: they draw the link.**

- **E. A. Burtt, *The Metaphysical Foundations of Modern Physical Science* (London: Kegan Paul, imprint 1925; often dated 1924; revised 1932)** [C, S11].
  - Intro, pp. 2–3: "The central place of epistemology in modern philosophy is no accident; it is a most natural corollary of something still more pervasive and significant, a conception of man himself, and especially of his relation to the world around him. Knowledge was not a problem for the ruling philosophy of the Middle Ages … That people subsequently came to consider knowledge a problem implies that they had been led to accept certain different beliefs about the nature of man and about the things which he tries to understand."
  - On Galileo, p. 80: "It was inevitable that in these circumstances man should now appear to be outside of the real world; man is hardly more than a bundle of secondary qualities. Observe that the stage is fully set for the Cartesian dualism — on the one side the primary, the mathematical realm; on the other the realm of man … Man begins to appear for the first time in the history of thought as an irrelevant spectator and insignificant effect of the great mathematical system which is the substance of reality."
  - On Newton, pp. 236–237: "that view of the cosmos which saw in man a puny, irrelevant spectator … of the vast mathematical system"; "The gloriously romantic universe of Dante and Milton … had now been swept away. Space was identified with the realm of geometry, time with the continuity of number. The world that people had thought themselves living in … was crowded now into minute corners in the brains of scattered organic beings. The really important world outside was a world hard, cold, colourless, silent, and dead."
  - Verdict: **full.** This is the closest single source for the reading: mathematical nature → man as spectator → epistemology at philosophy's centre. He takes "irrelevant spectator" from his reading of Russell's "A Free Man's Worship" (intro, p. 10) [C, S11].
- **R. G. Collingwood, *The Idea of Nature* (Oxford: Clarendon, 1945; opened in the OUP paperback reprint of 1972)** [C, S12].
  - On Galileo, pp. 102–104: "these terms were the exclusion of everything qualitative and the restriction of natural reality to a complex of quantities … The principle of science as understood by Galileo is that nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable." Then: "Secondly, that which has been extruded from the concept of nature must find a lodgement somewhere else in metaphysical theory. These homeless entities fall into two main divisions: first, qualities in general; secondly, minds. According to Galileo, whose views on this subject were adopted by Descartes and Locke and became what may be called the orthodoxy of the seventeenth century, minds form a class of beings outside nature, and qualities are explained as appearances to minds."
  - Intro, pp. 5–7: each cosmological movement "was followed by a movement in which the focus of interest shifted from nature to mind" (Socrates for the Greeks); "when in the eighteenth century the centre of gravity in philosophical thought swung over from the theory of nature to the theory of mind, Berkeley being the critical point here as Socrates was for the Greeks, the problem of nature inevitably stated itself in this form: how can mind have any connexion with something utterly alien to itself … namely nature? This was the question, at bottom the only question, concerning nature which exercised the great philosophers of mind, Berkeley, Hume, Kant, Hegel."
  - Verdict: **full**, and the cleanest wording of the "complement" reading: what is extruded from nature needs "a lodgement somewhere else", and philosophy's centre of gravity moves to mind. His frame is a recurring pattern (Greek, Renaissance, modern), which a page would need to keep.
- **Whitehead, *Science and the Modern World* (New York: Macmillan, 1925), ch. III "The Century of Genius"** [C, S13].
  - p. 77: the mind's sensations "are projected by the mind so as to clothe appropriate bodies in external nature … Thus nature gets credit which should in truth be reserved for ourselves: the rose for its scent: the nightingale for his song: and the sun for his radiance. The poets are entirely mistaken … Nature is a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless; merely the hurrying of material, endlessly, meaninglessly." He says this is "the practical outcome of the characteristic scientific philosophy which closed the seventeenth century" (an ironic summary of that view, not his own).
  - p. 79: "The enormous success of the scientific abstractions, yielding on the one hand matter with its simple location in space and time, and on the other hand mind, perceiving, suffering, reasoning, but not interfering, has foisted onto philosophy the task of accepting them as the most concrete rendering of fact. Thereby, modern philosophy has been ruined. It has oscillated in a complex manner between three extremes. There are the dualists … and the two varieties of monists, those who put mind inside matter, and those who put matter inside mind."
  - Verdict: **full**, from the side of a critic: the abstractions of science hand philosophy its agenda (mind against matter).
- **Ortega y Gasset, *¿Qué es filosofía?* (1929), Lesson II.** It is on the site, quoted from the Spanish; I did not re-open it. The philosopher "se avergonzó de no ser físico", and philosophy was "reducida, o poco menos, a la teoría del conocimiento" between 1860 and 1920 [C as on the site, S20]. Verdict: **full in outline** (physics' prestige → philosophy reduced to theory of knowledge). It is about the nineteenth century and physics, though, not about the narrowing of nature.

**Half: nature narrowed and the knower expelled, but no claim about philosophy's remainder.**

- **Koyré, *From the Closed World to the Infinite Universe* (Baltimore: Johns Hopkins, 1957), Introduction, pp. 1–2** [C, S14]: the revolution "can be described roughly as bringing forth the destruction of the Cosmos, that is, the disappearance, from philosophically and scientifically valid concepts, of the conception of the world as a finite, closed, and hierarchically ordered whole … and its replacement by an indefinite and even infinite universe … This, in turn, implies the discarding by scientific thought of all considerations based upon value-concepts, such as perfection, harmony, meaning and aim, and finally the utter devalorization of being, the divorce of the world of value and the world of facts." On the other readings he reports: man "lost his place in the world, or, more correctly perhaps, lost the very world in which he was living and about which he was thinking"; "it is indeed impossible to separate the philosophical from the purely scientific aspects of this process". Preface, p. viii: "the destruction of the cosmos and the geometrization of space". Verdict: **half.** He names the first step in the word "Cosmos" and the value/fact split, but he does not say the knower became philosophy's remainder.
- **Husserl, *Crisis* (1936), §9.** As SEP quotes Carr's translation: Galileo's achievement was the "mathematizing reinterpretation of nature" (Hua VI 54; Carr 53), and people came to "take for true being what is actually a method" (Hua VI 52; Carr 51) [C as quoted, S15]. The second line is the best one-line statement that *the method* is the driver. The German "entdeckender und verdeckender Genius" (discovering and concealing genius) is attested in a PhilPapers abstract [C as quoted, S21]. The §10–11 headings (dualism arising from the exemplary status of physics; dualism as "presupposition for the specialization of the sciences" and as the foundation of naturalistic psychology) are from memory [U]. If they check out, §11 draws the link to specialisation directly. Verdict: **half, possibly full** (open check).
- **Whitehead, *The Concept of Nature* (1920), ch. II** [C, S16]: "What I am essentially protesting against is the bifurcation of nature into two systems of reality … Thus there would be two natures, one is the conjecture and the other is the dream"; "The meeting point of these two natures is the mind, the causal nature being influent and the apparent nature being effluent." He calls the softer form "the theory of psychic additions", "secondary qualities which are only the mind's way of perceiving nature", "the outcome of common-sense in retreat", which "lands us to eighteenth and nineteenth century materialism". He also defines natural philosophy as "homogeneous" thought that "should never ask, what is in the mind and what is in nature" (ch. I–II) [C, S16]. Verdict: **half** as diagnosis, and at the same time one of the steps: his own natural philosophy leaves the knower out by design.
- **Schrödinger, *Mind and Matter* (1958; Tarner lectures 1956), "the principle of objectivation"**, as Hunt quotes it: "a certain simplification which we adopt in order to master the infinitely intricate problem of nature. Without being aware of it and without being rigorously systematic about it, we exclude the Subject of Cognizance from the domain of nature that we endeavor to understand. We step with our own person back into the part of an onlooker who does not belong to the world, which by this very procedure becomes an objective world" [C as quoted, S17]. The Tarner date and the 1958 imprint are standard [U]. Verdict: **half**, but the clearest scientist's statement that excluding the knower is a *method*. The site has a `schrodinger` page.
- **Nagel, *Mind and Cosmos* (2012)**, as Orr quotes it: physical accounts "leave out something essential, without which there would be no mind. And what they leave out is just what was deliberately left out of the physical world by Descartes and Galileo in order to form the modern concept of the physical, namely, subjective appearances" [C as quoted, S18]. Verdict: **half.** The site has a `nagel` page; the line is not on it.
- **Lovejoy, *The Revolt Against Dualism* (Open Court / Norton, 1930), ch. I, p. 29** [C, S19]: the bifurcation of nature "was the first thing needful, if it was to be made intelligible. The world of 'mental' entities served as an isolation-camp for all the 'wild data,' the refractory and anomalous facts, which would have disturbed the tidiness and good order of the physical universe"; psychophysical dualism "has in fact served throughout history as the safeguard of developing physical science". Verdict: **half, from the defending side.** The mind is the remainder of nature *made in order to* order nature. This is the residue reading inside nature, and Lovejoy approves of it. He also says both dualisms "converged in seventeenth-century philosophy in the hypothesis of 'ideas' (in the Cartesian and Lockian sense)" (ch. I; page not read off the scan) [C, S19].

**Residue only: philosophy as remainder, not tied to the narrowing of nature.**

- **Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), ch. XV** [C, S22]: the residue passage, already on the site. Two points matter for the reading:
  - His own example of a subject that left is the mind: "the study of the human mind, which was a part of philosophy, has now been separated from philosophy and has become the science of psychology."
  - What stays includes the knower's place in the universe: "Is consciousness a permanent part of the universe … or is it a transitory accident on a small planet …?"
  - So on Russell's own terms the residue is not "the knower" but the unanswered questions, among them the knower's place. He does not tie the residue to how nature was narrowed: what decides is "definite knowledge".
- **Russell, "On Scientific Method in Philosophy" (Herbert Spencer lecture, Oxford, 1914; in *Mysticism and Logic*, 1918)** [C, S23]:
  - "If the notion of the universe and the notion of good and evil are extruded from scientific philosophy, it may be asked what specific problems remain for the philosopher as opposed to the man of science?"
  - "there are no propositions of which the 'universe' is the subject".
  - Of Kant's problem: "the problem of logic can be solved exactly and perfectly; the problem of physics can probably be solved … the problem of theory of knowledge, however, remains very obscure".
  - Scientific method "compels us to abandon the hope of solving many of the more ambitious and humanly interesting problems of traditional philosophy. Some of these it relegates … to special sciences, others it shows to be such as our capacities are essentially incapable of solving".
  - Verdict: here the method narrows *philosophy*, extruding even the universe as a whole. That is a mirror of the nature narrative, worth knowing: the cosmos leaves philosophy too.
- **Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (London: Allen & Unwin, 1946), Introduction, pp. 9–10** [C, S24]: "All definite knowledge—so I should contend—belongs to science; all dogma as to what surpasses definite knowledge belongs to theology. But between theology and science there is a No Man's Land, exposed to attack from both sides; this No Man's Land is philosophy." Among its questions: "Is the world divided into mind and matter, and, if so, what is mind and what is matter? … Is man what he seems to the astronomer, a tiny lump of impure carbon and water impotently crawling on a small and unimportant planet? Or is he what he appears to Hamlet?" Verdict: **residue only**, but the first of his residue questions is Descartes's division.
- **Windelband, "Was ist Philosophie?" (*Präludien*, Freiburg and Tübingen, 1884)** [C, S25]. My renderings throughout.
  - pp. 18–19, his account of the post-Hegelian situation: "Alle einzelnen Gegenstände sind an die besonderen Wissenschaften vertheilt, die Philosophie ist wie der Dichter, der bei der Theilung der Welt zu spät kam" (all particular objects are shared out among the special sciences; philosophy is like the poet who came too late to the division of the world). Philosophy is "wie der König Lear, der all sein Gut unter seine Kinder vertheilte" (like King Lear, who divided all his goods among his children). The name passes to a theory of science: "sie ist nicht mehr die Lehre vom Weltall … sie ist die Lehre vom Wissen, keine Metaphysik der Dinge, sondern eine 'Metaphysik des Wissens'" (no longer the doctrine of the universe … the doctrine of knowledge, not a metaphysics of things but a "metaphysics of knowledge").
  - p. 19: in the differentiated state of the sciences philosophy is partly "das nervöse Centralorgan, in welchem der Lebensproceß der übrigen Organe zum Bewußtsein kommen soll", the nervous central organ in which the life process of the other organs is to come to consciousness. And: "Die Geschichte des Namens der Philosophie ist die Geschichte der Culturbedeutung der Wissenschaft", the history of the name of philosophy is the history of the cultural meaning of science.
  - pp. 32–34: "die einzig übrig bleibende Möglichkeit", the only possibility left, of marking philosophy off by its object; "In dieser Hinsicht bleibt der Philosophie Nichts zu thun übrig … sie findet alle Gruppen von Gegenständen durch besondere Wissenschaften besetzt" (in this respect nothing is left for philosophy to do; it finds every group of objects occupied by special sciences); "Das Object, das für sie übrig bleibt, sind die Beurtheilungen" (the object that remains for it is the valuations).
  - The poet image is Schiller's "Die Teilung der Erde" [U as to his source].
  - Verdict: **residue only, but in so many words** ("übrig bleibt"). What is left is knowledge of knowledge and the valuations, not nature's complement. The division he describes is of *objects*, not of method narrowing nature.
- **Dilthey, the "other half of the globus intellectualis" (1883)**: on the site, not re-opened [C as on the site, S20]. Verdict: a split of knowledge into halves; no residue claim.
- **Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979).** The site quotes the "tribunal of pure reason" [C as on the site, S20]. That philosophy took up the "theory of knowledge" with Locke and Kant is Wikipedia's summary [C that Wikipedia says so, S26]. The claim that Kant and the nineteenth-century professionalisation made epistemology philosophy's own subject once the sciences had left (ch. III) is from memory [U]. The book is not openly available. Verdict: probably **full** (mind invented by Descartes → epistemology as the discipline's foundation → philosophy's autonomy from science), but unverified.

**Counter: closing the remainder another way.**

- **Comte, *Cours de philosophie positive*, lesson 1 (1830)** [C, S27]: introspective psychology is "cette psychologie illusoire, dernière transformation de la théologie" (that illusory psychology, the last transformation of theology). The intellect is to be studied by physiology and by observing the sciences' own procedures, "les théories scientifiques comme autant de grands faits logiques" (scientific theories as so many great logical facts). And: "l'esprit humain peut observer directement tous les phénomènes, excepté les siens propres. Car, par qui serait faite l'observation?" (the human mind can observe all phenomena directly except its own; for by whom would the observation be made?). My renderings. Verdict: **counter.** He sees the knower problem but gives it to physiology and to the history of the sciences, not to a philosophical remainder. His philosophy's field is "l'étude des généralités scientifiques" (on the site).

**Reception, not verdict.** Burtt (1924/25), Collingwood (1945), Koyré (1957) and Whitehead (1925) are the classic "scientific revolution" historiography. The actors'-categories school (Cunningham, Cunningham and Williams 1993) and the reviewers who find "the old big picture … disintegrating" [C, S28] put the single seventeenth-century revolution in question. A page that leans on Burtt or Koyré should say "on Burtt's reading". Galileo's own text also cuts against Burtt's "Man … outside of the real world": Galileo moves the qualities into the living animal, not out of the world (§2).

**What the re-check changes:**
- **Order.** Steps 4 to 7 are one century, not a sequence. Bacon (1605) and Galileo (1623) come before Descartes (1641/44), and Locke (1690) and Newton (1713) come after him while still keeping God and spirits inside natural philosophy. Better: a seventeenth-century step with three moves (the qualities go to the sensing body; the knower is set outside extended nature; physics becomes geometry), and beside it the *name* "natural philosophy" still wide (Locke, Newton) until it narrows to physics in the nineteenth century.
- **"Only the mathematical counts"** starts with Galileo's §6 and Descartes's II.64, not with Kant. Kant makes it a criterion of "science proper".
- **Galileo's word.** The qualities go to the *sensitive body*, not to a mind (the word test in §2). Descartes is the step to mind.
- **Russell's residue is not the knower.** In his own example the mind leaves philosophy too (psychology). "The knower at the centre of the residue" is a reading. The sources that support it are Burtt (epistemology's centrality "no accident"), Collingwood (minds as "homeless entities" needing "a lodgement"; the centre of gravity swinging "from the theory of nature to the theory of mind"), Whitehead 1925 (philosophy saddled with mind and matter) and Ortega (philosophy reduced to the theory of knowledge). Russell supports only the residue half, Windelband the remainder in so many words, and Comte is against it.
- **Lovejoy and Schrödinger** give the reading's inner side: the mind as the remainder that the method sets apart *within* nature (Lovejoy's "isolation-camp"; Schrödinger's "simplification … to master … nature").
