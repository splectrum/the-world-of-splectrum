# Metaphilosophy, the Presocratics against three questions: research note

Purpose: to source a short section, placed before "A way of life" on the Greek and Latin page, on how the first Greek thinkers, or their ancient reporters, answered (1) What is philosophy? (2) What is its point? (3) How is it done? Headlines only. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

## 1. What it is

- **Not yet "philosophy".** Hadot, ch. 1 "Philosophy before Philosophy": "The words belonging to the philosophia family did not in fact appear until the fifth century B.C., and the term philosophia itself was not defined until the fourth century B.C., by Plato. Aristotle, however, and with him the entire tradition of the history of philosophy, applied the word 'philosophers' to the first Greek thinkers" [C, S1 p. 9]. And in ch. 2: "It is almost certain that Presocratic philosophers of the seventh and sixth centuries B.C., such as Xenophanes and Parmenides, knew neither the adjective philosophos nor the verb philosophein … much less the noun philosophia", and "despite certain ancient but very controversial evidence to the contrary, this was probably also the case for Pythagoras and Heraclitus" [C, S1 p. 15]. The SEP agrees that "philosophers" "is almost certainly not how they could have described themselves" [C, S2].
- **Their own name: *historia*, inquiry, into *phusis*.** Hadot: "The object of this intellectual undertaking, which they called historia, was universal phusis" [C, S1 p. 10]. His note cites Heraclitus B35 and *Phaedo* 96a7 [C, S1 notes]. Elsewhere: "the Presocratics apparently designated their intellectual undertaking as a historia—that is, an inquiry" [C, S1 p. 16]. Hadot glosses *phusis* as what "originally meant the beginning, the development, and the result of the process by which a thing constitutes itself" [C, S1 p. 10].
- **Plato's name for it.** *Phaedo* 96a: "When I was young, Cebes, I was tremendously eager for the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature. I thought it was a glorious thing to know the causes of everything, why each thing comes into being and why it perishes and why it exists" (Fowler) [C, S3]. The Greek is ταύτης τῆς σοφίας ἣν δὴ καλοῦσι περὶ φύσεως ἱστορίαν [C, S3]. Jowett has "that department of philosophy which is called the investigation of nature" [C, S4]. The "philosophy" there is Jowett's word; the Greek says "wisdom" (*sophia*).
- **"Peri physeos" as a title.** Burnet: the title "so commonly given to philosophical works of the sixth and fifth centuries B.C." does not mean the authors used it, "for early prose writings had no titles" [C, S5 §VII and n. 15]. He also says the titles of verse works are "of later date" [C, S5 n. on Xenophanes]. Galen's list of ancient works all titled *On Nature* [U; not opened]. The headline is that the title is a later librarians' label and Plato's phrase is the early evidence.
- **Aristotle's frame: the first philosophers and their principles.** *Metaphysics* I.3: "Of the first philosophers, then, most thought the principles which were of the nature of matter were the only principles of all things"; "Thales, the founder of this type of philosophy, says the principle is water" (Ross) [C, S6]. The Greek is τῶν δὴ πρώτων φιλοσοφησάντων … ἀρχὰς [C, S7, 983b]. He brings them in to test his four causes: "we shall either find another kind of cause, or be more convinced of the correctness of those which we now maintain" [C, S6]. The SEP: Aristotle "can trace a line of continuity of both subject and method from their work to his own", though he thinks them "unsuccessful and even amateurish" [C, S2]. Burnet calls *archē* in this sense "purely Aristotelian", "it does not occur once in the genuine fragments" [C as Burnet, S5]. That Theophrastus/Simplicius credit Anaximander with the word is a dispute over a detail [U]. One clause at most.
- **Aristotle's word *physiologoi*.** It occurs at *Metaphysics* I.8, 990a3: the Pythagoreans "agreeing with the other *physiologoi*" (τοῖς ἄλλοις φυσιολόγοις) [C, S7]. Ross renders the word "natural philosophers" in I.8 [C, S6, 989b/990a].
- **The one early "lover of wisdom": Heraclitus B35.** SEP: "those who are lovers of wisdom must be inquirers into many things" (DK22B35/LM9D40). The word "does not have the special sense that it acquires in the works of Plato and Aristotle" [C, S2]. Burnet: "Men that love wisdom must be acquainted with very many things indeed" (Bywater 49 = DK 35) [C, S5; DK number C, S8]. Authenticity: Hadot notes that Dumont "expresses doubts about the authenticity of the word 'philosophy'; so too Diels-Kranz". Hadot also writes: "If Heraclitus really did speak of 'philosophers' in his fragment 35, then he established a link between philosophy and inquiry" [C, S1 notes to ch. 2]. The Greek is ἵστορας φιλοσόφους ἄνδρας [U; recalled, not opened].
- **And against mere learning: B40.** "The learning of many things teacheth not understanding, else would it have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, and again Xenophanes and Hekataios" (Burnet, Bywater 16 = DK 40) [C, S5, S8]. The SEP has "Much learning does not teach understanding" (DK22B40) [C, S2].
- **Earliest attestation of the verb, as context.** Herodotus I.30, Croesus to Solon: φιλοσοφέων γῆν πολλὴν θεωρίης εἵνεκεν ἐπελήλυθας [C, S9]. Godley: "as one who loves learning you have traveled much of the world for the sake of seeing it" [C, S9]. Hadot (Chase): "since you have a taste for wisdom, you have visited many lands because of your desire to see" [C, S1 p. 15–16]. Hadot: "It is perhaps in Herodotus' work that we find the first mention of 'philosophical' activity" [C, S1 p. 15].

## 2. Its point

- **Anaxagoras, to contemplate.** *Eudemian Ethics* I.5, 1216a10–15: "when somebody persisted in putting various difficulties of this sort to Anaxagoras and went on asking for what object one should choose to come into existence rather than not, he replied by saying, 'For the sake of contemplating the heavens and the whole order of the universe.' Anaxagoras therefore thought that the alternative of being alive was valuable for the sake of some kind of knowledge" (Rackham) [C, S10]. In the Greek he answers with θεωρῆσαι, and the report is introduced by φασίν, "they say" [C, S10]. Diogenes Laertius II.10: "Being asked to what end he had been born, he replied, 'To study sun and moon and heavens'" (Hicks) [C, S11]. Both are later reports, anecdotes.
- **Aristotle: to know, for nothing else.** *Metaphysics* I.2, from "the history of the earliest philosophers": "it is owing to their wonder that men both now begin and at first began to philosophize … since they philosophized in order to escape from ignorance, evidently they were pursuing science in order to know, and not for any utilitarian end" (Ross) [C, S6]. The site's earlier research already holds the wonder locus, so it is only noted here. It is checked in Ross. The Bekker line is 982b12ff [U].
- **The useless contemplator, and the rejoinder.** Plato, *Theaetetus* 174a: Thales, "while he was studying the stars and looking upwards, he fell into a pit"; "The same jest applies to all who pass their lives in philosophy" (Fowler) [C, S12]. Aristotle, *Politics* I, 1259a: Thales, "taunted with the uselessness of philosophy", rented the olive presses ahead of a good crop, "so proving that it is easy for philosophers to be rich if they choose, but this is not what they care about" (Rackham) [C, S13]. Both are anecdote and reception.
- **A way of life: Pythagoras.** *Republic* 600a–b: Pythagoras "was himself especially honored for this, and his successors, even to this day, denominating a certain way of life the Pythagorean, are distinguished among their contemporaries" (Shorey) [C, S14]. The Greek is Πυθαγόρειον τρόπον … τοῦ βίου [C, S14]. This is the hinge to the next section.
- **Hadot on the prehistory of the way of life.** "We can therefore assume that a prehistory of spiritual exercises existed among the Presocratic thinkers and in Archaic Greece. Unfortunately, all that we know about the Presocratics is extremely lacunary, the testimonies are late, and the fragments … are very difficult to interpret" (ch. 9) [C, S1 p. 180]. He adds that thought about *phusis* "was often closely linked to ethical considerations, as it is in Heraclitus, and especially in Democritus" [C, S1 p. 21]. That gives a hedged headline, not a thesis.

## 3. How it is done

- **Xenophanes B18: seek, not wait for revelation.** Burnet: "The gods have not revealed all things to men from the beginning, but by seeking they find in time what is better" [C, S5, fr. 18]. SEP: "Indeed not even from the beginning did the gods indicate all things to mortals, but, in time, inquiring, they discover better" (DKB18/LMD53). The SEP calls it "optimistic about the capacities of human intelligence" [C, S2].
- **Xenophanes B34: no one knows clearly.** Burnet: "There never was nor will be a man who has certain knowledge about the gods and about all the things I speak of. Even if he should chance to say the complete truth, yet he himself knows not that it is so. But all may have their fancy" [C, S5, fr. 34]. SEP: "the clear and certain truth no man has seen … but opinion is found over all" (DKB34/LMD49). "Whether this is global or limited skepticism is controversial" [C, S2]. That is detail; the headline is that inquiry is joined to a limit.
- **Heraclitus: the senses, read with understanding, and himself.** B55: "The things that can be seen, heard, and learned are what I prize the most" (Burnet, Bywater 13 = DK 55) [C, S5, S8]. B107: "Eyes and ears are bad witnesses to men if they have souls that understand not their language" (Burnet, Bywater 4 = DK 107) [C, S5, S8]. B101: "I have sought for myself" (Burnet, Bywater 80 = DK 101) [C, S5, S8]. The SEP has "I enquired into myself" [C, S2]. The SEP's summary: the evidence of the senses is important, but "careful and thoughtful inquiry is also necessary"; the evidence "must be interpreted carefully" [C, S2].
- **Parmenides B7: judge by *logos*.** Burnet: "nor let habit by its much experience force thee to cast upon this way a wandering eye or sounding ear or tongue; but judge by argument the much disputed proof uttered by me" [C, S5]. SEP: "judge by reasoning (logos) the much-battled testing spoken by me" (DKB7/LMD8). Here "Logos … means thinking or reasoning" [C, S2]. Burnet's numbering here is fr. 1 lines 33–36, with DK B7 [U for the Burnet-to-DK line match; content matches the SEP's B7].

## 4. The word test

- **"Presocratic" is a modern label.** It was "coined by Johann August Eberhard (in the German version 'vorsokratische Philosophie') in a book on the history of philosophy published in 1788" (Graham, reporting Laks) [C as Laks, S15]. It was made canonical by Diels, *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (1903; Kranz's 6th ed. 1951) [C as NDPR, S15]. The SEP: "coined in the eighteenth century, was made current by Hermann Diels … meant to mark a contrast between Socrates who was interested in moral problems, and his predecessors" [C, S2]. Eberhard's book title is *Allgemeine Geschichte der Philosophie* [U].
- **The dispute over the label (headline-level).** The SEP's objections: the label is inaccurate as chronology, since "the last of them were contemporaneous with Socrates and even Plato". "Several of the early Greek thinkers explored questions about ethics". It "may also suggest that these thinkers are somehow inferior". "Some scholars now deliberately avoid the term" [C, S2]. Laks, *The Concept of Presocratic Philosophy* (French 2006; English trans. G. W. Most, Princeton 2018), sets a Socratic-Ciceronian reading (Socrates turned philosophy from nature to man) against a Platonic-Aristotelian one (a change of method, "from a philosophy of things to a philosophy of the concept"). He also asks in what sense they were philosophers at all [C as NDPR, S15]. Laks and Most's Loeb *Early Greek Philosophy* (9 vols, 2016) "pointedly" avoid "Presocratic". They do so in order "not to favor the main line of the physiologoi … to the neglect of the broader religious and humanistic currents", and they place Socrates among the Sophists (Mourelatos) [C as BMCR, S16].
- **Own term against translator's.** *historia* / *historas* (inquiry, inquirers) is the early word. Burnet's "acquainted with" in B35 and Jowett's "department of philosophy" in *Phaedo* 96a hide it, and "investigation" (Fowler) and "inquirers" (SEP) keep it [C, S3, S4, S5, S2]. *archē* as "principle" is Aristotle's term (Burnet) [C as Burnet]. *physiologoi* is Aristotle's word [C, S7]. *philosophos* in B35 is doubted [C as Hadot].
- **Hadot on the Presocratics.** Yes, he says something: ch. 1 "Philosophy before Philosophy", ch. 2 on the word, and ch. 9 "Prehistory" [C, S1]. That is the backbone.

## 5. Draft section (for discussion)

Placed after "The word", before "A way of life". Title option: **Before the word: the inquiry into nature**.

> **What it is.** The first Greek thinkers did not call themselves philosophers. In [Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)'s account the words of the *philosophia* family "did not in fact appear until the fifth century B.C."; it was [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/), "and with him the entire tradition of the history of philosophy", who applied the word "philosophers" to them. Their own word was *historia*, inquiry, and its object "was universal phusis". [Plato](/positioning/persons/p/plato/)'s Socrates recalls his youthful eagerness "for the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature", *peri physeos historia* (*Phaedo* 96a); the title *On Nature* given to their books is later. Aristotle read them as his forerunners in the search for causes: "Of the first philosophers, then, most thought the principles which were of the nature of matter were the only principles of all things", with [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus) "the founder of this type of philosophy" (*Metaphysics* I.3). One early fragment joins the two words: "Men that love wisdom must be acquainted with very many things indeed" ([Heraclitus](/positioning/persons/h/heraclitus/) B35, Burnet), where "acquainted with" renders *historas*, inquirers; whether Heraclitus wrote "lovers of wisdom" is doubted. He also held that "The learning of many things teacheth not understanding" (B40).
>
> **Its point.** Asked why one should choose to be born rather than not, [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras), so the story goes, answered "For the sake of contemplating the heavens and the whole order of the universe" (Aristotle, *Eudemian Ethics* 1216a). Aristotle generalised: the first philosophers began in wonder and "philosophized in order to escape from ignorance", "in order to know, and not for any utilitarian end" (*Metaphysics* I.2). A second answer points ahead: the *Republic* knows "a certain way of life the Pythagorean" (600b).
>
> **How it is done.** By seeking rather than by revelation: "The gods have not revealed all things to men from the beginning, but by seeking they find in time what is better" ([Xenophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophanes) B18); and with a limit: "There never was nor will be a man who has certain knowledge about the gods and about all the things I speak of" (B34). By the senses read with understanding: "Eyes and ears are bad witnesses to men if they have souls that understand not their language" (Heraclitus B107); and by turning inward: "I have sought for myself" (B101). [Parmenides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenides)'s goddess warns against "a wandering eye or sounding ear or tongue": "judge by argument" (*logos*, B7).
>
> The name "Presocratic" is modern, coined by J. A. Eberhard in 1788 and made standard by Hermann Diels's *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (1903). It is contested: some of these thinkers were Socrates' contemporaries, several took up ethics, and the newest edition, Laks and Most's *Early Greek Philosophy* (2016), avoids the term.

Fragments in Burnet's translation throughout (*Early Greek Philosophy*, 2nd ed. 1908, Gutenberg; the Heraclitus wording is the same in the 3rd-ed. text on Wikisource, which is arranged by DK number). The SEP's renderings keep "inquiring" in B18 and "inquirers" in B35, if a translation closer to *historia* is wanted; its translator is not stated (see open checks). The "In one view" table would gain a row: *Early Greek thinkers | inquiry into nature (historia) | to contemplate the order of the whole | seeking; the senses read by logos*.
