# Philosophy in Africa: research note

Purpose: to find what African sources say on the three metaphilosophical questions (1. What is philosophy? 2. What is its point? 3. How should one do it?), from ancient Egypt to the twentieth-century debate, for a possible Metaphilosophy page built like the India and China pages: an intro on the words and the naming dispute, then stages, then an "In one view" table. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Already checked in an earlier round and not redone here: Tempels (1945), Hountondji's "set of texts" and "ethnophilosophy" (the 1983 *Radical Philosophy* extract), Oruka's four trends (1978, published 1981) and the later six, Wiredu's conceptual decolonisation as the IEP quotes it. Those findings stand. Below they are only cited where a new line adds to them.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, keyed to the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = wording checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a source I could not open).

## 1. The words and the naming dispute (for the intro)

- **No common word.** There is no single precolonial word to test, because the languages are many. Each stage below has its own term. In Egypt it is *sbꜣyt*, "teaching, instruction", the name of the genre [C, A2 (tertiary)]. In Ethiopia it is Ge'ez *ḥatäta*, "inquiry, investigation" [C, B3; B4 renders it "Inquiries"]. In Timbuktu the terms are Arabic: *falsafa*, *kalām*, *fiqh*, *manṭiq* [C, C1]. The "philosophy" in "Bantu philosophy" is Tempels' word (checked earlier). The "philosophy" in Littmann's Latin title *Philosophi abessini* (1904) is the editor's word; the Hatata itself, in Sumner's English, speaks of "wisdom", "investigation", "intelligence" and "reason", never of philosophy [C, B1, full-text search].
- **The label is the dispute.** Hountondji's French title puts the phrase in quotation marks, *Sur la « philosophie africaine »* (checked earlier). Diagne gave the label its own history: "'Philosophie africaine': histoire d'une expression" (2015) [C as title, A6]. **Proposed page name: "Philosophy in Africa".** It follows the series, which names places, and it avoids taking a side on "African philosophy", which is the contested term. One point for the intro: Hountondji's definition ("texts written by Africans") goes by author, while "in Africa" goes by place. Amo (§6) falls inside the first and outside the second. That contrast is my inference, not a source's.
- **The question has a history.** In Diagne's account Hegel came first, for whom Africa was "exiled from reason, civilisation, true monotheistic religion and philosophical thinking" (Diagne's paraphrase), then Lévy-Bruhl's "mentality", then Tempels, then the critique of ethnophilosophy [C, C1 p. 22–24]. Outlaw (SEP) names the "intellectual annexation [of Egypt] to Europe that was urged by Hegel in *The Philosophy of History*" [C, A5]. If the page states Hegel on Africa, `hegel` has a backing gap.

## 2. Ancient Egypt: the instructions

**Q2/Q3 in the text's own words (Gunn's 1906 translation, the only full one I could open).**
- **The genre.** The text calls itself "the proverbs of fair speech … when instructing the ignorant in the knowledge of exactness in fair-speaking" (prologue B) [C, A1]. The instructions claim Old Kingdom authors but are generally dated to Middle Kingdom composition, the false attribution being "perhaps intended to give the texts greater authority" [C, A2]. That is a detail to state in a clause.
- **Knowledge has no limit, so learning is never finished.** "Be not proud because thou art learned; but discourse with the ignorant man, as with the sage. For no limit can be set to skill, neither is there any craftsman that possesseth full advantages" (maxim 1) [C, A1]. This is the best line for Q1/Q3.
- ***Maat*.** "Great is Truth, appointing a straight path; never hath it been overthrown since the reign of Osiris … the limits of justice are steadfast; it is that which a man repeateth from his father" (maxim 5) [C, A1]. Gunn renders *maat* as "Truth". Lichtheim's "Great is Maat, lasting in effect" is [U] (not opened).
- **How it is done: by hearing, from father to son.** Maxims 38–41 play on one root that Gunn glosses as "_'sôdem_, which in its meaning includes our _hear_ (_listen_) and _obey_" (n. 20). "A son that hearkeneth is as a Follower of Horus … he groweth old … He repeateth in like manner to his sons and daughters, so renewing the instruction of his father" (41) [C, A1]. **Word test:** "obedience" in Gunn is the same word as "hearing", and the page should say so.
- **Q1: none in the text's own words.** No instruction defines its own enterprise beyond "fair speech" and teaching.

**Is it philosophy? (reception only, both sides, no priority).**
- Adamson and Jeffers treat the instructions as philosophy: their episode is titled "Father Knows Best: Moral and Political Philosophy in the Instructions" [C, A6]. The older framing, Frankfort et al., *Before Philosophy* (1946), placed Near Eastern thought before philosophy [U, title only].
- **The reception claims.** G. G. M. James, *Stolen Legacy* (1954), argued that Greek thinkers "stole" Egyptian legacies. Outlaw calls it "A provocative and controversial argument, indeed". Cheikh Anta Diop (*The African Origin of Civilization*, 1974; the Paris thesis earlier) argued that ancient Egypt was a black African civilisation, with "enormous implications for revisions to histories of the origins of Western Philosophy" [C, A5]. Obenga's claim that the Egyptian *rḫ* ("knowing") or *mr-rḫ* is the word or source of "philosophy" is [U], from a search summary only.
- **The critics.** Lefkowitz, *Not Out of Africa* (1996). In Tritle's review, "Aristotle died before the Library at Alexandria was even built", and "As Lefkowitz acutely notes, philosophy and related ideas can not really be 'stolen'" [C, A3]. **Against the critics:** Bernal grants that the Afrocentrists "have been wrong on many particulars" and that Greeks could not have stolen "Aristotelian binary logic and Euclidian geometry". But "The term 'philosophy' is extraordinarily slippery but taken in the Socratic sense of 'wonder, or speculation on truth and reality,' there is every reason to suppose that it was present in Ancient Egypt", and "it was conventional wisdom among Greeks and Romans that philosophy had derived from Egypt" [C, A4].
- **Headline.** The Egypt dispute is a dispute about "philosophy", fought over origins. The page can report it in two sentences as reception and give its answer to the three questions from the text alone. Park's pre-1780 point is context only.

## 3. Ethiopia: the Hatata

**Text and translation.** Sumner's English translation, as scanned on the Internet Archive with Littmann's page and line markers (/p.7/ and so on) [C, B1]. Sumner's complete translation is *Ethiopian Philosophy* vol. II (1976) [C as cited, B3]. The 2023 translation by Lee, Worku and Belcher (De Gruyter) could not be opened (blocked), so its renderings are [U].

- **Q3: who would judge between the faiths?** "Indeed each one says: 'My faith is right, and those who believe in another faith believe in falsehood, and are the enemies of God.' … who would be the judge for such a kind of argument? No single human being [can judge:] for all men are plaintiffs and defendants between themselves … As my faith appears true to me, so does another one find his own faith true; but truth is one" (ch. IV, "The Investigation of Faith and of Prayer", /p.7–8/). The fault of the scholars: "they assume what they have heard from their predecessors and they do not inquire whether it is true or false" (ch. IV) [C, B1].
- **Q1/Q3: the method.** "To the person who seeks it, truth is immediately revealed. Indeed he who investigates with the pure intelligence set by the creator in the heart of each man and scrutinizes the order and laws of creation, will discover the truth" (ch. V, /p.9/). "God has indeed given reason to all and everyone so that they may know truth and falsehood … let us seek it with our reason … We cannot, however, reach truth through the doctrine of men, for all men are liars" (ch. VII, /p.14/) [C, B1].
- **Q2: the point.** "The creator himself wills that we adorn our life with science and work; for such an end did he give us reason and power … God does not create us perfect but creates us with such a reason as to know that we are to strive for perfection as long as we live in this world" (ch. VIII, "The Nature of Knowledge", /p.15–16/) [C, B1].
- **Walda Heywat.** In his colophon to his teacher's book: "I too have written a book in order that all the children of Ethiopia grow in knowledge and be counselled" [C, B1, end of the first treatise]. His own treatise is not in the scan. That it is more practical and pedagogical is [U].
- **Word test.** The text's own words are *ḥatäta*, "inquiry", plus "wisdom", "intelligence" and "reason". Two calls are later readers': Littmann's *Philosophi*, and Sumner's "a real philosopher in the strictest sense of the word" [C as quoted, B3, citing Sumner 1976:61]. The comparison with Descartes is reception [C, B3]; the page does not stage it.
- **Authenticity (headline only).** In 1920 Conti Rossini argued that the texts were written by the Capuchin Giusto d'Urbino, who had sent them to d'Abbadie in 1852–54, and Mittwoch supported him in 1934 [C as cited, B3]. Wion (2013, *Afriques*) revived the thesis. Her abstract asks whether "the two Ḥatatā [might] have been a response to" Giusto's ambition "to realize a work of philosophy", and her series title is "L'histoire d'un vrai faux traité philosophique" [C, B5]. Against her: Sumner's 1976 rebuttal, Getatchew Haile (2017), who was "now firmly inclined to believe that the original Hatata is the work of an Ethiopian debtera", and the 2023 translators, who are "all confident that two Ethiopians named Zara Yaqob and Walda Heywat composed these two texts" [C as quoted, B3]. **This changes the headline only by a clause**: "its authorship was disputed from 1920; most Ge'ez scholars now accept it". The page should not stage it.
- **Date.** The text is from the 1660s. Whether 1667 or 1668 is [U] and does not matter. The scan's notes put Fasiladas' reign at 1632–1667 and his successor's at 1667–1682 [C, B1].

## 4. North Africa and the Sahel

- **Overlap.** Augustine, Clement of Alexandria, Plotinus, Ibn Rushd and Ibn Khaldūn are on other Metaphilosophy pages (§0). The Africa page can say in one sentence that much of what is usually filed as ancient, medieval or Islamic philosophy was written in Africa, and link those pages.
- **Timbuktu bears on Q1 and Q2 through Diagne (2008), a good secondary source and itself a voice in the debate** [C, C1]:
  - Q1: "falsafa, had almost disappeared from the curriculum", but "philosophy is an all-encompassing way of thinking which goes far beyond the disciplinary boundaries": *kalām*, *fiqh*, Sufism, and *manṭiq*, logic, "considered by the peripatetic followers of Aristotle as the Organon, the instrument for all knowledge in general" (p. 24). He cites Diop: "Four centuries before Levy-Bruhl … Black Muslim Africa was commenting on Aristotle's 'formal logic'", and then qualifies the claim (p. 19, 24).
  - On the twentieth-century debate: "Euro-philosophers versus ethno-philosophers – they established the debate on African philosophy in total ignorance of the written tradition of the kind that is revealed by the Timbuktu manuscripts", against the "identification of Africa with orality, a generalisation which is just not accurate" (p. 19, 24).
  - Q2: Ahmad Baba, *Tuhfat al-fuḍalāʾ*. In Diagne's paraphrase, "knowledge is authentic and complete only when it is a way of life, when beyond the mastery of a science there is scrupulous attention to what a good life means, when the accomplished faqih (jurist) is also the fully realised 'arif (sage)". Baba cites the hadith "One hour of a scholar laying on his bed but meditating on his knowledge is more valuable than the worship of a devout person during seventy years" (p. 25, citing Sami and Zniber 1992: 29).
- **Headline.** This stage is useful twice: it answers the "oral, therefore not philosophy" assumption, and it supplies a way-of-life answer to Q2. Ahmad Baba's quoted lines come through Diagne. A page sentence on them must say "as Diagne reads him", or the Arabic or French edition must be checked.

## 5. Oral traditions and sages

Source: Presbey, "African Sage Philosophy", IEP [C, D1].
- **Q3: Oruka's method.** "We are seeking to unsheathe, through constant contacts and discussions with those concerned, the elaborate philosophical views and reasons from the living traditional Kenyan thinkers and sages" (Oruka 1975, as quoted). Academically trained philosophers interview named, non-academic sages and then "engag[e] philosophically with the interview material". Oruka distinguished "folk sages", who recount their community's beliefs, from "philosophic sagacity", sages who take "a critical, evaluative stance toward them", a distinction that copied "'first order' and 'second order' distinctions in philosophy" [C as quoted, D1].
- **Q2.** Oruka meant the project to help Kenyans "evaluate and be able to justify their cultural practices", and philosophy "had to address itself to the concrete problems facing Kenyans and Africans" [C, D1, Presbey's paraphrase].
- **The disputes that shape the headline.** Bodunrin: the sages were not enough like the Greeks, who wrote in a literate context. Ndaba: the ideal of "an individual, explicit, critical and self-critical ratiocinative consciousness" is "a Western notion", and he set against it the Zulu proverb "An eye—when it is one—does not succeed". Oluwole: Oruka defined philosophy so narrowly that his own sages would fail it. Hountondji in 2002 found "his earlier rejection of collective thought was excessive" and said roots should not become a "prison house" [C, D1]. **Headline: sage philosophy turns "what is philosophy?" into "who counts as a philosopher?"**, and the criteria Oruka used are themselves the dispute.
- **Griaule/Ogotemmêli.** Already discarded. Note only: Oruka held that Ogotemmêli was "at best a 'folk sage'", so Griaule's work was "culture philosophy" (*Sage Philosophy*, 1991 ed., 34, 47, 49–50) [C, D1]. Masolo reviewed the doubts about Griaule's method [C, D1].

## 6. The twentieth-century debate (new material only)

- **Kagame.** *La philosophie bantu-rwandaise de l'être* is dated Brussels 1956 in Hountondji's footnote [C, E1]. This closes the earlier [U] on the date.
- **Senghor (the view answered).** The essay is "Ce que l'homme noir apporte" (1939), in *Liberté I* (1964) [C, E3]. Its often-quoted sentence, "L'émotion est nègre, comme la raison hellène", is [U] as to exact wording and page; I found it only in search snippets. Hountondji's book covers négritude [C, E1, editorial intro]; his direct reply to the line is [U]. Use it on the page only as the view others answered, and only once the wording is checked.
- **Wiredu in his own words** ("Toward Decolonizing African Philosophy and Religion", *African Studies Quarterly* 1.4, 1998, 17ff.) [C, E2]. The 1995 book *Conceptual Decolonization in African Philosophy* remains [U].
  - Q1: "philosophy, or at any rate the best kind of philosophy, is a critique, for the most part, of fundamental conceptualization. That is to say, it is the critical examination of the conceptual framework upon which the thought of a culture is erected" (p. 18).
  - Q3: "By decolonization, I mean divesting African philosophical thinking of all undue influences emanating from our colonial past. The crucial word in this formulation is 'undue'" (p. 17). The language point: "a language, most assuredly, is not conceptually neutral; syntax and vocabulary are apt to suggest definite modes of conceptualization. Note, however, that I say 'suggest' not 'compel'" (p. 17). Hence "African philosophers have to be doubly critical", and the work "has of necessity to be comparative" (p. 18). Logic is the example: the Akan reminder "*Nokware mu nni abra*, literally, there is no conflict in truth, which, evidently, is an invocation of the principle of non-contradiction" (p. 21).
  - Q2/the limit: "African philosophers in our time cannot live by decolonization alone but also by the direct interrogation of reality. What is truth, goodness, freedom, time, causality, justice?" (p. 27). And: "It would be a great day for African philosophy when … African insights have become fully integrated into the principal branches of philosophy" (p. 19).
- **Oluwole (reception only).** *Socrates and Ọ̀rúnmìlà: Two Patron Saints of Classical Philosophy*, Ark Publishers, Lagos. The year is 2014 per E4 and 2015 per the brief, so [U] on the year [C on title and publisher, E4]. The reading that Orunmila "predates Socrates by her estimate" [C, E4] is a priority claim. If placed at all, it goes in as her comparison of two oral teachers, with no "before".
- **Amo (one line).** He was born in West Africa (c. 1700), took his doctorate at Wittenberg with *De humanae mentis apatheia* (1734), and wrote *Tractatus de arte sobrie et accurate philosophandi* (Halle, 1738), the "Treatise on the Art of Soberly and Accurately Philosophising" [C, F1, F2]. A secondary summary (after Mabe 2020) says he conceives of philosophy "as the continuous quest for wisdom beyond intellectual dishonesty, dogmatism and prejudice", and that it "proves its worth in action" [C as summarised, F2]. His own Latin definition is [U]. **Bearing:** the title is a direct answer to Q3, but as a German-academy answer. His headline relevance here is to the naming question: Hountondji wrote a study of him, introduced as on a philosopher "who happened (although one would not know it from his writings) to be African" [C, E1, editorial intro], and Oruka's "Four Trends" was first read at a symposium in Amo's name (Accra, 1978) [C, D1]. So he belongs in the intro or the debate stage, not in a stage of his own.

## 7. Draft outline (options, not decisions)

**Intro.** There is no common word; give each stage's own term (*sbꜣyt*, *ḥatäta*, *falsafa*/*manṭiq*). "Philosophy" was Tempels' and Littmann's word, and Hountondji put "African philosophy" in quotation marks. Hegel and Lévy-Bruhl framed the question, and Diagne gave the label a history. The name "Philosophy in Africa" (place) set against Hountondji's "texts written by Africans" (author), with Amo as the case where the two come apart. The usual sentence that the stages ran side by side, and that missing questions are left out.

| Stage | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| The instructions (Egypt) | — (the text calls itself "fair speech") | *maat*, "Great is Truth"; the straight path | hearing, which is also obeying; from father to son; "discourse with the ignorant man, as with the sage" |
| The Hatata (Ethiopia) | inquiry (*ḥatäta*) with "the pure intelligence set by the creator" | "to strive for perfection"; "that all the children of Ethiopia grow in knowledge" | by reason, not "the doctrine of men"; no human judge between faiths |
| The scholars of Timbuktu | philosophy "beyond the disciplinary boundaries": *kalām*, *fiqh*, Sufism, logic | knowledge as a way of life (Ahmad Baba, as Diagne reads him) | logic as "the instrument for all knowledge" |
| The ethnophilosophy debate | a people's collective world-view (Tempels, Kagame), or "a set of texts" (Hountondji) | for Hountondji, liberation into theory | analysis, explicit and argued |
| The sages | philosophic, not folk, sagacity | to evaluate and justify practices | interviewing named sages |
| Decolonisation | "the critical examination of the conceptual framework" | the flourishing of peoples; "the direct interrogation of reality" | "doubly critical"; comparative; testing in one's own language |

Reception (Egypt and the stolen-legacy dispute, Oluwole) goes in one short paragraph, under the Egypt stage or at the end of the intro, framed as a dispute about the word "philosophy", never as priority. Overlap with the ancient, medieval and Islamic pages goes in one sentence with links. Ubuntu gets a See also link only.

## 10. Person-page proposals (for discussion only)

Decided (Jules, 2026-10-19): no new person pages for the metaphilosophy subject; every name below stays an external link or unlinked.

- **Paulin Hountondji.** The importance case: he made the Africa debate a metaphilosophical one, and "ethnophilosophy" is now the field's standard term. He is the principal of the debate stage.
- **Kwasi Wiredu.** The importance case: conceptual decolonisation and the "suggest, not compel" view of language. This is close to the site's own interest in language and concepts, which is an argument the discussion should weigh, not a trigger.
- **Zera Yacob.** Weaker. A single text, an authorship dispute, and most of what matters fits on the stage itself.
- **Oruka** and **Amo**: no case beyond this page.
