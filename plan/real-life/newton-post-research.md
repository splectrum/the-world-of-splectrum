# Newton, a man in his time: research for a conversations post

Purpose: a handful of checked facts and short own-word lines for a ~600-word sketch of Isaac Newton: still full of the old (alchemy, theology, God and final causes inside natural philosophy) while giving birth to the new, and what drove him. A working note, not a site page; nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** checked against a source I opened (key in brackets, list at the end). **[C as quoted]** wording checked, but in a secondary source that quotes it. **[U]** unverified. Spelling of manuscripts is the Newton Project's normalized text unless marked diplomatic.

## 1. The life in brief

- Born at Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, "25 Dec./4 Jan." 1642/3: Christmas Day 1642 by the Julian calendar, 4 January 1643 by the Gregorian [C, NP-G]. SEP: "25 December 1642 (old calendar)"; father "died two months before Isaac was born"; Puritan family [C, SEP].
- Trinity College, Cambridge, 5/15 June 1661, as a subsizar [C, NP-G].
- Plague: at Woolsthorpe from summer 1665 until March 1667, apart from a short stay in Cambridge in spring 1666 [C, NP-G]; SEP: "all but three months from the summer of 1665 until the spring of 1667", his "so-called *annus mirabilis*" [C, SEP].
- **His own account, written about fifty years later during the Leibniz dispute** (draft, MS Add. 3968, folio marked 85r; Newton Project diplomatic text): series and the binomial rule "In the beginning of the year 1665"; "the direct method of fluxions" in November; "the next year in Ianuary had the Theory of colours & in May following I had entrance into ye inverse method of fluxions. And the same year I began to think of gravity extending to ye orb of the Moon", and found the forces "answer pretty nearly". Then: "All this was in the two plague years of 1665 & 1666. For in those days I was in the prime of my age for invention & minded Mathematicks & Philosophy more then at any time since" [C, NP-M; deletions dropped, "Philos" struck through in the MS]. **Caution:** a priority claim written c. 1716–20; historians weigh it as such [U for the scholarly weighing; the context is C].
- Lucasian Professor 19/29 October 1669, succeeding Barrow [C, NP-G].
- Halley's visit, summer (August) 1684: asked the curve under an inverse-square force, "His immediate answer was, an ellipse"; *De motu* sent in November [C, SEP; NP-G]. *Principia* July 1687, "largely at Halley's urging and entirely at Halley's expense" [C, NP-G].
- Warden of the Mint 1696, Master from 1699; President of the Royal Society 1703, re-elected annually until his death; knighted 1705 [C, SEP; NP-G]. (The Abbey page says President "from 1705 to 1727": an error against SEP and NP-G.)
- Died at Kensington 20 March 1727 (Old Style; 31 March New Style), "having refused the last rites" [C, NP-G; W]. Buried in Westminster Abbey on 28 March, in the nave; the gravestone reads "Hic depositum est, quod mortale fuit Isaaci Newtoni" [C, W].

## 2. The alchemy

- **How long, how much:** "a realm of activity that spanned some thirty years of his life"; "Newton wrote and transcribed about a million words on the subject of alchemy" [C, CIN-home]. Newman's book traces it "over a span of more than three decades" [C, PUP]. SEP: over sixty years "he put no less effort into chemical and alchemical research and into theology and biblical studies than he put into mathematics and physics" [C, SEP]. Keynes, more modestly: he had "glanced through ... at least 100,000 words" of it [C, K].
- **When it starts:** apparatus and alchemical treatises bought in 1669 [C, SEP]; two furnaces in a shed at Trinity and Zetzner's *Theatrum chemicum* in 1669 [C, NP-G].
- **His word:** "chymistry", the seventeenth-century term the project adopts for alchemy-and-chemistry together [C, CIN-about]. Word test: Newton's own papers use "Chymistry" (e.g. "vulgar Chymistry", below) [C, D].
- **Own words, the tract "Of Natures obvious laws & processes in vegetation"** (Dibner MS 1031 B, 1670–75 [C, NP-cat]): "Natures actions are either vegetable or purely mechanicall (grav. flux. meteors. vulgar Chymistry). The principles of her vegetable actions are noe other then the seeds or seminall vessels of things those are her onely agents, her fire, her soule, her life" [C, D]. The project calls it his attempt at "essentially, a 'theory of everything'" [C, D]. NP-G links it to his 1675 "Hypothesis" on light sent to the Royal Society [C, NP-G].
- **Own words, the secrecy** (to Oldenburg, 26 April 1676, on Boyle's mercury): the method "may possibly be an in let to something more noble, not to be communicated without immense dammage to the world if there should be any verity in the Hermetick writers"; "pray keep this letter private to your self" [C, NP-O].
- **Index chemicus:** Keynes MS 30, 1680s, c. 30,000 + 4,000 words [C, NP-cat]. Not opened as text.
- **What he sought, as witnesses and sources state it:** Humphrey Newton, his amanuensis 1684–89, to Conduitt, 1727/8: "About 6 weeks at Spring & 6 at the ffall the fire in the Elaboratory scarcely went out"; "The transmuting of Metals, being his Chief Design, for which Purpose Antimony was a great Ingredient"; "What his Aim might be, I was not able to penetrate into but his Paine, his Diligence at those sett times, made me think, he aim'd at som thing beyond the Reach of humane Art & Industry" [C, HN]. Newman (publisher's summary): alchemy as "a tool for attaining the material benefits associated with the philosopher's stone and an instrument for acquiring scientific knowledge" [C, PUP].
- **The sale:** Sotheby's, 13–14 July 1936; just over £9,000 for the lot [C, NP-S]; 329 lots, "over a third" alchemical; labelled "not fit to be printed" at his death [C, CIN-about]. Keynes, alerted late by his brother, bought from dealers afterwards, competed with Abraham Yahuda (who gathered the theology), and concentrated on the alchemical papers; bequeathed to King's College, Cambridge, 1946 [C, NP-S]. Keynes: "I managed gradually to reassemble about half of them, including nearly the whole of the biographical portion" [C, K].

## 3. The theology (one line)

- SEP: "Newton the anti-Trinitarian mild heretic who was not that much more radical ... than many others at the time", beside "the wild religious zealot predicting the end of the Earth" [C, SEP]. The Lucasian vows "appear to have precipitated his study of the doctrine of the Trinity" [C, SEP].
- 1690: two long letters to Locke on "corruptions of Scripture", "explicitly stating his own anti-trinitarian convictions"; and his view that his discoveries were rediscoveries of "prisca sapientia" known to ancient sages [C, NP-G].
- Keynes: "rather a Judaic monotheist of the school of Maimonides"; "a dreadful secret which Newton was at desperate pains to conceal all his life" [C, K].
- Own words (untitled articles, as Brewster prints them): "There is one God the Father, ever living, omnipresent, omniscient, almighty, the maker of heaven and earth, and one Mediator between God and man, the man Christ Jesus" [C as quoted, Br2, p. 349; manuscript not identified, U].

## 4. Keynes, "Newton, the Man"

- Written for the 1942 tercentenary, postponed by the war; Keynes died April 1946; read by his brother Geoffrey at Trinity in July 1946 [C, K]. Print: "Read by Mr. Geoffrey Keynes at the Newton Tercentenary Celebrations at Trinity College, Cambridge, on [17] July 1946, and therefore not revised by the author" (*Essays in Biography*, new ed. 1951, p. 310; the day is OCR "ij", read as 17) [C, EIB; day U]. First printed in the Royal Society's *Newton Tercentenary Celebrations* (1947) [U].
- **The passage, as printed** (EIB pp. 310–311, matching K): "In the eighteenth century and since, Newton came to be thought of as the first and greatest of the modern age of scientists, a rationalist, one who taught us to think on the lines of cold and untinctured reason. I do not see him in this light. ... Newton was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians, the last of the Babylonians and Sumerians, the last great mind which looked out on the visible and intellectual world with the same eyes as those who began to build our intellectual inheritance rather less than 10,000 years ago. Isaac Newton, a posthumous child born with no father on Christmas Day, 1642, was the last wonder-child to whom the Magi could do sincere and appropriate homage." [C, EIB, K]. ("wonder-child" breaks across a line in the print; K has "wonderchild".)
- Two more Keynes lines that suit the post: "with one foot in the Middle Ages and one foot treading a path for modern science"; and the close, "Copernicus and Faustus in one" [C, EIB, K]. Why "magician": "he looked on the whole universe and all that is in it as a riddle, as a secret which could be read by applying pure thought to certain evidence, certain mystic clues which God had laid about the world" [C, K].
- **Reception:** Keynes's is a position, not a finding: he judged the alchemy "wholly magical and wholly devoid of scientific value" [C, K]; Newman's book is presented against both pictures: "neither Enlightenment rationalist nor irrational magus" [C, PUP].

## 5. What drove him

- **"Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas"** (Plato is a friend, Aristotle is a friend, truth is a greater friend): heading of his student notebook *Quaestiones quaedam Philosophiae*, MS Add. 3996, f. 88r, early–mid 1660s [C, NP-Q]. An adapted scholastic tag, not his coinage [U for the tag's history].
- **"industry & a patient thought"**: "if I have done the publick any service this way 'tis due to nothing but industry & a patient thought" (to Bentley, 10 Dec. 1692) [C, NP-B]. Genuine and dated: the safest line on method.
- **"By always thinking unto them"**: reply to someone who asked how he made his discoveries; and "I keep the subject constantly before me, and wait till the first dawnings open slowly by little and little into a full and clear light" [C as quoted, Biot 1833 tr.]. Reported anecdotes, no source given for either in Biot [U for origin]. Keynes's reading fits: "his peculiar gift was the power of holding continuously in his mind a purely mental problem until he had seen straight through it" [C, K].
- **"If I have seen farther, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants"**: to Hooke, Cambridge, 5 Feb. 1675/6, after "What Descartes did was a good step. You have added much several ways" [C as quoted, Br1 ch. VI]. The original is at the Historical Society of Pennsylvania (blocked, 403). Spelling "further"/"sholders" in the MS [U]. An old commonplace (Bernard of Chartres via John of Salisbury) [U]. The reading as a jibe at Hooke's stature is reception and disputed [U].
- **The seashore**: "I do not know what I may appear to the world, but to myself I seem to have been only like a boy playing on the sea-shore ... whilst the great ocean of truth lay all undiscovered before me" [C as quoted, Br2 ch. XXVII]; Biot gives another wording and sources it to "a manuscript of Conduit" via Turner (1806) [C, Biot]. **Late, second-hand; wording varies.**
- **The apple**: Stukeley, revised memoir (Royal Society MS/142, c. 1752): on 15 April 1726, at Kensington, under apple trees, Newton "told me, he was just in the same situation, as when formerly, the notion of gravitation came into his mind ... occasion'd by the fall of an apple, as he sat in a comtemplative mood"; "why should it not go sideways, or upwards? but constantly to the earths centre?" [C, ST]. NP-G: "or so Newton himself is said to have claimed decades later" [C]. **Newton's own story, told sixty years on**; the head-strike is later embroidery [U].
- **Active principles, where old and new meet** (*Opticks*, Q31): the *vis inertiae* is "a passive Principle"; "By this Principle alone there never could have been any Motion in the World"; motion is conserved "by active Principles, such as are the cause of Gravity ... and the cause of Fermentation, by which the Heart and Blood of Animals are kept in perpetual Motion and Heat"; these are "not ... occult Qualities ... but ... general Laws of Nature ... their Truth appearing to us by Phænomena, though their Causes be not yet discover'd" [C, OPT]. And Q30: "The changing of Bodies into Light, and Light into Bodies, is very conformable to the Course of Nature, which seems delighted with Transmutations" [C, OPT].
- **Historians, as positions:** Dobbs (*Janus Faces of Genius*, 1991, in the National Humanities Center's summary): his "primary goal was not the study of nature for its own sake but rather an attempt to establish a unified system that would have included both natural and divine principles", tracing the "vegetable spirit" of alchemy and the "cause" of gravitation [C as summarised, NHC]. Newman (*Newton the Alchemist*, 2018, publisher's summary): he "does not justify Newton's alchemical research as part of a religious search for God ... nor does he argue that Newton studied alchemy to learn about gravitational attraction"; Newton "used ideas drawn from the alchemical texts to great effect in his optical experimentation" [C, PUP]. The Chymistry project: "we cannot erect a watertight dam separating them from his other scientific endeavors" [C, CIN-about].

3. About a million words on alchemy over some thirty years (CIN); "no less effort" than on mathematics and physics (SEP).

8. "Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas" (student notebook).
9. Anti-Trinitarian, kept secret; refused the last rites (NP-G; SEP; Keynes).

## Avoid or flag

- The apple: Newton's own late story via Stukeley (1726, written c. 1752); fine as "he later told", never as fact of 1666. No head-strike.
- "Shoulders of giants": genuine letter, borrowed commonplace; skip the Hooke's-height reading.
- Seashore and "thinking unto them": posthumous reports; attribute ("he is reported to have said").
- Keynes's Halley exchange ("Why, I've known it for years") and "profoundly neurotic": Keynes's telling, unsourced in the lecture.
- Pope's epitaph: never on the monument (W); a quotation, not a fact about Newton.
- "Seeds or seminall vessels" in the vegetation tract: the word "seed" is reserved in SPLectrum voice; fine inside a quotation, but check before using it in a post.
