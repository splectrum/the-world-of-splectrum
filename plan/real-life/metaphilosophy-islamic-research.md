## 1. al-Fārābī (d. 950)

**Q1, the map.**
- The *Enumeration of the Sciences* (*Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm*) gives "the traditional order of the Aristotelian sciences": language (grammar, metrics), logic, mathematics (arithmetic, geometry, optics, astronomy, music, weights, mechanics), natural philosophy, metaphysics, and political science, which "also includes kalām, i.e., non-philosophical theology, and fiqh or Islamic law" [C, S1]. "Al-Fārābī understood philosophy as all-encompassing" [C, S1]. No full English translation exists; Butterworth translated part V [C, S1].
- **Word test:** "non-philosophical theology" is the SEP author's gloss, not al-Fārābī's. His own definition of kalām in the *Enumeration*, as a faculty for making the founder's opinions and actions prevail and refuting what contradicts them, is [U, search summary only].
- In the *Attainment of Happiness* (Mahdi tr.) §53, the Greeks called this science "unqualified wisdom" and "highest wisdom", and "the scientific state of mind 'philosophy' (by which they meant the quest and the love for the highest wisdom)"; also "science of sciences", "mother of sciences", "wisdom of wisdoms" and "art of arts" [C, S2]. The same section gives a lineage: "It is said that this science existed anciently among the Chaldeans", then Egypt, the Greeks, the Syrians "and then to the Arabs", "expounded in the Greek language, later in Syriac, and finally in Arabic" [C, S2]. This is al-Fārābī's reported story, not a priority claim to place.

**Q2, the point.**
- §1: "The human things by which nations and citizens of cities attain earthly happiness in this life and supreme happiness in the life beyond are of four kinds: theoretical virtues, deliberative virtues, moral virtues, and practical arts" [C, S2]. In SEP's summary, happiness in the strict sense is "ultimate felicity", "a state associated with the afterlife", unlike Aristotle's *eudaimonia* [C, S3].
- §54: theoretical science alone is "defective philosophy": "To be a truly perfect philosopher, one has to possess both the theoretical sciences and the faculty for exploiting them for the benefit of all others according to their capacity" [C, S2].

**Q2/Q3, religion as imitation.**
- §55: one understands a thing either by its essence "perceived by the intellect" or "by causing it to be imagined through the similitude that imitates it"; assent comes either by "certain demonstration" or "persuasion". Intellect plus demonstration is philosophy; similitudes plus persuasion is what "the ancients call … 'religion'". "Therefore, according to the ancients, religion is an imitation of philosophy." Both "give an account of the ultimate end for the sake of which man is made—that is, supreme happiness". "Finally, philosophy is prior to religion in time" [C, S2].
- **Word test:** al-Fārābī puts the formula in the ancients' mouth ("according to the ancients"). "Imitation" and "similitude" are Mahdi's; Germann (SEP) renders the parallel passage of the *Perfect State* as "affinity and symbolic representation" [C, S3]. The Arabic term (*muḥākāt*) is [U].
- §59: the same things "are philosophy when they are in the soul of the legislator. They are religion when they are in the souls of the multitude" [C, S2].
- SEP: philosophy is superior "only" in epistemic level; pragmatically religion is "no less important", because most people depend on symbolic representation [C, S3]. That is the headline nuance: not religion demoted, but one truth taught two ways.

**The philosopher-ruler.**
- §58: "the idea of the Philosopher, Supreme Ruler, Prince, Legislator, and Imam is but a single idea" [C, S2]. §54: "the true philosopher is himself the supreme ruler", because bringing the theoretical into existence in cities needs "demonstrations, persuasive methods, as well as methods that represent things through images" [C, S2].
- **Word test:** "philosopher-king" is Plato's; al-Fārābī's own list is the five names. SEP's "philosopher prophet ruler" is its gloss [C, S1]. His political works draw on Plato's *Republic* and *Laws*, not Aristotle's *Politics*, which seems not to have reached Arabic [C, S1].
- §§60–61: "mutilated philosophy": the false, the vain and the counterfeit philosopher [C, S2]. A Q1 boundary: who counts as a philosopher.

**Q3, method.**
- Three kinds of discourse: demonstrative (the *Posterior Analytics*, "which alone is philosophical *stricto sensu*"), dialectical ("typical of the 'mutakallimūn' or theologians", the *Topics*), and rhetorical and poetical (used in the Qur'an and the Jewish and Christian scriptures "to address ordinary people") [C, S1]. Respect for demonstration led him "to attempt to fit any theoretical discipline in its framework" [C, S1].

**"Second Teacher".** SEP's logic entry: "the honorific bestowed upon him by Avicenna: the Second Teacher (after Aristotle)" [C, S4 as stated]. That Avicenna is the source of the title is doubtful to me [U]; the safe wording is "known as the Second Teacher, after Aristotle".

**Edition.** The text I used is Mahdi's translation as reprinted in a sourcebook (pp. 56–71, §§16–64, §1 given in note 6), with the Hyderabad page numbers in brackets. The excerpt's headnote names the full version: *Alfarabi: Philosophy of Plato and Aristotle*, ed. and tr. Muhsin Mahdi (Free Press 1962; rev. Cornell 1969, 2002) [C, S2]. Which sourcebook edition it is is [U] (probably *Medieval Political Philosophy: A Sourcebook*, 2nd ed., 2011).

## 2. Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna, d. 1037)

**Q1, the division.**
- The curriculum he describes in the *Autobiography* is the late-antique one: logic "as the instrument of philosophy (the Organon)", the theoretical sciences (physics, mathematics, metaphysics) and the practical (ethics, household management, politics) [C, S5].
- *The Cure* (*al-Shifāʾ*) covers "all the parts of philosophy in the Aristotelian tradition": logic in nine books, from the *Eisagoge* to the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics*; physics; mathematics; metaphysics (*Ilāhiyyāt*); practical philosophy [C, S5].
- **The headline addition:** to make the system "account for all reality", he brought "all manifestations of religious life and paranormal events" inside philosophy. Prophecy, dreams and the afterlife went to a "metaphysics of the rational soul"; the social side became a fourth practical science, "the discipline of legislating" (*al-ṣināʿa al-shāriʿa*) [C, S5]. Here religion is a subject within philosophy, where al-Fārābī made it philosophy's image.

**Q2, the point.**
- *Shifāʾ*, *Metaphysics* IX.7, in SEP's summary: "real happiness is the perfection of the rational soul through knowledge" [C, S5]. Gutas: "it is our cosmic duty to enable our intellects to reach their full potential and behave like the celestial ones, that is, think the intelligibles" [C, S5].
- The definition in *ʿUyūn al-ḥikma*, as Ḥājjī Khalīfa reports it: the Shaykh "explained *ḥikma* as the perfection of the human soul (*istikmāl al-nafs al-insāniyya*) through conceptions and assents, whether in theoretical or in practical things" (my rendering) [C, S6, *Kashf al-ẓunūn* I, 677]. The text of the *ʿUyūn* itself is [U] (archive.org copy returned a server error).

**Q3, method.**
- Philosophy is progressive and correctable: Aristotelianism is "not perfect, and it is the task of philosophers to correct and amplify it"; "it behooves his [Aristotle's] successors to gather the loose ends he left, repair any breach they find in what he constructed" (*Eastern Philosophy*, tr. Gutas) [C, S5].
- Even prophetic knowledge is syllogistic: it is "not an uncritical reception … merely on authority, but rather occurs in an order which includes the middle terms" (*Shifāʾ*, *De anima*, tr. Gutas) [C, S5].
- Five kinds of syllogistic by the assent they produce: demonstration, sophistry, dialectic (*jadal*), rhetoric and poetry, in the *Ishārāt* as given in Ṭūsī's commentary [C, S4].

**"Oriental philosophy".** Gutas (SEP) reads *al-Mashriqiyyūn* / *al-Ḥikma al-mashriqiyya* as *Eastern* Philosophy, named "to reflect his own locality in the East", a summa on disputed points presented as "true philosophy" [C, S5]. A mystical "Oriental wisdom" reading exists (Corbin, Nasr; one search summary has it turn "discursive" wisdom into "gustatory" wisdom) [U]. Headline value: the disputed reading decides whether Avicenna's late work changes the method. One clause at most.

**Flying man.** Not reached; it bears on the soul's self-awareness, not on what philosophy is. Discard.

## 3. The words

- **falsafa → ḥikma.** Griffel (2021), as quoted in a review: after al-Ghazālī, philosophers in the Greek line "avoided the word falsafa and replaced it with another word that assumed largely the same meaning", *ḥikma*, and a new type of philosopher, the *mutakallim*, "engaged in philosophy just as much as the falāsifa … but … consciously rejected the attribute of being a faylasūf" [C as quoted, S7]. The reviewer: *ḥikma* "had sacred legitimacy" (Qur'an: "Whoever is granted ḥikma has been granted much good"), and books of *ḥikma* improve the Avicennan system "from within", whatever revelation says [C, S7]. The same author could write *ḥikma* and *kalām* books with opposite positions (Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī on the world's eternity) [C, S7].
- **Later usage confirms it.** Ḥājjī Khalīfa (17th c.) files the whole subject under "the science of *ḥikma*", and sets "the *ḥikma* of illumination" among the philosophical sciences as Sufism stands among the Islamic ones, natural and divine *ḥikma* standing as *kalām* does [C, S6].
- **Suhrawardī (d. 1191).** *Philosophy of Illumination* §§2–4: what he "obtained through my intuition during my retreats and visions … I did not first arrive at it through cogitation … Subsequently I sought proof for it"; "the very intuition of the inspired and illumined Plato" [C, S8]. His project "sought to revive the wisdom (hikma) of the Ancients", Greek and Persian [C, S8]. **Q3 headline:** intuition first, proof after.
- **Mullā Ṣadrā (d. 1640).** *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fi-l-asfār al-ʿaqliyya al-arbaʿa*, "Transcendent wisdom of the four journeys of the intellect" [C, S9]. "Metaphysical doctrines can only be grasped by inner revelation (*mukāshafāt bāṭiniyya*) … and cannot be really known through rehearsing discursive doctrines" (*Asfār* IX, 146), yet "demonstration does not conflict with inner disclosure" [C, S9]. He urges philosophy as practice: "the study of wisdom requires spiritual exercise and forsaking the world" (a saying of ʿAlī) [C, S9]. SEP's author (Rizvi) cites Hadot and sums up: "Philosophy is thus a practice and a way of life" [C, S9]. **Headline:** the late Islamic stage answers Q2 and Q3 in the way-of-life terms the Greek and Latin page uses for antiquity, in a modern scholar's framing.
- **Word test:** "transcendent" (*mutaʿāliya*) is the common rendering; "theosophy" and "philosophy" are both used for *ḥikma* in translation [U for the range]. The author's own word is *ḥikma*, and its practitioner is a *ḥakīm*, with *ḥakīm mutaʾallih* glossed "divine philosopher" [C, S9].
- **kalām.** "kalām (literally 'speech') … books of inter-sectarian disputation over questions of metaphysics, that is, a form of rationalistic theology" (reviewer, after Griffel) [C, S7]. In the falāsifa's scheme its method is dialectic [C, S1].
- **"Arabic" vs "Islamic" philosophy.** Adamson: "Islamic philosophy" fails because "some of the philosophers that we're interested in weren't Muslims" (Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī, Maimonides) and it presupposes religious topics; "Arabic philosophy" fails because some of the work is in Persian and Hebrew, and late figures like Mullā Ṣadrā are far from the translation movement. He prefers "philosophy in the Islamic world", "a neutral designation", meaning regions "under the political domination of Islam" [C, S10]. He co-edited the *Cambridge Companion to Arabic Philosophy* "before I changed my mind" [C, S10]. Gutas's preference for "Arabic philosophy" and his critique of the Corbin–Nasr line are [U] (BJMES 2002 not opened).
- **The decline narrative.** In the review's summary, Hegel said Arabic philosophy "has no content of any interest" and Renan blamed "Islamic orthodoxy"; the decline-after-Ghazālī thesis then spread as far as Weinberg [C as reported, S7]. Griffel offers "a resounding refutation" (reviewer) [C, S7]. Bertolacci (SEP): "if it is right to call 'golden age' of Arabic philosophy the period 1100–1350", the Safavid era is the "platinum" age of Avicenna's reception [C, S11]. Gutas's "golden age … 1000–1350" title (2002) is [U, bibliographic only]. The reviewer says Griffel's end-point, the arrival of colonial institutions, lacks "specificity" [C, S7]. **Headline:** the "decline" is itself a nineteenth-century European reading, and the post-Ghazālī record now reads as a change of name (*ḥikma*) and genre.

## 4. Method: burhān, jadal, rhetoric; kalām

- The Arabic Organon included the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics* as parts of logic (Ibn Sīnā's nine logic books; al-Fārābī's eight parts) [C, S5, S12].
- "There never was a trivium in the Arabic-speaking philosophical world"; "the three arts" (*al-ṣināʿāt al-thalāth*) were "demonstration, dialectic and rhetoric" [C, S4].
- The sort was social as well as logical: demonstration for philosophers, dialectic for theologians, rhetoric and poetry for the many (al-Fārābī) [C, S1].
- **New on al-Ghazālī.** He turned the falāsifa's own standard against them: none of their twenty arguments meets "the high epistemological standard of demonstration (*burhān*)"; they rest "upon unproven premises that are accepted only among the falāsifa", "mere dialectical arguments", and so are *taqlīd*, repetition of the founders [C, S13]. **Headline:** the dispute over philosophy in Islam is fought on the falāsifa's method. The challenge is whether their arguments are demonstration at all, not reason against faith.
- Later, the logic handbook changed shape: legal dialectic replaced the *Topics*, and disciplines from grammar and theology replaced rhetoric and poetics [C, S4]. That is a detail; it can be dropped.

## 5. The interlock with the Latin West (as it bears on Q1–Q3)

- **Q1.** Toledo, second half of the twelfth century: Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus. al-Fārābī's *Enumeration*, translated by both, likely "served as a model for a coherent translation program" [C, S12]. Gundissalinus's *De divisione philosophiae* carried it into the thirteenth-century arts literature, which sometimes calls Gundissalinus "Alpharabius"; with it came Avicenna's principles for dividing the sciences [C, S12]. Gundissalinus took over al-Fārābī's eight-part logic with *Rhetoric* and *Poetics* inside it, and Arnoul de Provence objected that neither Aristotle nor common usage counts them [C, S12].
- **Q3.** "Al-Fārābī's emphasis on demonstration as the pivotal means for the acquisition of certain knowledge is an important innovation of Arabic philosophy, which reached the Latin West via Gundisalvi" [C, S12].
- **Q2.** al-Fārābī, Avicenna and Averroes share the view that happiness is reached through the intellect's conjunction with the active intellect, possible in this life for philosophers [C, S12]. Albert took it up; Aquinas rejected perfect happiness in this life (*ST* I-II q.3 a.2) [C, S12]. In 1277 Paris condemned "that there is no better state (of life) than studying philosophy" (art. 40) and that happiness is had in this life (art. 176), aimed at masters such as Siger and Boethius of Dacia [C, S12].
- **Averroes as "the Commentator".** Latin translations began with Michael Scot around 1220; "already by the 1230s he is known as 'the Commentator'"; "Averroist" was pejorative by the 1260s [C, S14]. Gundissalinus translated Avicenna's *De anima* with Avendauth [C, S12].
- **Headline:** the Latin "servant" stage got its map of philosophy, its stress on demonstration and its most disputed answer on philosophy's point from the Arabic texts. The 1277 article 40 is the Latin stage's clearest Q2 conflict.

## 6. Maimonides and Ibn Khaldūn (one line each, for the decision)

- **Maimonides (d. 1204).** Bears on Q1 and Q2. The *Guide* (1190) is "ostensibly a letter written to an advanced student who cannot decide whether to follow philosophy or the teachings of his religion"; the highest perfection is intellectual, and the Parable of the Palace (*Guide* III.51) puts those who "have proved all that can be proved" nearest the ruler [C, S15]. He is the Jewish counterpart of the Ibn Rushd answer, inside the Islamic world on Adamson's definition [C, S10].
- **Ibn Khaldūn (d. 1406).** Bears on Q1 and Q3 as a dissent. *Muqaddimah* ch. VI §30, "A refutation of philosophy. The corruption of the students of philosophy" (Rosenthal tr., III, 246 ff.): philosophers are those who think everything, articles of faith included, can be known "through (intellectual) speculation and not through tradition"; logic "is not adequate to achieve the avowed intentions" of the philosophers, but "has only a single fruit, namely, it sharpens the mind", and should be studied only after the religious sciences [C, S16]. A clean counter-answer for the page, if wanted.

## Draft outline of the page (options, not decisions)

Opening: *falsafa* as loanword, the love of wisdom (*ḥikma*), and the naming question in two sentences (Adamson's three labels). Stages, each with the three questions where the sources speak:

1. **The reception (al-Kindī, 9th c.).** Q1: the inherited definitions. Q2: imitating God, care for death. Q3: truth "from wherever it comes". (Material from the earlier round.)
2. **The philosopher-legislator (al-Fārābī, 10th c.).** Q1: the *Enumeration*'s map, with kalām and law inside political science; "science of sciences". Q2: supreme happiness; "religion is an imitation of philosophy", "according to the ancients"; Philosopher, Prince, Legislator, Imam "a single idea". Q3: demonstration over dialectic and rhetoric.
3. **The system (Ibn Sīnā, 11th c.).** Q1: the *Shifāʾ* division, with prophecy and legislation brought inside. Q2: the perfection of the rational soul. Q3: correct and amplify Aristotle; syllogism, even for the prophet.
4. **The challenge and the defence (al-Ghazālī, Ibn Rushd, 11th–12th c.).** Q1: falsafa against kalām. Q2: the Law commands philosophy. Q3: is it demonstration at all? (*taqlīd*); the three classes of people.
5. **Wisdom (*ḥikma*) after al-Ghazālī (12th–17th c.).** Q1: the change of name. Q3: Suhrawardī's intuition, then proof; Mullā Ṣadrā, demonstration with disclosure. Q2: spiritual exercise, philosophy as a way of life (Rizvi after Hadot). Possibly Ibn Khaldūn as the dissenting line at the end.

Then: a short paragraph on the interlock (Toledo, Gundissalinus, the Commentator, 1277 art. 40), and a closing note on the decline narrative and its revision. Table "In one view" as on the Greek and Latin page.
