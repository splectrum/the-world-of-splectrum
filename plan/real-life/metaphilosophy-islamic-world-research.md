# Metaphilosophy, Philosophy in the Islamic World: research note

Purpose: to source the section "Philosophy in the Islamic world". It is a landing page and eight pages, and it replaces the present single page `docs/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/islamic-world.md`. For each page the note gathers the tradition's own answers to the section's three questions: what the activity is (*falsafa*, *ḥikma*, *kalām*, *ʿilm*), what its point is, and how it is done. Modern scholars (Adamson, Griffel, Gutas, Hasse and others) are used for facts only: dates, which text says what, and translations. Their debates about the tradition are gathered in §11, so they can move to a Western page. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** means checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** means the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** means unverified. "My rendering" means my own English from the Arabic text I opened. The Arabic is usually OCR from archive.org: legible for the passages cited, but not a critical edition. Where I name an edition I could not identify, it is marked [U].

**Access.** These were reachable: SEP, IEP, archive.org (full texts of Lerner–Mahdi 1963, Ivry 1974, Gohlman 1974, Gutas 2013, Elder, Dodge 1970, Watt, Masʿad–Cragg 1966, Iqbal 1944, JRAS 1905), the old muslimphilosophy.com e-texts (Rosenthal's *Muqaddimah*, Hourani's *Decisive Treatise*, van den Bergh's *Tahāfut al-tahāfut*), ghazali.org, en/fr/ar Wikisource, and the alquran.cloud API. These were blocked or restricted: sacred-texts.com (Cloudflare 5xx); hindawi.org, al-islam.org, iqbalcyberlibrary.net, gallica.bnf.fr and babel.hathitrust.org (403); library.oapen.org and art.torvergata.it (bot wall); archive.org lending-only items (Wickens's *Nasirean Ethics*, Khalidi 2005, Nasr 2006, al-Jābirī's *Formation of Arab Reason*). See §18.

## 2. Landing page: the words and the tradition's own sortings

### The words

- **falsafa, faylasūf.**
  - al-Khwārizmī, *Mafātīḥ al-ʿulūm*, II, ch. 1 §1: *al-falsafa mushtaqqa min kalima yūnāniyya wa-hiya fīlāsūfiyā wa-tafsīruhā maḥabbat al-ḥikma… wa-maʿnā al-falsafa: ʿilm ḥaqāʾiq al-ashyāʾ wa-l-ʿamal bi-mā huwa aṣlaḥ*. My rendering: "*Falsafa* is derived from a Greek word, *fīlāsūfiyā*, which means love of wisdom; when it was Arabised one said *faylasūf*, and *falsafa* was then derived from it. The meaning of *falsafa* is knowledge of the realities of things and acting by what is best" [C, S3].
  - Ibn Khaldūn: those who think the whole of existence, articles of faith included, can be known "by mental speculation and intellectual reasoning… are called 'philosophers' falasifah, plural of faylasuf – which is Greek and means 'lover of wisdom'" (Rosenthal) [C, S1, VI.30].
  - al-Kindī's gloss *ḥubb al-ḥikma* [C as quoted, earlier round].
  - **Word test:** all three treat *falsafa* as a foreign word and gloss it through the native *ḥikma*.
- **ḥikma.**
  - Qurʾān 2:269: *wa-man yuʾta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya khayran kathīran*, "whoever has been given wisdom has certainly been given much good" (Saheeh International) [C, S40].
  - Ibn Sīnā, *Aqsām al-ʿulūm al-ʿaqliyya*: *al-ḥikma ṣināʿa naẓar yastafīdu minhā al-insān taḥṣīl mā ʿalayhi al-wujūd kulluhu fī nafsihi wa-mā al-wājib ʿalayhi ʿamaluhu… li-tashrufa bi-dhālika nafsuhu wa-tastakmila wa-taṣīra ʿāliman maʿqūlan muḍāhiyan li-l-ʿālam al-mawjūd, wa-tastaʿidda li-l-saʿāda al-quṣwā bi-l-ākhira, wa-dhālika bi-ḥasab al-ṭāqa al-insāniyya*. My rendering: "Wisdom is an art of theory by which a person acquires the attainment of how existence as a whole is in itself, and of what he must do… so that his soul may be ennobled and perfected and become an intelligible world corresponding to the existing world, and be prepared for ultimate happiness in the hereafter, according to human capacity." [C, S5a, quoted in ʿUthmān Amīn's footnote to the *Iḥṣāʾ*.] The Cairo 1908 *Tisʿ rasāʾil* text itself was not opened [U for the page]. Mahdi's translation of the *Divisions* (S6) gives only the following sections, not this definition.
  - **Note:** the "rational world resembling the existing world" that the brief assigns to Mullā Ṣadrā is first Ibn Sīnā's. Ṣadrā repeats it (§9).
  - Ibn Khaldūn: the intellectual sciences "are called the sciences of philosophy and wisdom" [C, S1, VI.18]; the Arabic wording is [U].
- **ʿilm, dhawq, īmān.** al-Ghazālī, *Munqidh*: "Certainty reached by demonstration is knowledge (`ilm); actual acquaintance with that `state' is immediate experience (dhawq); the acceptance of it as probable from hearsay and trial… is faith (iman). These are three degrees" (tr. Watt) [C, S15]. This is a native three-way sorting of kinds of knowing, usable on the landing page.
- **ʿaql / naql.** Ibn Khaldūn's two kinds of science: "one that is natural to man and to which he is guided by his own ability to think, and a traditional kind that he learns from those who invented it". In the traditional kind "There is no place for the intellect… save that the intellect may be used… to relate problems of detail with basic principles" (Rosenthal) [C, S1, VI.9]. The Arabic pair (*ʿaqliyya/ḥikmiyya* against *naqliyya/waḍʿiyya*) is [U, Arabic not opened]. A definition of *ʿaql* itself from al-Kindī's *On Definitions* or Ibn Sīnā's *Ḥudūd* was not reached (open check).
- **kalām.** Its definitions (al-Fārābī, Ibn Khaldūn, al-Ījī, al-Taftāzānī) are in §4. The tradition's own explanation of the name is al-Taftāzānī's list of reasons, one being that the science became "so to speak, 'the speech' (al-kalam) to the exclusion of all other sciences" (tr. Elder) [C, S27].
- **taʾalluh / baḥth.** Suhrawardī's pair, "deification" and "research" (§9). The renderings "intuitive philosophy" and "discursive philosophy" are Walbridge–Ziai's [U, not opened].

### The tradition's own sortings

- **al-Fārābī, *Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm*.** It has five chapters: (1) the science of language, (2) logic, (3) mathematics, (4) physics and metaphysics, (5) political science, jurisprudence and dialectical theology (Najjar's headnote, from the preface) [C, S5]. In the preface, its uses include comparing the sciences, which "is more excellent, more useful, more precise", and exposing "whoever pretends to know a particular science" (headnote paraphrase) [C as paraphrased, S5]. The Arabic text is S5a.
- **al-Khwārizmī, *Mafātīḥ al-ʿulūm*.** Two discourses (*maqālatān*): the first "for the sciences of the Law (*ʿulūm al-sharīʿa*) and the Arabic sciences joined to them", the second "for the sciences of the foreigners (*ʿulūm al-ʿajam*), the Greeks and other nations" (my rendering) [C, S3]. Kalām is chapter 2 of the first discourse, with seven sections, covering Islamic sects, Christians, Jews and "the people of religions and sects (*arbāb al-milal wa-l-niḥal*)". Philosophy is chapter 1 of the second discourse: theoretical (physics, mathematics, *al-umūr al-ilāhiyya*, "in Greek *thāwlūjiyā*") and practical (three parts). On logic, "some make it a third part, some a part of theoretical knowledge, some an instrument of philosophy" [C, S3]. **Headline:** the encyclopaedist files *falsafa* among the foreigners' sciences and *kalām* among the Law's sciences.
- **Ibn Sīnā, *Divisions of the Rational Sciences*.** "Science is divided into a theoretical, abstract part and a practical part… the end of the theoretical part is truth, and the end of the practical is the good." Practical science has three parts (ethics, household, politics). In politics, "the treatment of prophecy and the Law is contained in their two books on the laws"; "Through this part of practical wisdom one knows the necessity of prophecy and the human species' need of the Law" (tr. Mahdi) [C, S6]. Also: "By the nomoi, the philosophers do not mean what the vulgar believe, which is that the nomos is nothing but a device and deceit" [C, S6].
- **al-Shahrastānī, *al-Milal wa-l-niḥal*.** The first sound division of people by opinions is into *ahl al-diyānāt wa-l-milal* (people of religions and communities) and *ahl al-ahwāʾ wa-l-niḥal* (people of caprices and sects), "such as the philosophers, the Dahriyya, the Sabians, the worshippers of stars and idols, and the Brahmins" [C, S8; my rendering]. The ground of the division: whoever holds a belief either "benefits from another (*mustafīd*)", which is obedience, and "religion is obedience", or "is independent in his opinion (*mustabidd bi-raʾyihi*)". "Those wholly independent in opinion, who deny prophecies, are such as the philosophers, the Sabians and the Brahmins; they do not hold to revealed laws, but lay down rational bounds (*ḥudūdan ʿaqliyya*) so that they can live together by them" [C, S8; my rendering]. **Headline:** the doxographer classes philosophy as a sect of independent opinion, set against received religion. The edition of the archive.org text is [U].
- **Ibn Khaldūn, *Muqaddimah* VI.** The two kinds of science (VI.9, above). The intellectual sciences "are not restricted to any particular religious group. They are studied by the people of all religious groups who are all equally qualified to learn them". There are four (logic, physics, metaphysics, mathematics), or seven with the four mathematical sciences (VI.18). Then the chapter "A refutation of philosophy" (VI.30) [C, S1].
- **The Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, Epistle 1 (on number).** *al-falsafa awwaluhā maḥabbat al-ʿulūm, wa-awsaṭuhā maʿrifat ḥaqāʾiq al-mawjūdāt bi-ḥasab al-ṭāqa al-insāniyya, wa-ākhiruhā al-qawl wa-l-ʿamal bi-mā yuwāfiqu al-ʿilm*. My rendering: "Philosophy: its beginning is love of the sciences, its middle knowledge of the realities of existing things as far as human capacity allows, and its end speaking and acting in accordance with knowledge." The philosophical sciences are four (mathematics, logic, physics, metaphysics), studied in that order [C, S29, Qum reprint]. Not in the brief; strong landing-page material (§13).
- **Ḥājjī Khalīfa** (17th c.) files the subject under *ʿilm al-ḥikma* [C, earlier round].

## 3. The reception and al-Kindī

### How the sciences came, in the tradition's own accounts

- **al-Maʾmūn's dream** (Ibn al-Nadīm, *Fihrist*, ch. 7, "Mention of the Reason Why Books on Philosophy and Other Ancient Sciences Became Plentiful in This Country"). Aristotle appears to the caliph: "'What is good?' He replied, 'What is good in the mind.' I said again, 'Then what is next?' He answered, 'What is good in the law.' I said, 'Then what next?' He replied, 'What is good with the public.' I said, 'Then what more?' He answered, 'More? There is no more'" (tr. Dodge) [C, S7]. **Headline:** the tradition's own founding story orders reason, law and public opinion. It is a story, and the page should present it as one.
- **Ibn Khaldūn's account** (VI.18): al-Manṣūr asked the Byzantine emperor for mathematical works and received Euclid; al-Maʾmūn "sent ambassadors to the Byzantine emperors… to discover the Greek sciences and to have them copied in Arabic writing". The Muslims "contradicted the First Teacher (Aristotle) on many points" and "surpassed their predecessors in the intellectual sciences". He names al-Fārābī and Ibn Sīnā in the East, Ibn Rushd and Ibn Bājja in Spain. He reports that philosophy is now "greatly cultivated in the land of Rome and along the adjacent northern shore" [C, S1]. He also tells the story that ʿUmar ordered the Persian books thrown into the water [C, S1]; that is a contested anecdote. Leave it out, or give it as his report.
- **al-Fārābī's lineage** (*Attainment* §53): "It is said that this science existed anciently among the Chaldeans… then to Egypt… to the Greeks… to the Syrians, and then to the Arabs… expounded in the Greek language, later in Syriac, and finally in Arabic" (tr. Mahdi) [C, S5]. It is his reported story, not a priority claim.
- **Facts (scholarship).** The translations "blossomed" under the ʿAbbāsids (750–1258), "in particular in the first two centuries". Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. 756), under al-Manṣūr, is credited with an early logic compendium [C, S34]. The *Bayt al-ḥikma* "cannot be viewed as an institutional center for the translations" [C, S34]. There were two ninth-century groups: the "circle of al-Kindī" and Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (d. 873) with his son and associates. Then came Abū Bishr Mattā and Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī, the "Baghdad Aristotelians" [C, S34]. Many translators were Syrian Christians [C, S30].

### al-Kindī (d. after 866, usually put in the early 870s [C, S30])

- **What it is.** *On First Philosophy* opens: "the human art which is highest in degree and most noble in rank is the art of philosophy, the definition of which is knowledge of the true nature of things, insofar as is possible for man. The aim of the philosopher is, as regards his knowledge, to attain the truth, and as regards his action, to act truthfully" (tr. Ivry, Abū Rīda p. 97) [C, S4]. The Arabic: *ṣināʿat al-falsafa allatī ḥadduhā ʿilm al-ashyāʾ bi-ḥaqāʾiqihā bi-qadr ṭāqat al-insān* [C, S4a]. "The noblest part of philosophy and the highest in rank is the First Philosophy, i.e., knowledge of the First Truth Who is the cause of all truth" [C, S4].
- **Its point.** "The knowledge of the true nature of things includes knowledge of Divinity, unity and virtue, and a complete knowledge of everything useful… It is the acquisition of all this which the true messengers brought from God" (Ivry) [C, S4]. He gives an argument against the opponents: they must say acquiring it is necessary or not; if not, "it is necessary for them to bring a cause of this, and to give a demonstration of this; and the presentation of cause and demonstration are part of the possession of knowledge" [C, S4].
- **How it is done.**
  - Collectively, over generations: "no man by the diligence of his quest has attained the truth… When, though, the little which each one of them who has acquired the truth is collected, something of great worth is assembled"; it was assembled only "in preceding past ages, age after age, until this our time" (Ivry) [C, S4].
  - Taking truth from anywhere: "We ought not to be ashamed of appreciating the truth and of acquiring it wherever it comes from, even if it comes from races distant and nations different from us" (Ivry) [C, S4]. The Arabic: *wa-yanbaghī lanā an lā nastaḥyī min istiḥsān al-ḥaqq wa-iqtināʾ al-ḥaqq min ayna atā, wa-in atā min al-ajnās al-qāṣiya ʿannā…* [C, S4a]. SEP's version, "from wherever it comes. Even if it should come from far-flung nations and foreign peoples", is the SEP author's [C, S30].
  - Against the critics, who "traffic in religion, though they are devoid of religion… one who trades in religion does not have religion" [C, S4].
  - The programme: "presenting the ancients' complete statement… and completing that which they did not state completely, following the custom of the language and contemporary usage" [C, S4].
- **On Definitions**, the six definitions: keep as now, [C as quoted, Stefaniuk], and name the parallel with Elias's six on `late-antiquity`.

### The dispute over Greek logic (not in the brief; a strong reception text)

- **Abū Bishr Mattā against Abū Saʿīd al-Sīrāfī**, Baghdad, 320 AH (932 CE), recorded by Abū Ḥayyān al-Tawḥīdī (tr. Margoliouth 1905) [C, S22].
  - Mattā: "I understand by Logic an instrument whereby sound speech is known from unsound, and wrong sense from right: like a balance" [C, S22].
  - al-Sīrāfī: "If Logic be the invention of a Greek made in the Greek language and according to Greek conventions… whence does it follow that the Turks, Indians, Persians, and Arabs should attend to it, and make it umpire…?" [C, S22].
  - Mattā: "in matters apprehended by the intellect all men are alike, as for example four and four are eight with all nations" [C, S22].
  - al-Sīrāfī: "Grammar, then, is Logic, only abstracted from the Arabic language, and Logic is Grammar, only rendered intelligible by language" [C, S22].
- **Headline:** the tradition's own debate over whether Greek philosophy is universal or one language's grammar. It frames the whole reception page.

## 4. Kalām: the theologians' rational discipline

- **al-Fārābī's definition** (*Iḥṣāʾ* V): "The art of dialectical theology (kalam) is a positive disposition that enables man to argue in the defense of the specific opinions and actions stated explicitly by the founder of the religion, and against everything that opposes these opinions and actions." The jurist "takes the opinions and the actions… as axioms" and infers; the theologian "defends the things that the jurist uses as axioms" (tr. Najjar) [C, S5].
  - The Arabic: *ṣināʿat al-kalām malaka yaqtadiru bihā al-insān ʿalā nuṣrat al-ārāʾ wa-l-afʿāl al-maḥdūda allatī ṣarraḥa bihā wāḍiʿ al-milla wa-tazyīf kull mā khālafahā bi-l-aqāwīl* [C, S5a].
  - **Word test:** "dialectical theology" is Najjar's rendering of *ṣināʿat al-kalām*, the "art of speech". Druart's "non-philosophical theology" is a further gloss.
  - He goes on to sort the theologians' methods: some argue religion is above reason; some harmonise texts with sense and common opinion; some counter-attack other religions; some silence opponents "from shame… or from fear"; some will use "falsehood, sophistry, confounding, and contentiousness" [C, S5]. That is the falāsifa's own view of kalām from outside.
- **al-Fārābī, *Kitāb al-ḥurūf* §§108–111.** *Al-milla idhā juʿilat insāniyya fa-hiya mutaʾakhkhira bi-l-zamān ʿan al-falsafa*: "religion, if made human, comes later in time than philosophy", since it teaches the many by persuasion and images what philosophy found. *Ṣināʿat al-kalām wa-l-fiqh mutaʾakhkhiratān bi-l-zamān ʿanhā wa-tābiʿatān lahā*: "the arts of kalām and fiqh are later in time than religion and follow it". Philosophy precedes religion "as the user of instruments precedes the instruments". Kalām, following a religion that teaches by images and persuasion, "knows only persuasive things" (my rendering) [C, S5b]. **Headline:** the falāsifa's own placing of kalām: two steps below demonstration, and dialectical or rhetorical.
- **al-Ashʿarī, *al-Ḥathth ʿalā l-baḥth*** (= *Istiḥsān al-khawḍ fī ʿilm al-kalām*; Frank's edition, S26).
  - The opponents: "a group of people made ignorance their capital; reflection and inquiry into religion weighed on them; they inclined to *taqlīd*". They call speaking of "body and accident, motion and rest… the atom and the leap, and the attributes of God" innovation, since the Prophet did not speak of them (my rendering) [C, S26].
  - His replies: the Prophet also never said that whoever inquires into these is an innovator, so by your own rule you are innovators. And the Prophet was not ignorant of these things: their principles are "found in the Qurʾān and the Sunna in general form, not in detail", as in Abraham's argument from the setting star, moon and sun, that what moves from place to place is no god (my rendering) [C, S26].
  - McCarthy's 1953 English translation was not reached [U].
- **al-Ghazālī, *Munqidh*, on kalām:** "a science, I found, which, though attaining its own aim, did not attain mine. Its aim was merely to preserve the creed of orthodoxy and to defend it against the deviations of heretics… So God brought into being the class of theologians, and moved them to support traditional orthodoxy with the weapon of systematic argument" (Watt) [C, S15]. When theologians turned to "substances and accidents", "since that was not the aim of their science, they did not deal with the question thoroughly" [C, S15].
- **Ibn Khaldūn, VI.14.** "This is a science that involves arguing with logical proofs in defense of the articles of faith and refuting innovators who deviate in their dogmas from the early Muslims and Muslim orthodoxy" [C, S1].
  - His history: when logic spread, "the school of recent scholars" arose, begun by al-Ghazālī and followed by Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī ("Ibn al-Khatib").
  - "The later scholars were very intent upon meddling with philosophical works… the two approaches have been mixed up… This has gone so far that the one discipline is no longer distinguishable from the other." The philosophers study bodies "in so far as they move or are stationary"; the theologians "in so far as they serve as an argument for the Maker".
  - "This science… is not something that is necessary to the contemporary student" [C, S1].
  - **Headline:** the tradition's own report of what modern scholars call the post-classical merger.
- **al-Ījī, *al-Mawāqif*.** *wa-l-kalām ʿilm yuqtadaru maʿahu ʿalā ithbāt al-ʿaqāʾid al-dīniyya bi-īrād al-ḥujaj wa-dafʿ al-shubah*: "kalām is a science by which one is enabled to establish religious beliefs by bringing proofs and repelling doubts" (my rendering) [C, S28, OCR legible]. al-Ījī died 756/1355 [C, S28 metadata].
- **al-Taftāzānī** (1322–1389 [C, S27 n.]), *Sharḥ al-ʿAqāʾid al-Nasafiyya* (tr. Elder).
  - The science of beliefs "is the science of the unity (al-tawhid) and attributes (al-sifat) of the Deity… entitled al-Kalam" [C, S27].
  - After the translations, theologians "mixed up with kalam much of philosophy in order to understand thoroughly the goals of philosophy… until they included in kalam most of physics and metaphysics and plunged into mathematics until theology was hardly to be distinguished from philosophy had it not been that it included 'things to be believed on authority' (al-sam'iyat). This is the kalam of the Later Theologians" [C, S27].
  - Its point: kalām is "the most noble of the sciences… because its aim is the attaining of the happiness of this life and the next" [C, S27].
- **Maimonides on kalām's origins** (*Guide* I.71, tr. Friedländer).
  - The Mutakallimūn took their propositions "from the works of those Greek and Syrian scholars who attempted to oppose the system of the philosophers"; Christian scholars "laid the foundation for this science of Dogmatics", "John the Grammarian, Ibn Adi" among them [C, S20].
  - Their fault: "they… did not investigate the real properties of things: first of all they considered what must be the properties of the things which should yield proof for or against a certain creed" [C, S20]. **Headline:** the falsafa side's verdict on kalām's method, from a Jewish author writing in Arabic.

## 5. al-Fārābī (d. 339/950 [C, S9 n.]): the philosopher, the imām and the lawgiver

- **What it is.** *Attainment* §53: the Greeks called it "true wisdom and the highest wisdom… and the scientific state of mind, philosophy (by which they meant the quest and the love for the highest wisdom)… the science of sciences, the mother of sciences, the wisdom of wisdoms, and the art of arts" (Mahdi 1963) [C, S5].
- **Its point.**
  - *Attainment* §54: "When the theoretical sciences are isolated and their possessor does not have the faculty for exploiting them for the benefit of others, they are defective philosophy" [C, S5].
  - §58: "the idea of the Philosopher, Supreme Ruler, Prince, Legislator, and Imam is but a single idea" [C, S5].
  - *Iḥṣāʾ* V: "That which is true happiness cannot possibly be of this life, but of another life after this"; political science sorts true happiness from "such things as wealth, honor, and the pleasures" [C, S5].
- **Philosophy and religion.**
  - §55: assent comes by "certain demonstration or… persuasion"; "according to the ancients, religion is an imitation of philosophy… Finally, philosophy is prior to religion in time" [C, S5].
  - Also §55: if the intelligibles themselves are used with persuasive methods, "then the religion comprising them is called popular, generally accepted, and external philosophy" [C, S5].
  - §59: "They are a religion for others, whereas, so far as he is concerned, they are philosophy" [C, S5].
  - *Kitāb al-ḥurūf* §§108–111 (above, §4).
- **How it is done.**
  - Demonstration against dialectic, sophistry and rhetoric: dialectical and sophistical powers, and "opined" or "counterfeit" philosophy, come before demonstrative philosophy in time, "as the nourishment of the tree precedes the fruit" (*Ḥurūf* §111, my rendering) [C, S5b].
  - §60, "mutilated philosophy": "the counterfeit philosopher, the vain philosopher, or the false philosopher" [C, S5].
- **Writing.** *Summary of Plato's Laws*, introduction: "the wise Plato did not feel free to reveal and uncover the sciences for all men. Therefore he followed the practice of using symbols, riddles, obscurity, and difficulty, so that science would not fall into the hands of those who do not deserve it" (tr. Mahdi). This comes with the story of the ascetic who escaped the tyrant's men without lying [C, S5]. **This is al-Fārābī's own statement.** Strauss's "esoteric writing" reading of it is Western framing (§11).
- **Word test.** "Imitation" and "similitude" are Mahdi's words; the Arabic (*muḥākāt*, *mithālāt*) is [U for §55]. In *Ḥurūf* §§108–110 the words are *mithālāt* and *khayālāt*, "likenesses" and "images" [C, S5b]. "Dialectical theology" is the translator's word for kalām (§4).

## 6. Ibn Sīnā (980–1037 [C, S6 headnote]): the system and its purpose

- **What it is.** Science divides into theoretical (end: truth) and practical (end: the good); prophecy and the Law belong to the practical part (*Divisions*, Mahdi) [C, S6]. *Ḥikma* is the soul becoming "an intelligible world corresponding to the existing world" (*Aqsām*) [C, S5a].
- **The two books, and their purpose** (Prologue to *The Cure*, §4, tr. Gutas). "I also wrote a book other than these two, in which I presented philosophy as it is naturally [perceived] and as required by an unbiased view which neither takes into account in [this book] the views of colleagues in the discipline… this is my book on Eastern philosophy. But as for the present book, it is more elaborate and more accommodating to my Peripatetic colleagues" [C, S12, T9]. Ibn Ṭufayl reports the same: Ibn Sīnā declared at the beginning of the *Shifāʾ* that "the truth in his opinion is otherwise… and that he who seeks pure truth should look for it in his Illuminative [or Oriental] Philosophy" (tr. Atiyeh) [C, S5].
- **The *Easterners* (*al-Mashriqiyyūn*), prologue.** "We have resolved to compile a treatise on matters about which researchers have disagreed. In it we shall neither proceed by following Partisan considerations, our own fancy, Customary Practice, or personal habit" (tr. Gutas) [C, S12, T10]. He describes Aristotle's successors as men who spent "their lives in efforts to understand what he accomplished best and in Partisan Adherence to some defective theories he originated… neither… thinking it permissible to consider the statements of the ancients in need of addition, correction, or revision" [C, S12, T8]. Add "loose ends" (T8 §1), already on the page [C, S12].
  - **Word test:** *mashriqī* means "Eastern"; "Illuminative/Oriental" is the reading that Atiyeh's bracket records. The Gutas–Corbin–Nasr dispute is Western framing (§11). On the page, give the title and Ibn Sīnā's own description only.
- **How he learned** (*Autobiography*, tr. Gohlman).
  - "I read the Metaphysics [of Aristotle], but I could not comprehend its contents… even when I had gone back and read it forty times and had got to the point where I had memorized it… 'This is a book which there is no way of understanding.'" A book-seller then sold him "Abu Nasr al-Farabi's book on the objects of the Metaphysics… in no time the objects of that book became clear to me" [C, S9]. Gohlman's note: the book is either the short *Aghrāḍ* or possibly *Kitāb al-ḥurūf* [C, S9 n. 30]; that is a detail.
  - Method: "because of those problems which used to baffle me, not being able to solve the middle term of the syllogism, I used to visit the mosque frequently and worship… until He opened the mystery of it to me" [C, S9].
  - "at that time I had a better memory for learning, but today my knowledge is more mature; otherwise it is the same; nothing new has come to me since" [C, S9].
- **Method in the late *Ishārāt*.** *Pointers and Reminders* (*al-Ishārāt wa-l-tanbīhāt*): a style of "pointers and reminders… which the student would complete himself", taken from the late-antique topos on Aristotle's obscurity [C, S35, Gutas]. This is the factual base for any "method" line.
- **Floating man.** Not reached in a primary text. That Ibn Sīnā presents it as a *tanbīh* ("alerting") rather than a proof is [U]. Keep it out unless that is checked (§16).

## 7. al-Ghazālī (1059–1111 [C, S5 n.]) and Ibn Rushd (d. 595/1198 [C, S9 n.])

### al-Ghazālī, *al-Munqidh min al-ḍalāl* (tr. Watt)

- **The impulse:** "To thirst after comprehension of things as they really are was my habit and custom from a very early age… the bonds of mere authority (taqlid) ceased to hold me… for I saw that Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims" [C, S15].
- **The four classes of seekers:** "(1) the Theologians (mutakallimun), who claim that they are the exponents of thought and intellectual speculation; (2) the Batiniyah, who consider that they, as the party of 'authoritative instruction' (ta'lim), alone derive truth from the infallible imam; (3) the Philosophers, who regard themselves as the exponents of logic and demonstration; (4) the Sufis or Mystics, who claim that they alone enter into the 'presence'… and possess vision and intuitive understanding." "The truth cannot lie outside these four classes" [C, S15]. Naive belief cannot be regained: "the glass of his naive beliefs is broken" [C, S15].
- **Method for criticism:** "a man cannot grasp what is defective in any of the sciences unless he has so complete a grasp of the science in question that he equals its most learned exponents" [C, S15].
- **The philosophers' sects:** "the Materialists (Dahriyun), the Naturalists (Tabi`iyun), and the Theists (Ilahiyun)" [C, S15].
- **Their sciences:** "the philosophical sciences are six in number: mathematics, logic, natural science, theology, politics, ethics" [C, S15].
  - Mathematics is demonstrative and not against religion, but has two "drawbacks". A student impressed by it supposes the metaphysics equally proven, and "A grievous crime indeed against religion has been committed by the man who imagines that Islam is defended by the denial of the mathematical sciences" [C, S15].
  - "The arguments in elementary matters of mathematics are demonstrative whereas those in theology (or metaphysics) are based on conjecture" [C, S15].
  - Politics is borrowed "from the Divine scriptures" and ethics "from the teaching of the mystics" [C, S15].
- **Where it ends:** mysticism "cannot be apprehended by study, but only by immediate experience (dhawq-literally 'tasting')"; "it is above all the mystics who walk on the road of God" [C, S15].

### al-Ghazālī, *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*

- **Purpose** (tr. Kamali): "I decided to write this book in order to refute the ancient philosophers. It will expose the incoherence of their beliefs and the inconsistency of their metaphysical theories"; "it is our purpose to disillusion those who think too highly of the philosophers, and consider them to be infallible"; "I will approach them in order to attack them, not to defend something of mine own" [C, S16].
  - **Translator caution:** the ghazali.org e-text heads Kamali's version "Translated into English from Urdu Translation" [C, S16]. For quotation on the page, prefer Marmura (BYU, 2nd ed. 2000) (§18).
- The *fatwā* at the end, declaring public teachers of the three positions unbelievers [C, S31].

### Ibn Rushd, *Faṣl al-maqāl* (tr. Hourani; Gibb Memorial printing of 1976)

- **Purpose:** "to examine, from the standpoint of the study of the Law, whether the study of philosophy and logic is allowed by the Law, or prohibited, or commanded, either by way of recommendation or as obligatory" [C, S17].
- **What it is:** philosophy is "nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan" [C, S17]. The proof-text is "Reflect, you have vision" (Q 59:2, *fa-iʿtabirū yā ulī al-abṣār* [C, S40]), "textual authority for the obligation to use intellectual reasoning" [C, S17].
- **How:** "by intellectual reasoning… the most perfect kind of study using the most perfect kind of reasoning" (demonstration) [C, S17]. Help from predecessors "regardless of whether this other one shares our religion or not". The analogy: in a valid sacrifice "no account is taken… of whether the instrument belongs to one who shares our religion" [C, S17]. This echoes al-Kindī's "wherever it comes from".
- **The three classes:** "the natures of men are on different levels with respect to [their paths to] assent": demonstration, dialectic, rhetoric. "This divine religion of ours has summoned people by these three methods", citing Q 16:125, "Summon to the way of your Lord by wisdom and by good preaching, and debate with them in the most effective manner" [C, S17; Qur'an text C, S40]. People in relation to scripture: "the rhetorical class… the overwhelming mass"; "the people of dialectical interpretation"; "the demonstrative class, by nature and training, i.e. in the art of philosophy" [C, S17].
- "Truth does not oppose truth"; philosophy "the friend and milk-sister of religion" [C, S17]. **Headings word test:** Hourani's chapter heads ("The Law makes philosophic studies obligatory") are in square brackets, i.e. the translator's [C, S17].
- **Against al-Ghazālī:** Abū Ḥāmid "was mistaken about the Peripatetic philosophers, in ascribing to them the assertion that God… does not know particulars at all" [C, S17].

### Ibn Rushd, *Tahāfut al-tahāfut* (tr. van den Bergh)

- "The aim of this book is to show the different degrees of assent and conviction attained by the assertions in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, and to prove that the greater part has not reached the degree of evidence and of truth" [C, S18].
- On the principles of religion: the ancient philosophers held that "they are the principles of the religions, and the man who inquires into them and doubts them merits punishment… one can only attain knowledge after the attainment of virtue" [C, S18]. This is a strong line on philosophy's limits in its own voice.

## 8. al-Andalus: Ibn Bājja, Ibn Ṭufayl, Maimonides

- **Ibn Bājja (d. 1138 [C, S5 headnote]), *Tadbīr al-mutawaḥḥid*** (tr. Berman, from Asín Palacios's 1946 edition).
  - The word: "governance" (*tadbīr*) is "the ordering of actions to an end" [C, S5].
  - The Weeds: those "who stumble upon a true opinion that does not exist in the city or the opposite of which is believed in the city… are called Weeds" [C, S5]. In imperfect cities "the happy… will possess only the happiness of an isolated individual"; "These individuals are the ones meant by the Sufis when they speak of the 'strangers'… having travelled in their minds to other stations" [C, S5].
  - Purpose: "We intend to discourse here about the governance of this solitary man… just as the doctor states how the isolated man in these cities should manage himself in order to be healthy" [C, S5]. The solitary "must associate with those who pursue the sciences", or "emigrate to the ways of life in which the sciences are pursued—if such are to be found" [C, S5].
  - Ibn Rushd on the book: "the only one to treat this subject" (headnote quotation) [C as quoted, S5].
  - **Word test:** *nawābit* for "Weeds" and *ghurabāʾ* for "strangers" are [U] (Arabic not opened).
- **Ibn Ṭufayl (d. 1185 [C, S5 headnote]), *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān*** (tr. Atiyeh, from Gauthier's 2nd ed., Beirut 1936).
  - Stated aim: an answer to a friend who asked for "the secrets of the illuminative [or oriental] philosophy referred to by… Ibn Sina" [C, S5].
  - Two ways of knowing: "taste (dhawq)" against "theoretical perception, which results from syllogistic reasoning". The parable: the man born blind, who knows the city by his other senses and whose sight is then restored, finding "nothing… different from what he has believed it to be" but with "a greater lucidity and clarity, and an exalted pleasure" [C, S5].
  - A survey of Andalusian philosophy: mathematics first, then logic, then Ibn Bājja, unfinished. "Do not think that the philosophy that reached us in the works of Aristotle, Abū Nasr, and in [Avicenna's] Healing is sufficient" [C, S5].
  - The end of the story: Ḥayy finds that the Law's teachings are "the similitudes" of what he saw ("reason and tradition agree"). He fails to teach the islanders and concludes that "all wisdom, guidance to the right path, and good fortune reside in the utterances of the apostles of God… nothing else is possible", and tells them to keep to the external practices [C, S5].
  - Why he wrote it: the spread of "corrupt opinions aired and broached by some contemporaneous pretenders to philosophy"; "we have not left whatever secrets we confided to these pages without a thin veil and a delicate curtain, which is easy to break by those worthy of doing so" [C, S5].
- **Maimonides (*Guide*; tr. Friedländer 1904, Wikisource).**
  - Purpose: "The object of this treatise is to enlighten a religious man who has been trained to believe in the truth of our holy Law… and at the same time has been successful in his philosophical studies. Human reason has attracted him to abide within its sphere… Hence he is lost in perplexity and anxiety" [C, S20].
  - Its primary object: "to explain certain words occurring in the prophetic books", homonyms and figures. A knowledge of "Logic and Natural Philosophy" is indispensable [C, S20].
  - Method: "seven causes of inconsistencies and contradictions to be met with in a literary work" [C, S20]. The seventh, concealment, is the hinge of the Straussian reading (§11).
  - Palace parable (III.51): "those who have succeeded in finding a proof for everything that can be proved… are in the palace"; "so long as you are engaged in studying the Mathematical Sciences and Logic, you belong to those who go round about the palace in search of the gate" [C, S20].
  - Arabic: that the *Guide* was written in Judaeo-Arabic is [U this round] (SEP Maimonides fact; reopen). On kalām: §4.
- **Jews and Christians within the tradition.** Maimonides on the Andalusian Jewish scholars, who "followed the teachings of the philosophers" and "did not adopt any of the methods of the Mutakallemim" [C, S20]. Christian translators and philosophers: Ḥunayn, Mattā, Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī [C, S34]. al-Fārābī's *Enumeration* was read "in the Arabic original" by Judaeo-Arabic authors, and Falaquera and Kalonymos made Hebrew extracts [C, S5 headnote]. Narboni wrote a Hebrew commentary on *Ḥayy* (1349) [C, S5].
- **Ibn Rushd** could sit here as an Andalusian (placement option, §14).

## 9. The later ḥikma

- **Suhrawardī, *Ḥikmat al-ishrāq*, introduction** (Arabic, S24; my renderings unless marked).
  - Why written: his companions asked him to write "what came to me by taste (*bi-l-dhawq*) in my retreats and visions" [C, S24].
  - "Knowledge is not a preserve of one people, so that the gate of the unseen world is shut after them"; the worst age is one in which "the carpet of striving (*ijtihād*) is rolled up" [C, S24].
  - "It did not come to me first by thought (*bi-l-fikr*)… then I sought the proof for it" [C, S24; and as quoted, SEP].
  - "The world has never been without wisdom (*ḥikma*) and without a person upholding it… he is God's vicegerent on His earth"; the difference between earlier and later sages "is only in words and in their habits of explicitness and allusion" [C, S24].
  - **Ranks of sages:** *ḥakīm ilāhī mutawaghghil fī al-taʾalluh ʿadīm al-baḥth*, *ḥakīm baḥḥāth ʿadīm al-taʾalluh*, *ḥakīm ilāhī mutawaghghil fī al-taʾalluh wa-l-baḥth*, and so on down to *ṭālib li-l-baḥth faqaṭ*. Leadership goes to the one deep in both; failing that, to the one deep in *taʾalluh*. There is "no leadership on God's earth for the researcher deep in research who has not gone deep in *taʾalluh*" [C, S24].
  - Readers: "This book of ours is for seekers of *taʾalluh* and *baḥth*… whoever wants research alone, let him take the way of the Peripatetics (*ṭarīqat al-mashshāʾīn*), for it is good and sound for research alone" [C, S24].
  - **Word test:** *taʾalluh* is literally "becoming god-like"; Walbridge–Ziai's "intuitive philosophy" [U] is an interpretive rendering.
- **Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (1201–1274).** *Akhlāq-i Nāṣirī*, compiled 633/1235 and dedicated to the Ismaili governor Nāṣir al-Dīn Muḥtashim; a twenty-year commentary on Ibn Sīnā's *Ishārāt*; a refutation of al-Shahrastānī, *Maṣāraʿat al-muṣāriʿ* [C, S36 IEP].
  - The *Akhlāq*'s definition of *ḥikmat* and its division into theoretical and practical: [U]. Wickens's translation is lending-only, and the Persian lithograph OCR on archive.org is unusable (§18).
  - The *Ishārāt* commentary as a defence of Ibn Sīnā against Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī: [U]. Also [U]: the quip that Rāzī's commentary is "a wounding (*jarḥ*), not a commentary (*sharḥ*)", attributed to Ṭūsī.
- **Mullā Ṣadrā, *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fī al-asfār al-ʿaqliyya al-arbaʿa*** (Beirut, Dār iḥyāʾ al-turāth al-ʿarabī, vol. 1; my renderings).
  - **Definition** (opening of the *muqaddima*): *iʿlam anna al-falsafa istikmāl al-nafs al-insāniyya bi-maʿrifat ḥaqāʾiq al-mawjūdāt ʿalā mā hiya ʿalayhā wa-l-ḥukm bi-wujūdihā taḥqīqan bi-l-barāhīn lā akhdhan bi-l-ẓann wa-l-taqlīd bi-qadr al-wusʿ al-insānī, wa-in shiʾta qulta naẓm al-ʿālam naẓman ʿaqliyyan ʿalā ḥasab al-ṭāqa al-bashariyya li-yaḥṣula al-tashabbuh bi-l-bārī taʿālā*. "Philosophy is the perfecting of the human soul by knowing the realities of existing things as they are, and judging of their existence by verification through demonstrations, not by taking on opinion and *taqlīd*, as far as human capacity allows; or, if you like, the ordering of the world as an intelligible order according to human capacity, so that likeness to the Creator may be attained" [C, S25].
  - The two parts: the theoretical, whose end is "the soul's being imprinted with the form of existence in its order, completeness and perfection, and its becoming an intelligible world resembling the concrete world (*ʿāliman ʿaqliyyan mushābihan li-l-ʿālam al-ʿaynī*), not in its matter but in its form"; and the practical [C, S25]. He cites the prophetic prayer "Lord, show us things as they are" (*rabbi arinā al-ashyāʾ kamā hiya*) [C, S25].
  - **The four journeys** (preface): "Know that the wayfarers among the gnostics and the saints have four journeys: from creation to the Real (*min al-khalq ilā al-ḥaqq*); by the Real in the Real (*bi-l-ḥaqq fī al-ḥaqq*); from the Real to creation by the Real (*min al-ḥaqq ilā al-khalq bi-l-ḥaqq*); by the Real in creation (*bi-l-ḥaqq fī al-khalq*). I arranged my book… in four journeys and named it *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya fī al-asfār al-ʿaqliyya*" [C, S25].
  - **Headline:** Ṣadrā's definition joins Ibn Sīnā's (the soul as an intelligible world) and al-Kindī's and al-Fārābī's (likeness to God), and his structure is a Sufi itinerary.
  - Keep from SEP, [C as quoted, earlier round]: "inner revelation… cannot be really known through rehearsing discursive doctrines"; "demonstration does not conflict with inner disclosure"; the ʿAlī saying.
  - Death date 1640 [U this round].
- **Ibn Khaldūn's dissent** (VI.30) closes the classical line or opens the landing page (placement option). He notes that the intellectual sciences live on "in the non-Arab 'Iraq and, farther east, in Transoxania", and names al-Taftāzānī as versed in them [C, S1, VI.18]. That is the tradition's own observation that *ḥikma* continued in the East.

## 10. Modern

- **al-Afghānī, letter to the *Journal des Débats*, 18 May 1883**, replying to Renan's Sorbonne lecture of 29 March 1883 [C, S37].
  - The religions "se ressemblent toutes. Aucune entente ni aucune réconciliation ne sont possibles entre ces religions et la philosophie. La religion impose à l'homme sa foi et sa croyance, tandis que la philosophie l'en affranchit totalement ou en partie" ("are all alike. No agreement and no reconciliation are possible between these religions and philosophy. Religion imposes its faith and belief on man, while philosophy frees him from them wholly or in part") [C, S37].
  - "Toutes les fois que la religion aura le dessus, elle éliminera la philosophie… le triomphe ne sera pas pour la libre pensée, parce que la raison déplaît à la foule" ("Whenever religion has the upper hand, it will eliminate philosophy… the triumph will not be free thought's, because reason displeases the crowd") [C, S37].
  - Hope: Muslim society "arrivera un jour à briser ses liens" ("will one day manage to break its bonds"), as Christian society did [C, S37].
  - Against Renan: "Les Européens ont fait bon accueil à Aristote, émigré et devenu arabe ; mais ils ne songeaient nullement à lui quand il était grec et leur voisin" ("The Europeans welcomed Aristotle once he had emigrated and become Arab, but gave him no thought when he was Greek and their neighbour") [C, S37].
  - He read Renan in "une traduction plus ou moins fidèle" ("a more or less faithful translation") [C, S37]. That his letter was itself written in Arabic and translated is [U].
  - **Headline:** al-Afghānī's own answer is close to Ibn Rushd's three classes, inverted into pessimism.
- **Muḥammad ʿAbduh, *Risālat al-tawḥīd*** (from lectures given in Beirut in exile after 1882; tr. Masʿad and Cragg 1966) [C, S21 intro].
  - "Islam supervened, to present its case to reason, to call on mind and intelligence for action" [C, S21].
  - Against those who held "that reason and religion had nothing in common": "if that claim were to be allowed, religion would not be a means whereby man could be guided" [C, S21].
  - On the philosophers: "the only concern of philosophic rationalists was to gain knowledge… most of the philosophers were subject to two influences… an admiration for all that derived from the Greek philosophers… and with it a too precipitate inclination to accept their authority" (p. 38) [C, S21].
  - ʿAbduh's debate with Faraḥ Anṭūn over Ibn Rushd (1902–3) [U]; a strong modern line on what philosophy is to be (§13).
- **Muhammad Iqbal, *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*** (lectures at Madras, Hyderabad and Aligarh; Lahore reprint May 1944) [C, S23].
  - Preface: "I have tried to meet, even though partially, this urgent demand by attempting to reconstruct Muslim religious philosophy with due regard to the philosophical traditions of Islam and the more recent developments in the various domains of human knowledge" [C, S23].
  - Lecture I: "The spirit of philosophy is one of free inquiry. It suspects all authority. Its function is to trace the uncritical assumptions of human thought to their hiding places" [C, S23].
  - Lecture V: "while Greek philosophy very much broadened the outlook of Muslim thinkers, it, on the whole, obscured their vision of the Quran"; "the spirit of the Quran was essentially anti-classical" [C, S23].
  - Dates: 1930 first edition, 1934 OUP with a seventh lecture [U].
- **Muṣṭafā ʿAbd al-Rāziq, *Tamhīd li-taʾrīkh al-falsafa al-islāmiyya*** (OpenITI text, S38; my renderings).
  - Method: "a return to Islamic rational reflection (*al-naẓar al-ʿaqlī al-islāmī*) in its first simplicity, and tracing its stages", against Western scholars who trace it to non-Arab sources and Muslim scholars who "weigh philosophy on the scale of religion" [C, S38].
  - Thesis: "this *ijtihād bi-l-raʾy* in the legal rulings is the first thing that sprouted of rational reflection among the Muslims… and in its precincts ripened a philosophical science, *uṣūl al-fiqh*… before Greek philosophy did its work" [C, S38].
  - Scope: "*ʿilm uṣūl al-fiqh*… is not weakly connected with philosophy", and the study of Islamic philosophy will come to include it beside kalām and Sufism [C, S38].
  - He wrote the pages while teaching at the Egyptian University, until he left it "at the start of 1939" [C, S38]. The 1944 publication date is [U].
  - **Headline:** the modern reply to Renan from inside al-Azhar's world. Islamic philosophy begins in jurisprudence, not in translation.
- **Muḥammad ʿĀbid al-Jābirī (b. Figuig 27 Dec 1936, d. 3 May 2010 [C, S39]), *Naqd al-ʿaql al-ʿarabī*.** The three systems: *bayān*, *ʿirfān* and *burhān*, glossed "rhetoric or linguistic analysis, gnosticism, and deductive reasoning". "The first two dominated Arab-Islamic culture and the third… died with Ibn Rushd and Ibn Khaldun after him", strongest "in the Maghreb". Ibn Sīnā and al-Ghazālī "de-rationalized" the Arab-Islamic mind; he calls for "an epistemological break… at the level of concepts" [C as summarised, S39, Hashas]. His own wording is [U] (§18). **Caution:** an online search summary got the three terms backwards. Check against the text.
- **Ṭāhā ʿAbd al-Raḥmān.** *Fiqh al-falsafa* (vol. 1, *al-Falsafa wa-l-tarjama*; vol. 2, *al-Qawl al-falsafī*) and the critique of a universal philosophy (*al-Ḥaqq al-ʿarabī fī al-ikhtilāf al-falsafī*): all [U]. He is named among al-Jābirī's critics [C, S39]. The open-access Brill volume (Hashas and al-Khatib 2020) is bot-walled (§18).
- **Seyyed Hossein Nasr, *Islamic Philosophy from Its Origin to the Present: Philosophy in the Land of Prophecy* (SUNY 2006).** He writes that Mullā Ṣadrā "sought to unify the knowledge received through burhân or demonstration, 'irfân or contemplation, intellectual intuition and gnosis and Qur'ân… thereby giving full expression to philosophy cultivated in the land of prophecy" (p. 224) [C as quoted, S39a, Acar review]. Nasr's own definition of "prophetic philosophy" is [U] (§18). **Placement note:** Nasr is a voice of the tradition here. His historiographical quarrel with Gutas is Western framing (§11).

## 11. Western framing: to move to the Western section

These are the Western scholars' debates about the tradition. Facts drawn from the same scholars stay on the tradition pages.

1. **The name of the field.** Adamson: "Islamic" and "Arabic" too narrow; "philosophy in the Islamic world", "a neutral designation" [C, earlier round]. Gutas's preference for "Arabic philosophy" [U].
2. **Griffel's revision.** The *Tahāfut* as a philosophical book in the genre of kalām [C, S31]; post-classical *ḥikma* replacing the word *falsafa*, and theologians who "engaged in philosophy just as much as the falāsifa" [C as quoted, earlier round]; "heirs to the late-antique tradition…" [C, earlier round].
3. **Sabra's "naturalization"** of philosophy and the Greek sciences into kalām (1987, cited by Griffel) [C, S31].
4. **The decline narrative and its critics.** Renan and Hegel, as the reviewer reports them: Hegel said Arabic philosophy "has no content of any interest" [C as reported, earlier round]. The spread of the decline thesis (Weinberg) [C as reported, earlier round]. Bertolacci's "golden" and "platinum" ages [C, earlier round]. Gutas's "golden age… 1000–1350" title [U].
5. **Renan, *L'Islamisme et la science* (Sorbonne, 29 March 1883; Calmann Lévy 1883)** [C, S37a]. "Ce grand ensemble philosophique, que l'on a coutume d'appeler arabe, parce qu'il est écrit en arabe, mais qui est en réalité gréco-sassanide. Il serait plus exact de dire grec" ("this great philosophical whole, usually called Arab because it is written in Arabic, but which is in reality Graeco-Sasanian. It would be more exact to say Greek"). "Averroès, Avicenne, Albaténi sont des Arabes, comme Albert le Grand… Spinoza sont des Latins" ("Averroes, Avicenna, Albatenius are Arabs as Albertus Magnus… Spinoza are Latins") [C, S37a]. Pair it with al-Afghānī's reply (the reply itself stays on the modern page).
6. **Gutas against Corbin and Nasr** on *al-Mashriqiyyūn*, "Eastern" against "Oriental/Illuminative" [C for Gutas's position, earlier round; U for Corbin and Nasr]. Gutas also faults Nasr's translation of the *Easterners* introduction as incomplete [C, S12].
7. **Gutas's framing of Ibn Sīnā**: religion made a subject within philosophy, against al-Fārābī's image model [C, earlier round].
8. **Hadot's "way of life" applied to Mullā Ṣadrā** (Rizvi) [C, earlier round]. The same rule applies as on the Greek pages.
9. **Druart's and Germann's glosses**: "non-philosophical theology"; philosophy superior "only" epistemically [C, earlier round].
10. **Leo Strauss and the Lerner–Mahdi school**: esoteric writing, built on al-Fārābī's *Plato's Laws* introduction (the ascetic; "symbols, riddles, obscurity") and Maimonides' seventh cause [the primary lines C, S5 and S20; Strauss's own texts U, not opened]. The 1963 sourcebook's introduction is in this line [C, S5, it frames medieval political philosophy as the philosophy–Law relation]. Use its translations, not its framing.
11. **Europe reading *Ḥayy*.** Pococke's Latin, *Philosophus Autodidactus* (1671); Keith (1674) and Ashwell (1686) from the Latin; Ockley (1708) from the Arabic, subtitled "by the meer Light of Nature", with an appendix against "Enthusiasts" [C, S5 headnote]. This is reception, so it goes to the West reading the other traditions.
12. **The Western scholars' disputes over al-Jābirī** (Tarabichi, Laroui, Arkoun, Ḥanafī as critics [C, S39]). These are Arab critics, so they are within the tradition, not Western framing. Keep them on the modern page if anywhere. Listed here only to mark the difference.

## 12. "Into the Latin West": to the Western medieval page

To move with the present wording, re-marked (the Western page should also take §11.5 and §11.11):

- Toledo, second half of the twelfth century: Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus. al-Fārābī's *Enumeration*, translated by both, "served as a model for a coherent translation program" [C, S32]. Gundissalinus's own abbreviating translation revised Gerard's earlier one, and his "Arabicized treatise" was "the main channel of al-Fārābī's influence" [C, S32]. The Lerner–Mahdi headnote dates Gerard's complete Latin version to "about 1175", and has Gundisalvi extract "about half of it in his composite work De Scientiis" [C, S5].
- "Al-Fārābī's emphasis on demonstration as the pivotal means for the acquisition of certain knowledge is an important innovation of Arabic philosophy, which reached the Latin West via Gundisalvi" [C, S32].
- Ibn Rushd's translation "began with Michael Scott, around 1220"; "already by the 1230s he is known as 'the Commentator'"; "Averroist" pejorative "by the 1260s" [C, S33].
- Happiness through conjunction with the active intellect, possible in this life, shared by al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā and Ibn Rushd. Aquinas rejected it; 1277, arts. 40 and 176 [C, S32 for art. 176 and "no better state"; art. 40 is already on `medieval`].
- Ibn Khaldūn's report that philosophy is now studied "in the land of Rome and along the adjacent northern shore" [C, S1] could open the Latin paragraph from the Islamic side. It is optional.
