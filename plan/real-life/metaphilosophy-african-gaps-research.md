# Philosophy in Africa, the gaps: research note

Purpose: to fill eight gaps for the Africa section of the Metaphilosophy subject, which is being rebuilt as a landing page plus one page per line or school. Each page answers three questions in running prose: what the activity (the love of knowledge and wisdom, under whatever name) is, what its point is, and how it is done. The answers come from the tradition's own texts and thinkers. Scholars of any origin are used for facts only: dates, what a word meant, translations. European verdicts on whether Africa "has philosophy" are not the frame; African thinkers answering them are. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, keyed to the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = wording checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[C, tertiary]** = checked in Wikipedia or Wiktionary; good for a date or a gloss, not for a quotation. **[U]** = unverified. "My rendering" = my English from a German or French text.

**Access.** Archive.org was reachable, but its lending copies (Gyekye 1987, Mudimbe 1988, Hountondji 1983, Nyerere 1966, Boyd 1989, Hiskett 1973, Mbiti's Heinemann printing) return 401. Two books I used, Abimbola (1975) and Oluwole (2017), are archive.org uploads by a private user, not publisher copies. I used them only for short quotations; the page should cite the printed books. I did not use an archive.org upload labelled as a libgen copy of Hunwick (2003). `publication.codesria.org` timed out this round, so Diagne (2016) was read from a mirror of CODESRIA's open-access PDF (F3). `repository.uwc.ac.za` returned 404. `fulcrum.org` (Mudimbe) is behind a subscription.

## 1. Words for the activity in African languages

**Akan (Twi).**
- *Nyansa* = wisdom. Dzobo: "The word *nyansa* is usually used to translate the English term 'wisdom', but sometimes it is translated as knowledge". He derives it from "*nya* and *nsa* meaning 'that which is obtained and is never exhausted', i.e., a lesson which is learned from experience and is lasting" [C, D3, Dzobo ch. 3, p. 75]. The etymology is Dzobo's; I have no second source. He points to Gyekye (1987), ch. 4, for "a somewhat different and detailed analysis of nyansa" (editors' note) [C, D3].
- Wise person: *onyansafo* (pl. *anyansafo*), in the Twi texts of Christaller's 1879 proverb collection as Rattray prints them [C, D4, nos. 594, 597–599]. Dzobo pairs Akan *anyansafo* with Ewe *nunyala* [C, D3, p. 75].
- **Ewe**, for contrast: *nunya* = knowledge, *nunyala* = wise person. Dzobo says both "must have a practical bearing on the conduct of life" [C, D3, pp. 75–76].
- ***Nyansapɛ* as "the Akan word for philosophy"**: **[U]**. I found no source I could open. Searches return *nyansapɔ* ("wisdom knot", an Adinkra symbol), which is a different word; keep them apart. Check Gyekye 1987, ch. 4 (Sources to fetch).

**Proverbs on wisdom (Akan).**
- "Wisdom is not in the head of one person (*nyansa nni onipa baako ti mu*)". Gyekye cites it as the ground of "One head does not go into council (*ti koro nko agyina*)", and reads it to mean "that one should not, or cannot, regard one's own intellectual position as final or unassailable or beyond criticism, but must expect it to be evaluated by others" [C, D3, Gyekye ch. 12, p. 250; the OCR reads "nvansa", "aqvina"].
- "Wisdom is not gold dust that it should be tied up and put away" (*Nyansa nnye sika na wɔakyekyere asie*) [C, D4, no. 596, Rattray's translation; C, D3, Dzobo p. 76, "Wisdom is not (like) money which may be kept in a safe"]. Rattray's no. 600 is "No one ties up a wisdom-bag, and takes it and puts it away in a box and comes and stands in the courtyard and says, 'Explain the matter to me'" [C, D4].
- The Ananse tale. The Spider "collected all the wisdom of the world and shut it up in a gourd"; the gourd broke, "and men came and picked up what each could carry away" (Rattray, "taken down from the lips of a native") [C, D4, p. 66]. Dzobo tells the same tale of "knowledge" [C, D3, p. 76 n. 3].
- Ewe: "Knowledge is like a baobab tree; no one person can embrace it with both arms" (*Nunya adidoe, asi metune o*). Akan: "Knows all, knows nothing" (*Nim, nnim*) [C, D3, p. 76].
- **Word test, *Nokware mu nni abra*.** Rattray renders it "In truth there is no deceit" [C, D4, no. 601]. Wiredu renders it "there is no conflict in truth", which he reads as the principle of non-contradiction (this is the rendering on the current page). The two translations differ on *abra*. If the page keeps Wiredu's reading, it should say it is his.

**Yoruba.**
- *Ọgbọ́n* = wisdom. Abimbola gives Ifá's praise-name *Akéréfinúṣọgbọ́n*, "the small man with a mind full of wisdom" [C, D1, p. 2; diacritics lost in the OCR, restored from the standard spelling]. Oluwole objects that the English "Yoruba god of Wisdom" would go back into Yoruba as "Orunmila olorun ogbon", a phrase "nowhere in the extant literature" [C, D2, Preface].
- *Ìmọ̀* = knowledge. M. A. Makinde (1983), quoted by Oluwole: Ifá "is described as an inexhaustible repository of knowledge (*Imo aimo tan*)" [C as quoted, D2].
- *Òye* = understanding: **[U]** from a dictionary. Yoruba Wikipedia titles its philosophy article *Ìmọ̀ye* (*ìmọ̀* + *òye*) and glosses *philosophia* as "ife oye", love of understanding [C, tertiary, D6]. That is evidence of current usage, not of a classical term.
- *Ìmọ̀ ìjìnlẹ̀* as the modern term for philosophy: **[U]**, not found. Yoruba Wikipedia uses *ìjìnlẹ̀* ("depth") in other coinages.
- *Babaláwo*. Abimbola: "*awo* which means 'secret' … *babalawo* which can roughly be translated into English as 'father of the secrets'" [C, D1, p. 5]. Oluwole: "Father of esoteric knowledge", and the "appropriate translation … in contemporary understanding is: 'Professor of [Yoruba] Classical Philosophy'" [C, D2, Introduction, pp. 22–23]. **Word test:** the second rendering is Oluwole's argument, not a gloss.

**Swahili.** *Falsafa* = philosophy, borrowed from Arabic *falsafa* (< Greek). *Hekima* = wisdom, from Arabic *ḥikma*. *Busara* = "prudence, foresight, tact; wisdom", from Arabic *baṣāra* [C, tertiary, D5]. So the Swahili words for the activity are Arabic loans. *Ujamaa* is "familyhood" [C as quoted, E3].

**Ge'ez / Amharic.**
- *Ṭǝbäb* (root ṭ-b-b) = "ability, skill; wisdom, knowledge" in Amharic. *Ṭäbib* = "wise; artisan, craftsman" in Amharic and Tigrinya [C, tertiary, B4]. The Ge'ez sense is [U] (Dillmann or Leslau not opened). The overlap of "wise" and "craftsman" in one root is a fact of the lexicon. Any link to the Egyptian "craftsman" lines (§2) would be my observation and should not go on a page unsourced.
- The title *Maṣḥafa falāsfā ṭabibān*, "Book of the Wise Philosophers" [C, B1, title page]. *Falāsfā* is a loan ("philosophers", via Arabic). Amharic *fälasfa* = philosopher, and *fəlsəfəna* = philosophy [C, tertiary, B4, D6].

**Other modern coinages, tertiary only.** The Wikipedia titles for "Philosophy" in some African languages split two ways. Loans: Hausa and Swahili *Falsafa*, Kinyarwanda *Filozofi*, Sesotho *Filosofi*, Xhosa *Ifilosofi*. Coinages: Igbo *Ákọ na Uche*, Zulu *Injulalwazi*, Wolof *Xeltu*, Shona *Ugondamano*, Somali *Murtimaal* [C, tertiary, D6]. Their glosses are [U]. Useful only as a one-line illustration that some languages borrow the Greek word and others build one; I would not put them on a page without a linguist's source.

## 2. Ancient Egypt beyond Ptahhotep

All three texts are available in Blackman's 1927 English of Erman's German (1923) (A1), and Amenemope also in Budge (1924) (A2). Both are old. The modern standard is Lichtheim, which I did not open. These are two-step translations (hieratic → German → English), so wording should be marked "tr. Blackman from Erman".

**Instruction for Merikare** (Erman: "Merikere"). A father-king advises his son; the setting is Herakleopolitan, "between the Old and Middle Kingdoms" [C, A1, p. 75]. Composition is "probably of Middle Kingdom date (2025–1700 BC)", set in the First Intermediate Period (c. 2150–2025 BC) [C, tertiary, A3]. That is a detail of dating; state it in a clause.
- The point: "Be a craftsman in speech, so that thou mayest prevail, for the power of (a man) is the tongue, and speech is mightier than any fighting" [C, A1, pp. 75–76].
- How it is done: "Truth cometh to him fully kneaded, after the manner of that which the forefathers spake. Copy thy fathers, them that have gone before thee. Behold, their words endure in writing. Open (the book) and read, and copy the knowledge, so that the craftsman too may become a wise man (?)" [C, A1, p. 76]. Erman's note: the image is from brewing, "truth already worked up into shape is before you in the ancient writings" [C, A1]. The "(?)" is Erman's, so the "wise man" clause is uncertain in his reading.
- "Do right so long as thou abidest on the earth" [C, A1, p. 77].

**Instruction of Amenemope.** Ramesside composition (c. 1300–1075 BCE); 30 chapters; genre "instruction (Egyptian *sebayt*)" [C, tertiary, A3]. Budge dated it earlier; follow the modern date.
- The title, in Budge's translation: "The beginning of the teaching concerning life (i.e., of how to live) … [it] will make him to follow a straight course on the roads of life" [C, A2, pp. 140–141].
- How it is done, ch. 1: "I pray thee to lend me thine ears, hearken to the things that will be said [by me]. I pray thee to give thy mind … to the difficult matters which will be unravelled [by me]. The setting of them in thy heart will be advantageous [to thee]" [C, A2, ll. 47–49].
- The close, ch. 30: "Behold the Thirty Chapters! They will give [thee] pleasure, they will teach [thee] … They will give knowledge to (i.e., make to be wise) the ignorant man … set them, I beseech thee, in thy heart; Make thyself to be the man who has unravelled their contents, Thinking out and understanding [their] teaching" [C, A2, ll. 539–547, pp. 178–179]. Budge's brackets are his supplements.
- Wikipedia's "silent man" against "heated man" and "Age of Personal Piety" are modern scholars' readings [C, tertiary, A3]. Leave them out, or attribute them.

**The Dispute between a Man and his Ba** (Erman: "The Dispute with his Soul of one who is tired of Life"). It survives on a Berlin papyrus of the Middle Kingdom [C, A1, p. 86 n. 1]; Wikipedia's "probably … Amenemhat III" is [C, tertiary, A3]. Genre is disputed in a detail: Erman files it under "Meditations and Complaints", not instructions [C, A1, contents], while Wikipedia calls it *sebayt* [C, tertiary, A3]. State that it is a dialogue and move on.
- Form: a man and his *ba* argue "before certain judges, whose tongue is not biased" (Erman's summary) [C, A1, p. 86]. **Word test:** *ba* is Egyptian; "soul" is the translator's word [C, A1; A3].
- The *ba*'s counsel: "Hearken thou unto me, lo, it is good for a man when he hearkeneth. Follow the glad day and forget care" [C, A1, p. 88]. That ties back to Ptahhotep's hearing/obeying.
- Q1 (what the activity is) is not stated in the text. What it shows is the form: argument with oneself, to a verdict.

**Diop and Obenga: Egypt read as African philosophy (their own words where found).**
- **Cheikh Anta Diop** (1923–1986) [C, tertiary, A6]. "L'Égypte jouera, dans la culture africaine repensée et rénovée, le même rôle que les antiquités gréco-latines dans la culture occidentale" (*Civilisation ou barbarie*, 1981, p. 12). Also "le retour à l'Égypte dans tous les domaines est la condition nécessaire pour réconcilier les civilisations africaines avec l'histoire" [C as quoted, A4, Malango Kitungano]. Wikipedia reports that he proposed this rebuilding as early as 1948 [C, tertiary, A6].
- **Théophile Obenga** (b. 1936) [C, tertiary, F4]. *African Philosophy: The Pharaonic Period, 2780–330 BC* (Per Ankh, 2004) has chapters "The Intellectual Vocation" (p. 242) and "In Praise of the Intellectual Life" (supplement F) [C, A5, contents only]. Two things are [U]: his reading of Egyptian *rḫ* / *rekhet* as "philosophy", "inquiry into the nature of things" (in his chapter in Wiredu ed., *A Companion to African Philosophy*, 2004, pp. 31–49), and the 1990 French original *La philosophie africaine de la période pharaonique*.
- **Where they belong.** Their statements are twentieth-century African programmes. Diop's is to make Egypt Africa's classical antiquity. They are not Egyptian answers to the three questions, so they belong with the modern lines (§6/§7), with one sentence and a link on the Egypt page. The priority framing ("stolen", "before the Greeks") stays reception, reported with its critics, as the current page does.

## 3. Ethiopia beyond the Hatata

**The Book of the Wise Philosophers** (*Maṣḥafa falāsfā ṭabibān*).
- Origin. It is a collection of sayings that goes back to late Greek "Apophthegmenliteratur", reached Arabic through Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's circle, and was put into Ge'ez by "Michael den Sohn des Abba Michael den Bischof" (colophon) [C, B1, pp. 5–8]. Sumner: Abba Mikael, "a writer of Egyptian origin", completed it "between 1510 and 1522". It was rendered "by the mouth of Mikael": he "orally rendered an Arabic text into current language and an Ethiopian wrote it in Ethiopic" [C, B2, pp. 429–430]. Sumner published it as *Ethiopian Philosophy* vol. I (1974) [C as cited, B2 and B3]. Its form: most paragraphs begin "A wise man said" [C, B2; Cornill's "Es sprach ein Weiser", B1].
- What wisdom is and is for, in the collector's preface (Cornill's German, my renderings) [C, B1, pp. 16–19]:
  - the book "gladdens hearts and gives understanding to the understanding … through the wisdom of the wise and the proclamation of the learned; and it is a light of inquiry and a lamp of insight" ("ein Licht des Forschens und eine Leuchte der Einsicht");
  - "You learn in one month what you would not learn in all your days from the mouth of wise men … sitting before the steps (i.e. the chairs) of instructed men";
  - "the Holy Scripture is a mine of knowledge and wisdom. For the soul is an intellectual and a bodily power."
- How it is done: conversation with books. A sage found alone says he is not alone: "here Galen speaks the truth, and Hippocrates instructs, and Socrates teaches, and Plato speaks, and Aristotle serves as witness … and David teaches, and Paul preaches" [C, B1, p. 14, my rendering]. Sextus's maxim: "know it then, the great wisdom, by which you come to bear the ignorance of fools" [C, B1, p. 20, my rendering].
- **Word test.** The title's *falāsfā* is a loan. The sayings speak of "the wise" (*ṭabib*), so "philosophers" stands for the sages of the collection. Sumner calls it "philosophy in a broad sense", meaning "wisdom", and calls the Hatata "philosophy in a strict sense" ("from wisdom to rationalism") [C, B2, pp. 437–438]. That is Sumner's framing. The page should not take it as its frame.

**The Life and Maxims of Skendes** (Ethiopic Secundus).
- A translation-adaptation of an Arabic account "conceived shortly before the Xth century", itself from a second-century Greek original. The Ethiopic dates to "the first quarter of the XVIth century" [C, B2, pp. 430–431]. Three sections: the life, 55 questions, 108 questions [C, B2]. Sumner, vol. IV (1974) [U, as cited].
- One line bears on "how it is done". Skendes, troubled by a maxim, reads "that the veracity of a statement is in need of a testing" and resolves to "find out that experience is the completion of the truth of any statement". The test ends in his mother's death and his vow of silence; he then answers the emperor in writing [C, B2, p. 431, Sumner's summary and translation]. **Placement note:** it is a Greek tale adapted. If it is used at all, it is one sentence on what Ethiopia chose to translate. Probably discard (see §11).

**Dates.** The Book of the Wise Philosophers, 1510–22 [C, B2]. Skendes, early sixteenth century [C, B2]. The Hatata, 1660s; the current page carries it. Zera Yacob's dates on Wikipedia (1600–1693) are [C, tertiary, F4] and rest on the text's own autobiography; I would not state them bare.

## 4. West African Islamic scholarship

**Ahmad Baba** (1556–1627) [C, F3, p. 59; tertiary, C4].
- His own words, as far as I could reach them: he called his teacher Mohammed Baghayogho "the master of us all and our protector". Diagne cites this as reported by al-Sa'di, *Taʾrīkh al-Sūdān* [C as quoted, F3, p. 59, n. 141].
- His *Tuhfat al-fuḍalāʾ* (French tr. Sami and Zniber, Rabat 1992). Diagne: it "proposes a typically classical argument affirming the superiority of the pursuit of wisdom over all other ends … the conclusion of the work is inspired by Al Ghazâlî's views on its theme, the search for wisdom". Ahmad Baba "quotes" the saying "The Ink of the Scholars is more precious than the blood of the martyrs" and "makes it the heart of his work" [C, F3, Preface and p. 60]. These are Diagne's reading; the saying itself is a hadith Ahmad Baba cites.
- Exile: deported to Morocco after 1591, he spent fourteen years teaching in Marrakech [C, F3, p. 59].
- *Nayl al-ibtihāj* (biographical dictionary, 1596): [U] (search summary only).
- The curriculum (Diagne): logic stayed because it was "considered an instrument (organon) for all the other sciences"; al-Maghīlī's *Rajaz* and al-Akhḍarī's *Sullam* were taught "in verse to facilitate memorizing … as if it were necessary in some way to re-oralize the written in the interests of pedagogy" [C, F3, pp. 58–59]. Diagne prints al-Akhḍarī's dates as "1414–46", which looks like a slip; detail only, so leave the dates off.

**ʿUthmān dan Fodio** (c. 1754–1817) [C, C1; tertiary, C4: 15 Dec 1754 – 20 Apr 1817].
- On teaching women (*Nūr al-albāb*, English via an 1897–98 French version, in Hodgkin): "Most of our educated men leave their wives, their daughters, and their captives morally abandoned, like beasts, without teaching them what God prescribes … How can they thus shut up their wives, their daughters, and their captives, in the darkness of ignorance, while daily they impart knowledge to their students? … to instruct one's wives, daughters, and captives is a positive duty, while to impart knowledge to students is only a work of supererogation" [C, C1, pp. 194–195]. **Word test:** a three-step translation (Arabic → French → English), so the page should say so.
- On knowledge and time (*Tanbīh al-ikhwān*, c. 1811, tr. Palmer): "Every learned man judges according to the knowledge of his age. Conditions change with the times, and the cure changes with the disease" [C, C1, pp. 191–192].
- His brother **ʿAbdullāh dan Fodio** (c. 1766–1828), on how learning was done (*ʿIdāʾ al-nusūkh*, 1812, tr. Hiskett). He lists his shaykhs and what he read with ʿUthmān: theology, syntax, "the knowledge of mysticism which belongs to the forming of good character in oneself and that which belongs to perfecting oneself in science", law, exegesis, hadith. "Many a scholar and many a seeker after knowledge came to us from the East … from the West … Here I collect them for you in verse in order to make the memorizing of them easy" [C, C1, pp. 188–190].
- Muhammad Bello on the Shehu's preaching: "He cared for none of them except that his words should be useful to them" [C, C1, p. 198].
- Diagne: the Sokoto scholars "developed a rich literature in Hausa and Fulfulde" in Arabic script (*ajami*). He ties this to Wiredu's slogan "Let's philosophize in our languages": that project "has a tradition, established by the West African literati whom Ousmane Kane has called 'non-Europhone intellectuals'" [C, F3, p. 60].

**Nana Asma'u** (1793–1864) [C as titled, C2 citing Boyd and Mack 1997; tertiary, C4]. *The Caliph's Sister* (Boyd) is catalogued "1793-1865" [C, archive.org record]. Detail only; use 1864.
- Her Hausa poem *Waʿazi* ("Admonition"), dated AH 1273 (1856–57) per Boyd and Mack [C as reported, C2]. In the Boston University Ajami project's translation (2022): "Obtaining food and seeking after education are valid reasons for leaving your homes. The faith requires that you seek knowledge. Thus, when a woman leaves her home to seek knowledge, she violates no prohibition" [C, C2]. The same poem is "A Warning, II" in Boyd and Mack, *Collected Works* (1997), pp. 242–246 [C as cited, C2]. The two translations differ in emphasis, by the BU editors' own account [C, C2].
- Her women-teacher network (*jaji*, *ʿyan taru*): [U]. Not reached in a source I could open.

**Grouping.** Timbuktu and Sokoto belong on one page. They share Arabic as the medium and the Islamic sciences as the frame (*ʿilm*, logic as instrument, verse for memory), and Sokoto adds Hausa and Fulfulde in Arabic script and the teaching of women. Diagne's book links both to the modern language question (Wiredu). That makes a natural bridge to the "after the debate" page, not a reason to merge them.

## 5. Oral corpora

**Ifá** (Abimbola, *Sixteen Great Poems of Ifá*, UNESCO 1975; the 1976 *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* not opened).
- What it is: "The Yoruba people themselves regard Ifa as the great authority on their mythology, history and philosophy. Indeed, Ifa can be regarded as the unwritten text-book of Yoruba culture" [C, D1, Preface]. "Ifa is recognised by the Yoruba as a repository for Yoruba traditional body of knowledge embracing history, philosophy, medicine and folklore" [C, D1, p. 32]. "Philosophy" here is Abimbola's English.
- Its point: Ifá "was a witness to everyman's choice of destiny. Therefore it is necessary to go to him always to find out the true path of one's destiny" [C, D1, p. 4]. *Orí* (destiny, "head") is the first "highly philosophical concept" Abimbola names [C, D1].
- How it is done. The corpus has 256 *odù*: 16 major (*ojú odù*) and 240 minor. Each has many verses (*ẹsẹ*), long ones being *Ifá ńlá* [C, D1, pp. 29–30]. Training: "a startling example of an indigenous African pre-literate academic system", ten to twenty years before initiation and at least five after, then specialisation as "physicians, midwives and psychiatrists" [C, D1, Preface, pp. 10–11]. "One gets a great poem of Ifa only in return for another great poem of Ifa" [C, D1, p. 30].

**Sophie Oluwole** (1935–2018) [C, tertiary, F4]. *Socrates and Ọ̀rúnmìlà: Two Patron Saints of Classical Philosophy*. First edition Ark, Lagos, 2014 [C, archive.org record]; I read the 3rd edition, 2017 [C, D2, imprint].
- On Ifá as philosophy: "Many Ifa texts are treatises on science, religion, philosophy, mathematics, law, politics, education, poetry, sociology, etc. This means that Ifa oral corpus is the people's philosophy, in its classical conception. In plain language, the Babalawo are traditional Yoruba philosophers. … They are not philosophers in the narrow sense of academic philosophers, which my colleague and I are" [C, D2, Introduction, pp. 22–23].
- Her reply to Wiredu. She quotes Wiredu (1976): "if you want to know the philosophy of the given people, you do not go to aged peasants or fetish priests …" Her answer is that Socrates "left behind no written work" and was "an aged poor Peasant", and "Yet he was acknowledged the best philosopher of his time" [C, D2, Introduction, pp. 23–24].
- Her own working definition: philosophy is "the realm of Study in which attempts are made to characterize Matter and Idea … and how all these determine Truth, Rationality and the type of Knowledge philosophers seek and can communicate through religious, scientific, social, critical and rational expressions" [C, D2, Preface].
- Method: "A comparison of African Philosophy and Western Philosophy is best treated as a dialogue rather than as a debate", and "continual engagement in debates about the existence of African philosophy is an intellectual vice" [C, D2, ch. 6 or Postscript, after p. 144].
- An Ifá verse she cites (her appendix): "Wisdom is like a road that goes in different directions. Therefore, nobody can be its only and absolute custodian. For just as children are wise, so also are adults" [C, D2, ch. 6, c. pp. 135–144]. Whose translation this is (Emanuel's or hers) is [U].
- The "both born around 500 BC" parallel and the twelve likenesses are priority and likeness claims. Leave them out (§11).

**The orality dispute** (as the African participants put it).
- Hountondji: in oral cultures "the mind is too preoccupied with preserving knowledge to find the freedom to criticize it" (*Myth and Reality*, 1996 ed., p. 103) [C as quoted, F3, p. 54].
- Mamoussé Diagne (2006) answers with "the method of dramatization", "a fundamental characteristic of civilizations of orality" [C as quoted, F3, p. 54].
- S. B. Diagne: orality "too involves intertextuality … In this way orality returns on itself, becoming a critical reworking of its own stories" [C, F3, pp. 54–55].

**Sages** (Oruka; already on the page). New here: Oruka's 1972 "Mythologies as African Philosophy" faulted "both Placide Tempels' book *Bantu Philosophy* and John Mbiti's book *African Religions and Philosophy*". Tempels, Mbiti and W. E. Abraham, "by anonymizing everyone", gave "philosophy without philosophers" (Presbey) [C, D7]. Hallen and Sodipo's interlocutors were Yoruba *onísègùn* (herbalist-healers) organised in an *ẹgbẹ́* [C, D7]. Their *ìmọ̀*/*ìgbàgbọ́* analysis (*Knowledge, Belief and Witchcraft*, 1986) is [U].

## 6. Négritude and the nationalist philosophers

Oruka's "nationalist-ideological" trend. Oruka's own sorting is African-made, so a page may use it.

**Léopold Sédar Senghor** (1906–2001) [C, tertiary, E5].
- The 1939 sentence ("Ce que l'homme noir apporte", in *L'Homme de couleur*, 1939; repr. *Liberté I*, 1964). Wording **[U]**: two forms circulate, "L'émotion est nègre, comme la raison hellène" and "… comme la raison est hellène", and the page is cited as both p. 24 and p. 52 of *Liberté I*. It must not go on a page until *Liberté I* is opened.
- His later gloss, in his own words ("Qu'est-ce que la négritude ?", *Études françaises* 3.1, 1967, pp. 3–20):
  - "Là où la raison discursive, la raison-œil du Blanc s'arrête aux apparences de l'objet, la raison intuitive, la raison-étreinte du Nègre, par-delà le visible, va jusqu'à la sous-réalité de l'objet, pour, au-delà du signe, en saisir le sens."
  - "Bien sûr, je simplifie. Il reste que le Blanc européen est, d'abord, discursif; le Négro-Africain, d'abord, intuitif. Il reste que tous les deux sont des hommes de raison, des *homines sapientes*, mais pas de la même manière." [C, E1, pp. 5–6]
  - He credits Césaire with forging the word négritude "dans les années 1932-1934" [C, E1, p. 3].
  - Political method: debate is "d'abord précédés d'un dialogue", aimed at "l'unanimité au sens étymologique du mot" [C, E1].

**Marcien Towa** (1931–2014) [C, tertiary, E5]. *Essai sur la problématique philosophique dans l'Afrique actuelle* (Clé, Yaoundé, 1971) and *Léopold Sédar Senghor : Négritude ou servitude ?* (1971 per Wikipedia; 1976 per Ba) [C, tertiary E5; E4]. I opened neither book.
- On Senghor: Senghor has "fait du Nègre un être si complètement dominé par l'émotion et l'instinct et reconnaît si volontiers l'européanité exclusive de la raison, que l'on peut se demander si son dessein réel est de nier ou de servir l'impérialisme européen" (Towa 1971: 24) [C as quoted, E4].
- The programme: "Notre liberté … passe par l'identification et la maîtrise du principe de la puissance européenne; car si nous ne nous approprions pas ce principe, si nous ne devenons pas puissants comme l'Europe, jamais nous ne pourrons sérieusement secouer le joug de l'impérialisme européen" (1971: 55) [C as quoted, E4]. Also the need "de révolutionner nos cultures de fond en comble pour éviter leur disparition" (1971: 68) [C as quoted, E4].
- **Placement:** Towa is a critic of négritude and of ethnophilosophy, so he sits with Hountondji in the debate, not with the nationalists.

**Kwame Nkrumah** (1909–1972) [C, tertiary, E5]. *Consciencism* (Heinemann 1964; read in the Panaf 1970 printing, page numbers as marked in that text) [C, E2, imprint].
- What philosophy is for: "The statement, elucidation and theoretical defence of such a principle will collectively form a philosophy. Hence philosophy admits of being an instrument of ideology" (p. 56). "Philosophy, too, is one of the subtle instruments of ideology and social cohesion. Indeed, it affords a theoretical basis for the cohesion" (pp. 63–64) [C, E2]. So the brief's phrasing is close but not exact. Use "admits of being an instrument of ideology".
- Against academic philosophy: by the time systems reach the universities "they have lost the vital power which they had at their first statement … This is a result of the academic treatment which they are given". Ordinary Africans "sought knowledge as an instrument of national emancipation and integrity … I was one of this number" (pp. 3–4) [C, E2].
- The name: "philosophical consciencism … will give the theoretical basis for an ideology whose aim shall be to contain the African experience of Islamic and Euro-Christian presence as well as the experience of the traditional African society" (p. 68). It is "the map in intellectual terms of the disposition of forces which will enable African society to digest the Western and the Islamic and the Euro-Christian elements in Africa" (pp. 74–75) [C, E2].
- Wiredu adds that in the United States Nkrumah "wrote an M.A. thesis on ethnophilosophy", not using the word in its later pejorative sense, and later began a doctorate under Ayer [C, D3, Wiredu Introduction, p. 2].

**Julius Nyerere** (1922–1999) [C, tertiary, E5]. "Ujamaa—the Basis of African Socialism" (1962; in *Freedom and Unity*, 1966, and *Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism*, 1968). Socialism is "an attitude of mind" (1967 printing, p. 162). "Africans have no more need of being 'converted' to socialism than we have of being 'taught' democracy. Both are rooted in our past—in the traditional society which produced us" (p. 170) [C as quoted, E3]. *Mwalimu*, "teacher", was his title [U as to source; common knowledge]. Briefly only: his answer to Q2 is political, and he did not write on philosophy as such.

## 7. After the debate

**John S. Mbiti** (1931–2019) [C, tertiary, F4]. *African Religions and Philosophy* (Praeger 1969; Heinemann 1969; read in the Anchor 1970 printing) [C, F1, imprint].
- His use of the word: "we shall use the singular, 'philosophy,' to refer to the philosophical understanding of African peoples concerning different issues of life". "'African philosophy' here refers to the understanding, attitude of mind, logic and perception behind the manner in which African peoples think, act or speak in different situations of life." "What, therefore, is 'African Philosophy,' may not amount to more than simply my own process of philosophizing the items under consideration: but this cannot be helped, and in any case I am by birth an African." "Philosophical systems of different African peoples have not yet been formulated" (p. 2). "While religion can be discerned … philosophy is not so easily distinguishable" (p. 1) [C, F1].
- The sentence, in context: "The individual can only say: 'I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am.' This is a cardinal point in the understanding of the African view of man" (Anchor p. 141; Heinemann pagination differs) [C, F1]. The site's `ubuntu` rendering drops the first comma and the semicolon; check before linking the two.
- **Placement:** by his own definition (a collective "philosophy" behind how peoples think) and by Oruka's critique (§5), Mbiti belongs with the ethnophilosophy debate, as its second major instance after Tempels, not "after the debate".

**Kwame Gyekye** (1939–2019) [C, tertiary, F4]. *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought: The Akan Conceptual Scheme* (CUP 1987; rev. Temple 1995) [C as cited, D7, F2]. The book itself was not opened (401). Three things are **[U]** until it is opened: its case that communal thought is philosophy, against Hountondji; its claim that the communal ideas began with individual, anonymous wise persons; and its ch. 4 on *nyansa*. What I could check in his own words is the proverb and its reading (§1, D3, ch. 12).

**V. Y. Mudimbe** (1941–2025) [C, tertiary, F4]. *The Invention of Africa: Gnosis, Philosophy, and the Order of Knowledge* (Indiana, 1988) [C, archive.org record]. His definition of *gnosis* (from *gnōskō*, "to know": "seeking to know, inquiry, methods of knowing, investigation") is **[U]**. Diagne reports Mudimbe (2008 interview): the book "is precisely based on the question: 'What is Africa? And what do we understand by "Africa" when we use the term?'" [C as quoted, F3, p. 6]. **Word test:** *gnosis* is Mudimbe's chosen Greek term, set beside "philosophy" in the title.

**Mogobe Ramose** (birth year not found; do not infer). *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (Mond, Harare, 1999). The site's `ubuntu` subject already backs *ubu-ntu*. "Ubuntu is the root of African philosophy" is **[U]** (found only in a Portuguese paraphrase). One search summary says the book is organised around three Northern Sotho maxims, including *motho ke motho ka batho* [U]. Metaphilosophy needs only a link to `ubuntu` and one sentence, if the wording is checked.

**Souleymane Bachir Diagne** (b. 1955) [C, tertiary, F4]. *The Ink of the Scholars: Reflections on Philosophy in Africa* (CODESRIA 2016, tr. Adjemian; French *L'encre des savants*, 2013) [C, F3, imprint].
- Why the title: it "is meant to shift the focus from the usual discussion about orality and oral tradition to the consideration of a tradition of written erudition … an integral part of the reflection on philosophy in Africa" (Preface) [C, F3].
- What philosophy needs, stating the critics' case: "A philosophy to which one adheres, literally, without the possibility of questioning it … is no longer a philosophy: such was the substance of the critique of ethno-philosophy … his critics saw precisely the lack of what would constitute philosophy, namely distance" (pp. 21–22) [C, F3].
- On language: "there is nothing more concrete than the word 'abstract' itself … The reason that there are no concrete languages is because there are no abstract ones. All that we find are abstract uses of the words of language" (p. 23) [C, F3].
- On translation, with Berman: "'The essence of translation is to be an opening, a dialogue, a métissage, a decentering. Translation is putting in touch, or it is nothing.'" He reads *Bantu Philosophy* as holding both the "appropriational" and the "ethical" aims of translation (p. 17) [C, F3]. The Berman sentence is Berman's. Diagne's own claim is the double reading of Tempels.
- Kagame: his thesis is dated 1955 here (published 1956). Detail only [C, F3, p. 23].

**Wiredu, new in his own words** (*Person and Community*, Introduction, "The Ghanaian Tradition of Philosophy", 1992) [C, D3]:
- "One of the inescapable duties of contemporary Ghanaian (and generally African) philosophy is to correct them", meaning "crass errors of interpretation and even of description" in Western-made accounts of traditional thought (p. 7).
- "Philosophy is a dialectic, and a dialectic implies the interplay of opposites. Where two or three or more are gathered in a philosophic enterprise, there opposing points of view shall be found." His example is his own disagreement with co-editor Gyekye over Menkiti's view of personhood (p. 10).
- Wiredu (1931–2022) [C, F2b].

## 9. Findings that bear on the grouping

1. **There are African-made sortings of the field.** Oruka's trends (ethnophilosophy, philosophic sagacity, nationalist-ideological, professional) are the field's own classification, as Sima Tan's six schools are for China. The landing page could introduce the lines through them, with the earlier written traditions (Egypt, Ethiopia, the Islamic scholars) set before them. Chimakonam's IEP periodisation is another African-made scheme [C, F2], but it carries its own labels ("Excavators", "Afro-deconstructionists") that the page need not adopt.
2. **Mbiti belongs with the debate, not after it.** His own definition makes "philosophy" the collective outlook behind how peoples think, and Oruka named him with Tempels (§5, §7).
3. **Towa belongs with the critics.** He attacks Senghor and ethnophilosophy together, so he sits beside Hountondji, not with the nationalists (§6).
4. **Diop and Obenga are modern.** Their statements are twentieth-century programmes about Egypt. The Egypt page should tell the instructions by their own texts and point onward (§2).
5. **Timbuktu and Sokoto make one page.** They share Arabic and the Islamic sciences, and Sokoto adds *ajami* and the teaching of women. Diagne links both to the language question (§4).
6. **Oral corpora need their own page.** Ifá, proverbs and sages answer the three questions in their own terms (Ifá: knowledge for finding one's destiny; Akan: wisdom as practical, shared, never finished). The orality dispute (Hountondji, the two Diagnes, Oluwole) is part of that page, told by African participants.
7. **Ethiopia gains a second text.** The Book of the Wise Philosophers is translated and adapted, not composed in Ethiopia. Its preface answers "what is it for" ("a light of inquiry and a lamp of insight"). The Hatata answers with inquiry by reason. Sumner's "from wisdom to rationalism" is his frame; the page can set the two texts side by side without it.
8. **The word test splits the field in two.** Some languages borrow *philosophia* (Ge'ez *falāsfā*, Swahili and Hausa *falsafa*); others have their own words for wisdom and knowledge (Akan *nyansa*, Yoruba *ọgbọ́n*/*ìmọ̀*, Ewe *nunya*, Amharic *ṭǝbäb*). That is a clean opening for the landing page, and it is all checked except *nyansapɛ* and *ìmọ̀ ìjìnlẹ̀*.

**Option A: seven pages after a landing page**, roughly in time order:
