# Metaphilosophy in China, the gaps: research note

Purpose: to fill seven gaps for the China section of the Metaphilosophy subject: the disputers (School of Names), the *fa* tradition (Legalists), Dark Learning, Buddhist doctrinal classification (*panjiao*), Wang Chong, Qing evidential learning, and (optional) the Yin-Yang school. For each it asks the section's three questions (what the activity is, what its point is, how it is done), told by the tradition's own texts and thinkers. Scholars are used for facts only: dates, what a word meant, translations. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified. "My rendering" = mine, from the fetched Chinese text.

**Access.** ctext.org returns a Cloudflare challenge page, so I did not use it. Chinese Wikisource, archive.org, SEP and IEP were reachable. W. K. Liao's *Han Fei Tzu* (1939) is on archive.org but access-restricted (401). The other paywalled translations are listed under "Sources to fetch".

## 1. The disputers (*bianzhe* 辯者), later "School of Names" (*mingjia* 名家)

**The label.**
- The word *mingjia* 名家 first appears in Sima Tan's essay on the six schools, preserved in *Shiji* 130, which lists 陰陽、儒、墨、名、法、道德 [C, S1]. So "School of Names" is a Han label.
- Fraser (SEP): "2nd-century B.C.E. Han dynasty archivists dubbed these thinkers the 'School of Names' … The 'school' is a taxonomical fiction"; "Before the Han dynasty, the social group … was known as the *bian zhe*—'disputers' or 'dialecticians'". "Disputers" is "probably a more appropriate English label" [C, S2]. Fraser's entry says the label may have been coined by "Sima Qian"; the essay is his father Sima Tan's, as Pines's SEP entry says [C, S2, S5]. Detail only; say "Sima Tan".
- The word *bianzhe* is attested in "Tianxia": 天下之辯者相與樂之, "the disputers of the world delighted in it together" (my rendering) [C, S3].
- The *Hanshu* bibliography files seven names under 名家: Deng Xi, Yin Wen, Gongsun Long (14 *pian*), Chenggong Sheng, Huizi (1 *pian*), Huang Gong and Mao Gong [C, S4]. Its account derives the school from the ritual officers and grants it Confucius's 必也正名乎！名不正則言不順, "it must be the rectifying of names! If names are not correct, speech does not flow". "When the carping do it, it is only hooking, splitting and confusion" (及譥者為之，則苟鉤鈲析亂而已; my rendering) [C, S4].
- **Word test.** *Ming* 名 = "names", i.e. words; *shi* 實 = "stuff" or "actualities" (Fraser), "reality" (Dubs, Forke) [C, S2]. "Sophists" for them is a Western comparison (Fraser: "superficial similarities") [C, S2]. Discard it.

**Dates.** Hui Shi "fl. 313 B.C.E."; Gongsun Long "c. 320–250 B.C.E.", a retainer of the Lord of Pingyuan (d. 252 BCE) in Zhao [C, S2].

**Hui Shi, as "Tianxia" reports him** (*Zhuangzi* 33, a Miscellaneous chapter, not by Zhuangzi himself) [C, S3]:
- 惠施多方，其書五車，其道舛駁，其言也不中: "Hui Shi had many methods and his books filled five carts; his way was mixed and confused, his words missed the mark" (my rendering).
- The ten theses open 至大无外，謂之大一；至小无內，謂之小一, "the utmost great has nothing outside it: call it the great one; the utmost small has nothing inside it: call it the small one". They include 今日適越而昔來 ("go to Yue today and arrive yesterday"), and they end 氾愛萬物，天地一體也, "love the myriad things widely; heaven and earth are one body" (my renderings).
- The disputers then answered him with paradoxes of their own (卵有毛, 白狗黑, 一尺之捶，日取其半，萬世不竭 …), "and responded to Hui Shi without end all their lives" (辯者以此與惠施相應，終身无窮) [C, S3].
- The chapter's verdict: 以反人為實，而欲以勝人為名. Fraser: "He took opposing others as the substance of his activity and desired to make a name for himself by defeating others" [C, S2, S3]. 弱於德，强於物, "weak in virtue, strong on things". 逐萬物而不反，是窮響以聲，形與影競走也。悲夫, "he chased the myriad things and did not come back: like silencing an echo by shouting, a body racing its shadow. Sad!" (my rendering) [C, S3].

**Gongsun Long, in his own text.**
- Fraser: of the received *Gongsunlongzi*, "Probably only the 'White Horse,' the essay 'Indicating Things,' and a bit of another dialogue are genuine pre-Han texts"; the introductory chapter is "obviously of relatively late date" [C, S2].
- "White Horse" (白馬論): 馬者，所以命形也；白者，所以命色也；命色者，非命形也。故曰白馬非馬. "'Horse' is what names the shape; 'white' is what names the colour; what names the colour does not name the shape. So I say a white horse is not a horse" (my rendering) [C, S6].
- "Pointing and Things" (指物論) opens 物莫非指，而指非指, "of things, none is not pointed-to; yet the pointing is not pointing" (my rendering; Fraser's title is "Indicating Things"). Fraser: it "appears to be intentionally obscure" [C, S2, S6].
- **What it was for, as the late introduction states it** (跡府, not Gongsun Long's own): 疾名實之散亂 … 欲推是辯以正名實而化天下焉, "pained by the scattering of names and stuff … he wished to extend this disputation to rectify names and stuff and so transform the world" (my rendering) [C, S6]. The same chapter has him refuse Kong Chuan, Confucius's descendant, who would be his pupil if he dropped the white horse: 龍之所以為名者，乃以白馬之論爾。今使龍去之，則無以教, "what I am known for is the white-horse argument; if I drop it, I have nothing to teach". He claims Confucius did the same when he distinguished "a man of Chu" from "a man" [C, S6]. **Headline:** the stated aim is the Confucian one, the rectifying of names (*zhengming*). But the statement is a later compiler's, so attribute it to the text, not to Gongsun Long.
- In "Qiushui" (*Zhuangzi* 17, an Outer chapter) Gongsun Long describes himself. Fung's rendering: "When young I studied the ways of the early kings. When I grew up I understood the practice of human-heartedness and righteousness. I unified the like and the unlike, and separated hard and white. I proved the impossible as possible, and affirmed what others denied. I controverted the wisdom of all the philosophies…" [C, S7 Fung vol. 1; text C, S8]. Word test: "all the philosophies" is the translator's for 百家 (the hundred houses). The chapter answers him with the frog in the well [C, S8].
- Fraser on another reading: "His sophistries may have been intended primarily as a kind of light entertainment" (citing a scholar's view); the exegesis has "no consensus in sight" [C, S2]. That is a scholarly dispute over details; leave it out.

**How others characterised them.**
- **"Tianxia":** 桓團、公孫龍，辯者之徒，飾人之心，易人之意，能勝人之口，不能服人之心，辯者之囿也. Fraser: they "exaggerate others' hearts and change others' intentions. They can defeat others' mouths, but cannot persuade their hearts" [C, S2, S3].
- **Xunzi, "Against the Twelve Masters"** (非十二子) on Hui Shi and Deng Xi: 不法先王，不是禮義，而好治怪說，玩琦辭，甚察而不惠，辯而無用，多事而寡功，不可以為治綱紀；然而其持之有故，其言之成理. "They do not model themselves on the former kings or affirm ritual and right; they like to work at strange doctrines and play with odd phrases; very acute but not kind, disputatious but useless, busy but with little result, they cannot serve as the guideline of order. And yet what they hold has its grounds, and what they say makes sense" (my rendering) [C, S9]. "Dispelling Obsession" (解蔽): 惠子蔽於辭而不知實, "Huizi was blinkered by words and did not know the stuff" (my rendering) [C, S9].
- **Xunzi on disputation itself.** "The gentleman must engage in disputation" [C as quoted, S2]. The disputers' fault is not disputing but disputing to no use.
- **Sima Tan:** 名家使人儉而善失真；然其正名實，不可不察也. His gloss: 名家苛察繳繞，使人不得反其意，專決於名而失人情 … 若夫控名責實，參伍不失，此不可不察也. "The School of Names makes people constrained and apt to lose the genuine, yet its rectifying of names and stuff must be examined … It scrutinises minutely and tangles, so that people cannot get back to their meaning; it decides everything by names and loses people's feelings … but holding stuff to account by its name and cross-checking without error must be examined" (my rendering) [C, S1]. Fraser: "determine things only by names and neglect people's feelings" [C, S2].
- **Guo Xiang** (commentary on "Tianxia"; see §3 for him): before he read the chapter he had taken Zhuangzi for "one of the disputers". Of the paradoxes: 吾意亦謂無經國體致，真所謂無用之談也。然膏粱之子，均之戲豫，或倦於典言，而能辯名析理 … 不猶賢於博奕者乎. Fung's translation: "These bear no relation to the functioning of the state; indeed, they may truly be called useless talk. But since young aristocrats must have some amusement when they are tired with the canonical writings, if they can distinguish names and analyze principles … is it not better than gaming?" [C, S10 text; S7 Fung vol. 2 for the English].

**The shared word with the Mohists.**
- The Mohist "Lesser Selection" defines the activity: 夫辯者，將以明是非之分，審治亂之紀，明同異之處，察名實之理，處利害，決嫌疑焉 [C, S11]. The disputers' word and the Mohists' word are the same *bian*.
- Fraser: "insofar as 'the School of Names' is simply a label for early thinkers interested in language and dialectics, the later Mohists themselves largely fit under this label". Many passages in the Mohist canons rebut the disputers. Gongsun Long cites the Mohist "all-inclusive care" and "may once have numbered among the Mohists" [C, S2].
- The headline for grouping: ***bian* is the activity; Mohists and disputers are two ways of doing it, the Mohists to settle benefit and harm, the disputers (on others' telling) to win.** The critics all ask the same thing: what is disputation for?

## 2. The *fa* tradition ("Legalists", *fajia* 法家)

**The label.**
- Pines (SEP): "Legalism … is a popular—albeit quite inaccurate—designation"; he writes "the *fa* tradition". "The term *fajia* was coined by Sima Qian's father, Sima Tan (d. 110 BCE)"; it was "designated primarily as a bibliographic category"; "no early thinker identified himself as a member of the 'School of *fa*'" [C, S5]. Liu Xiang applied it to ten texts in the imperial library (*Hanshu* 30) [C, S5]. The *Hanshu* list includes 商君二十九篇 and 韓子五十五篇 [C, S4].
- Word test: *fa* 法 is "standard, model, law". "Legalism" and "School of Law" are Western renderings; Waley tried "Realists" [C, S5].
- **Dates:** Shang Yang d. 338 BCE; Shen Buhai d. 337 BCE; Han Fei d. 233 BCE [C, S5]. Han Fei presents himself (*Han Feizi* 43) as synthesiser of Shang Yang and Shen Buhai [C, S5].
- **The Han verdicts.** Sima Tan: 法家嚴而少恩；然其正君臣上下之分，不可改矣. His gloss: they do not distinguish kin from stranger or noble from base, 一斷於法, everything decided by *fa*; "a one-time policy that could not be constantly applied" (Pines's English) [C, S1, S5]. The *Hanshu*: 信賞必罰，以輔禮制, "sure rewards and certain punishments, to support the ritual order"; but "when the harsh do it, they do away with teaching and transformation, discard humaneness and love, and rely on punishment alone to bring about order" (my rendering) [C, S4].

**Shang Yang (*Book of Lord Shang*).**
- **Against antiquity** (ch. 1 更法): 治世不一道，便國不必法古. Duyvendak: "There is more than one way to govern the world and there is no necessity to imitate antiquity, in order to take appropriate measures for the state" [C, S12, S13].
- **The ten things** (ch. 3 農戰): 詩、書、禮、樂、善、修、仁、廉、辯、慧，國有十者，上無使守戰. Duyvendak: "If, in a country, there are the following ten things: odes and history, rites and music, virtue and the cultivation thereof, benevolence and integrity, sophistry and intelligence, then the ruler has no one whom he can employ for defence and warfare" [C, S12, S13]. Pines: "Poems, Documents, rites, music, goodness, self-cultivation, benevolence, uprightness, argumentativeness, cleverness" [C, S5].
  - Word test: 辯 *bian* is "sophistry" in Duyvendak and "argumentativeness" in Pines. It is the same word the Mohists and disputers use for disputation.
- **The six lice** (ch. 13 靳令, 六蝨): rites and music, Odes and Documents, cultivation and goodness, filial and fraternal duty, sincerity and good faith, integrity, humaneness and right, opposing war, being ashamed to fight [C, S12].
- Pines: the "alienating rhetoric … is concentrated only in a few chapters"; "the text assaults not morality as such but rather moralizing discourse", i.e. its bearers, the travelling men of service [C, S5]. The book is composite ("his" book, in Pines's quotation marks) [C, S5]; how composite is [U].

**Han Fei.**
- **"Five Vermin" (五蠹, ch. 49).**
  - 儒以文亂法，俠以武犯禁, "the Ru disorder the law with their learning (*wen*), the knights-errant break the prohibitions with arms" (my rendering) [C, S14].
  - The five vermin are: the "scholars" (學者), who 稱先王之道以籍仁義，盛容服而飾辯說，以疑當世之法, "cite the way of the former kings to borrow humaneness and right, dress grandly and adorn their disputations, so as to cast doubt on the laws of the day"; the speech-makers (言談者); the sword-bearers; those who evade service; and merchants and artisans (my renderings) [C, S14].
  - Times change: 世異則事異, "when the age differs, affairs differ"; ruling today's people by the former kings' government is like the farmer of Song watching the stump for another hare (守株) [C, S14].
  - On talk: 今境内之民皆言治，藏商、管之法者家有之，而國愈貧，言耕者衆，執耒者寡也. "Now everyone in the realm talks about government, and every household keeps the laws of Shang Yang and Guan Zhong, yet the state grows poorer: many talk of ploughing, few hold the plough" (my rendering). 故明主用其力，不聽其言, "so the clear-sighted ruler uses their strength and does not heed their words" [C, S14].
  - The conclusion, 49.13: 故明主之國，無書簡之文，以法爲教；無先王之語，以吏爲師. Pines: "in the state of a clear-sighted sovereign there are no texts written on bamboo strips, but the law is the teaching; there are no 'discourses' of the former kings, but officials are the teachers" [C, S5, S14]. Fung/Bodde: "there is no literature of books and records, but the laws serve as teachings. There are no sayings of the early kings, but the officials act as teachers" [C, S7 vol. 1].
- **"Eminence in Learning" (顯學, ch. 50).**
  - It opens 世之顯學，儒、墨也, "the eminent learnings of the age are the Ru and the Mohists" [C, S15].
  - After Confucius the Ru split into eight and after Mozi the Mohists into three, 取舍相反不同，而皆自謂真孔、墨，孔、墨不可復生，將誰使定世之學乎, "taking and rejecting opposite things, yet each calls itself the true Confucius or Mozi; Confucius and Mozi cannot come back to life, so who is to settle the learning of the age?" Both cite Yao and Shun, three thousand years back (my rendering) [C, S15].
  - The test: 無參驗而必之者，愚也；弗能必而據之者，誣也, "to be certain without cross-checked evidence is stupidity; to rely on what one cannot be certain of is deceit" (my rendering) [C, S15].
  - Pines on 50.4: if the ruler approves a teaching he employs its adherents; if not, "he should dismiss its adherents and cut it off" [C, S5].
- **"Inquiry into Disputation" (問辯, ch. 41).** It is the most direct statement of what speech is for.
  - 辯安生乎？ … 生於上之不明也, "Where does disputation come from? From the ruler's lack of clarity" (my rendering) [C, S16].
  - 夫言行者，以功用爲之的彀者也, "words and deeds take achievement and use as their target" (my rendering). An archer who shoots at random and hits the tip of an autumn hair is not a good archer, because he has no fixed target [C, S16].
  - 堅白、無厚之詞章，而憲令之法息. Fung: "When such phrases as those of the 'hard and the white' and 'no thickness' make their appearance, laws and commands come to an end"; Bodde's note: "Subjects debated on by Kung-sun Lung and Hui Shih respectively" [C, S7 vol. 1; text S16].
  - **Headline: the *fa* texts answer the disputers and the Ru together. Speech is measured by its use to the state, and the teacher is the official.**
- **Li Si, 213 BCE.** The proposal to remove the *Poems*, the *Documents* and the hundred schools' discourses from private hands ends "let them take an official as a teacher!" [C as quoted, S5, citing *Shiji* 87]. Pines: often read as "the victory of 'Legalist' over 'Confucian' ideology, but this is wrong"; what mattered was "state control over intellectual life" [C, S5]. One clause on a page at most.
- **Later echo.** Dai Zhen (§6) calls the Song use of *li* worse than Shen Buhai and Han Fei: 其禍甚於申、韓 [C, S22].

**The three questions, read off the texts.**
- **What learning is:** what the Ru and Mohists do, talk that is "not the people's business" (微妙之言，非民務也, ch. 49) [C, S14].
- **Its point:** none of its own. Words have use as their target.
- **How it is done:** in this tradition, by state law, with officials as teachers.

## 3. Dark Learning (*xuanxue* 玄學)

**The label.**
- Alan Chan (SEP "Neo-Daoism"): "Chinese sources generally identify this development as *Xuanxue*, or 'Learning (*xue*) in the Profound (*xuan*)'". *Xuan* "literally denotes a shade of black with dark red", and by extension what is hidden or mysterious (cf. *Laozi* 1) [C, S17].
- "As a proper term, *Xuanxue* gained currency during the fifth century C.E. It named a branch of learning that formed a part of the curriculum of the imperial academy". It centred on the *Yijing*, *Laozi* and *Zhuangzi*, the "Three … Profound" (*sanxuan*) [C, S17]. In a wider sense it is a retrospective name for third- to sixth-century thought [C, S17].
- Chan: "the label 'Neo-Daoism' is ambiguous"; *xuanxue* "was not a partisan 'Daoist' school" and "not a Daoist movement aimed at dismantling the teachings of Confucius" [C, S17].
- Word test: "Dark Learning", "Learning of the Profound", "Mysterious Learning" and "Neo-Daoism" are all translators'. *Xuanxue* is the period's own term, from the fifth century.
- **Dates:** Wang Bi 226–249; He Yan c. 207–249; Guo Xiang d. 312 [C, S17].
- **What it was for.** Chan: it arose "after the fall of the Han", when intellectuals "interrogated tradition afresh, so as to arrive at a new blueprint for order"; "any blueprint for lasting peace and harmony must rest on a clear understanding of Dao" [C, S17]. As with Sima Tan's schools, the point is order (*zhi*).

**Wang Bi on words, images and meaning** (*Zhouyi lüeli*, "Ming xiang" 明象) [C, S18]:
- 夫象者，出意者也。言者，明象者也。盡意莫若象，盡象莫若言 … 故言者所以明象，得象而忘言。象者所以存意，得意而忘象。猶蹄者所以在兔，得兔而忘蹄；筌者所以在魚，得魚而忘筌也. "Images are what bring out meaning; words are what make the images clear. Nothing exhausts meaning like images, nothing exhausts images like words … So words are for making images clear: once you have the image, forget the words. Images are for holding meaning: once you have the meaning, forget the image. As the snare is for the hare, and once you have the hare you forget the snare; as the fish trap is for the fish, and once you have the fish you forget the trap" (my rendering).
- Then: 是故存言者，非得象者也；存象者，非得意者也 … 得意在忘象，得象在忘言, "so whoever holds on to the words has not got the image; whoever holds on to the image has not got the meaning … getting the meaning lies in forgetting the image, getting the image in forgetting the words" (my rendering).
- The target is the Han image-and-number exegesis of the *Changes*: 義苟在健，何必馬乎？類苟在順，何必牛乎, "if the meaning is strength, why must it be a horse? if the category is compliance, why must it be an ox?" 互體不足，遂及卦變，變又不足，推致五行。一失其原，巧愈彌甚, "when the interlocking trigrams were not enough they went on to hexagram changes; when the changes were not enough they pushed on to the five phases; once the origin is lost, the cleverer the worse" (my rendering) [C, S18].
  - The Wikisource page also prints a paragraph that reads as a later commentary on the first; quote only the paragraphs above. Which paragraphs are Wang Bi's needs a check against an edition [U].
- **The source he draws on.** *Zhuangzi* 26 "Waiwu": 荃者所以在魚，得魚而忘荃。蹄者所以在兔，得兔而忘蹄。言者所以在意，得意而忘言。吾安得夫忘言之人而與之言哉 [C, S19]. The `zhuangzi` page carries this.
  - Wang Bi inserts a middle term, the image (*xiang*). His text cites the *Changes* commentary's 立象以盡意, "set up images to exhaust meaning" [C, S18].
- **The debate around it.** Chan: the question goes back to the *Yijing*, "where Confucius is made to ask whether words can fully disclose meaning". Ouyang Jian held that words exhaust meaning (*yan jin yi*). Xun Can (c. 212–240) called the classics the "chaff" of the sages' learning. Wang Bi "supplies a fuller and more nuanced argument": "the means of interpretation must not be confused with the end itself" [C, S17].

**The sage and nothingness** (*Shishuo xinyu* 4.8; also *Sanguozhi* 28, commentary) [C, S20; S17 for the locators].
- Pei Hui asks: 夫無者，誠萬物之所資，聖人莫肯致言，而老子申之無已，何邪, "nothingness is truly what the myriad things depend on, yet the sage [Confucius] would not speak of it, while Laozi expounds it endlessly. Why?"
- Wang Bi: 聖人體無，無又不可以訓，故言必及有；老、莊未免於有，恆訓其所不足, "the sage embodies nothingness, and nothingness cannot be taught, so his words necessarily concern what is; Laozi and Zhuangzi had not got free of what is, so they were forever teaching what they lacked" (my renderings).
- **Headline for the "what is it" question:** the highest understanding is embodied, not said. Confucius ranks above Laozi because he did not talk about it.

**Guo Xiang, briefly.**
- He was "by far the most important interpreter of the *Zhuangzi* in Chinese history", the redactor of the 33-chapter text. He was accused, as early as the fifth century, of taking over Xiang Xiu's commentary; "recent scholarship generally agrees that Guo had drawn his own conclusions" (Chan) [C, S17]. The plagiarism charge is a detail; at most a clause.
- **His preface** (南華真經序, under his name) [C, S10]: 夫莊子者，可謂知本矣 … 此其所以不經而為百家之冠也。然莊生雖未體之，言則至矣。通天地之統，序萬物之性，達死生之變，而明內聖外王之道. "Master Zhuang may be said to know the root … this is why, though not a classic, his book is the crown of the hundred schools. Yet although Master Zhuang did not embody it, his words reach the utmost: they comprehend the order of heaven and earth, set out the natures of the myriad things, reach through the changes of life and death, and make clear the way of sageliness within and kingliness without" (my rendering).
  - Whether the preface is Guo Xiang's own is questioned by some scholars [U].
  - **Headline:** the same ranking as Wang Bi's (knowing the root against embodying it), and a second locus, after "Tianxia", for *neisheng waiwang* (sageliness within, kingliness without).
- His note on the disputers ("better than gaming") is in §1.

## 4. Doctrinal classification (*panjiao* 判教) and Xuanzang

**What it is and why.**
- Nicholaos Jones (SEP "Huayan"): Indian scriptures "arrived in China piecemeal", so "early Chinese adherents … did not perceive contributions from different Indian traditions as fundamentally inconsistent". This "led to the practice of (Ch.) *panjiao* (doctrinal classification), according to which different philosophical systems are treated not as exclusive, but as partial conceptions of the truth, which can be arranged hierarchically in terms of how complete (literally, 'rounded,' Ch. *yuan*) their grasp of the truth is" [C, S21].
- In the texts' own terms: Chegwan (§ Tiantai, below) calls the object 東流一代聖教, "the whole of the sage's teaching that flowed east" [C, S23]. Fazang's first-listed predecessor gives the rationale from the *Vimalakīrti*: 佛以一音演說法，眾生隨類各得解, "the Buddha expounds the Dharma with one sound; beings each understand it according to their kind" [C, S24].

**Fazang (643–712), Huayan** [dates C, S21, S25].
- He surveys the classifications before him: 古今諸賢所立教門差別非一。且略敘十家以為龜鏡, "the teachings set up by the worthies of past and present differ, not just one; I briefly set out ten of them as a mirror" (my rendering) [C, S24]. The ten begin with Bodhiruci's "one-sound teaching" [C, S24]. **Headline: *panjiao* is a genre with many competing schemes, and the classifiers knew it.**
- His own scheme (*Huayan wujiao zhang*, T45 no. 1866): 聖教萬差，要唯有五：一小乘教，二大乘始教，三終教，四頓教，五圓教. "The sage's teachings differ in ten thousand ways, but in essence there are only five: the Lesser Vehicle, the initial Great Vehicle, the final [Great Vehicle], the sudden, and the round [or perfect] teaching" (my rendering) [C, S24].
- Jones: the fifth, the Round Teaching, is "his own teaching" [C, S21]. Rothschild (IEP) presents Fazang as a court ideologue for Wu Zhao and calls him "a propagandist"; the classification placed "Flower Garland at the apex" [C, S25]. That is Rothschild's judgement, to be attributed if used. **Headline:** a classification also ranks the classifier's own school first.

**Zhiyi (538–597), Tiantai** [dates C, S26].
- Ziporyn (SEP "Tiantai"): the four teachings by content (化法四教) are Tripitaka, Shared, Separate and Perfect (藏通別圓). They are "not … four different teachings, but … increasingly subtle explications of one and the same content". For Tiantai the question about a doctrine is not "is it true?" but "in what sense, and in what context, is it true, i.e., liberating?" [C, S26].
- **The "five periods and eight teachings" formula** (五時八教) is stated, and credited to Zhiyi, in the Korean monk Chegwan's *Tiantai sijiao yi* (天台四教儀): 天台智者大師以五時八教判釋東流一代聖教，罄無不盡, "the Great Master Zhizhe of Tiantai, with the five periods and eight teachings, classified and explained the whole of the sage's teaching that flowed east, leaving nothing out" (my rendering) [C, S23].
  - The five periods, also called the five flavours, are the *Huayan*, the Deer Park (the Āgamas), the Vaipulya, the Prajñā, and the Lotus and Nirvāṇa periods.
  - The eight teachings are four modes (頓漸秘密不定: sudden, gradual, secret, indeterminate), "like a physician's prescription" (如世藥方), and four contents (藏通別圓), "like distinguishing the medicine's flavours" (如辨藥味) [C, S23].
- Whether the fixed five-period chronology is Zhiyi's own or a later systematisation (Zhanran, Chegwan) is [U]. A search summary points that way; I could not open a scholarly source. It only matters if a page states the formula as Zhiyi's words. Safe wording: "the Tiantai scheme, later summed up as five periods and eight teachings". Chegwan's dates are [U] (10th century).

**Zongmi (780–841), *Inquiry into the Origin of Humanity* (*Yuanren lun* 原人論, T45 no. 1886)** [dates C, S21; text C, S27; Gregory's 1995 translation not opened, see Sources to fetch]. All renderings mine.
- **The point: to know where one comes from.** 萬靈蠢蠢皆有其本，萬物芸芸各歸其根 … 今我稟得人身而不自知所從來，曷能知他世所趣乎？曷能知天下古今之人事乎？故數十年中學無常師，博攷內外以原自身，原之不已，果得其本. "All living things have their root; the myriad things each return to their source … I have received a human body without knowing where it came from: how could I know where it goes in another life, or the human affairs of the world, past and present? So for decades I studied with no fixed teacher, searching widely in inner and outer [Buddhist and non-Buddhist] teachings to trace my own origin; I traced it without stopping, and in the end found its root."
- **The ranking of the three teachings.**
  - 然孔、老、釋迦皆是至聖，隨時應物，設教殊塗。內外相資，共利群庶, "Confucius, Laozi and Śākyamuni were all perfect sages; following the times and responding to beings, they set up teachings by different roads. Inner and outer teachings support each other and together benefit the people."
  - 雖皆聖意而有實有權，二教唯權，佛兼權實, "though all are the sages' intent, there is the real and the provisional: the two teachings are provisional only; Buddhism has both."
  - 策萬行，懲惡勸善，同歸於治，則三教皆可遵行；推萬法，窮理盡性，至於本源，則佛教方為決了, "for urging the myriad practices, punishing evil and encouraging good, all returning together to order (*zhi*), all three teachings may be followed. For investigating the myriad dharmas, exhausting pattern and fully realising nature, and reaching the root source, Buddhism alone is conclusive."
- 然當今學士各執一宗, "but scholars today each cling to one school" [C, S27].
- **How it is done.** The method has two directions. 初從淺至深 … 斥滯令通而極其本, "first from shallow to deep … refuting what is stuck so that it opens through, to reach the root". Then 後依了教 … 會偏令圓而至於末, "following the complete teaching … gathering the partial so that it becomes round, down to the branches" [C, S27].
  - The four chapters: refuting the delusions (of Confucians and Daoists); refuting the partial and shallow (Buddhists of the incomplete teachings); directly revealing the true source; and harmonising root and branch, 會前所斥，同歸一源，皆為正義, "gathering what was refuted before: one source, and all of it correct meaning" [C, S27].
  - Five Buddhist grades sit inside it: 人天教、小乘教、大乘法相教、大乘破相教、一乘顯性教. The lowest is matched to "the world's teaching of the five constants": 不殺是仁；不盜是義 …, "not killing is humaneness, not stealing is right…" [C, S27].
- Jones: "The central innovation is its inclusion of Daoism and Confucianism"; the title "might be a play on" Han Yu's anti-Buddhist essay *Yuan Dao* [C, S21]. Chengguan, before him, had already criticised the Ru and the Daoists [C, S21].
- **Headline:** Zongmi's "returning together to order" echoes Sima Tan's "one destination by different roads … all work at bringing about order" (*Shiji* 130: 同歸而殊涂 … 此務為治者也) [C, S1]. The echo is my observation, not Zongmi's claim. Keep it as juxtaposition on the page or drop it.

**Xuanzang (602–664): the other answer to disagreement.** Huili's biography (*Da Tang Da Ci'ensi Sanzang fashi zhuan*, juan 1): 法師既遍謁眾師 … 詳考其義，各擅宗途，驗之聖典，亦隱顯有異，莫知適從，乃誓遊西方以問所惑，並取《十七地論》以釋眾疑，即今之《瑜伽師地論》也. "Having visited all the masters … and examined their meanings, [he found that] each held to his own school's road, and checked against the scriptures these too differed, some explicit and some hidden; not knowing whom to follow, he vowed to travel west to ask about his doubts and to bring back the *Treatise on the Seventeen Stages* to resolve everyone's doubts, the present *Yogācārabhūmi*" (my rendering) [C, S28]. **Headline:** the classifiers rank the teachings; Xuanzang goes for the better text. One or two lines; the site's translation-theory and Yogācāra pages already carry his translating.

## 5. Wang Chong (27–c. 100 CE), *Lunheng* 論衡

**Dates.** McLeod (IEP): born "in the year 27 C.E. (according to his autobiography)"; died "around 100 C.E." The IEP heading gives 25–100; Zufferey's monograph title has "27-97?" [C, S29]. Use **27–c. 100**; "c. 97" is one estimate.

**Its point: hatred of falsehood.**
- The statement is in ch. 61 **佚文** (Forke's "Lost Texts"), not ch. 84 [C, S30, S31 vol. 2]: 《詩》三百，一言以蔽之，曰：思無邪。《論衡》篇以十數，亦一言也，曰：疾虛妄. Forke: "[Should one sentence express the purport of all the 300 Odes of the Shiking it would be:— 'Do not harbour wicked thoughts,'] and for ten and more chapters of the Lun-hêng one device might be chosen, viz. 'Hate fictions and falsehoods.'" The first half quotes *Analects* 2.2 (Forke's note) [C, S31 vol. 2].
  - Word test: *ji xuwang* 疾虛妄 is literally "hate the empty and the false". "Fictions" is Forke's.
- Ch. 84 **對作** (Forke: "Replies in Self-Defense"): 是故《論衡》之造也，起眾書並失實，虛妄之言勝真美也 … 故《論衡》者，所以銓輕重之言，立真偽之平. Forke: "In a great many books reality has no place left: falsehood and immorality triumph over truth and virtue … For this reason the Lun-heng weighs the words, whether they be light or heavy, and holds up a balance for truth and falsehood" [C, S30, S31 vol. 1]. *Heng* 衡 in the title is the balance.
- Also in ch. 84: 冀悟迷惑之心，使知虛實之分. Forke: "I fervently desire to rouse the misguided minds, and to teach them, how to tell the full from the hollow". And 不得已，故為《論衡》, "there was no help, therefore I wrote the Lun-heng" [C, S30, S31 vol. 1]. *Bu de yi* 不得已 is Mencius's word for why he disputes: 予豈好辯哉？予不得已也 (3B9) [C, S32].
- Writing answers a need. Ch. 84 ties each book to its occasion: the *Spring and Autumn* because Zhou declined, Mencius because Yang and Mo confused the Ru, Han Fei because Han was weak. 賢聖之興文也，起事不空為，因因不妄作, "when worthies and sages produce writing, it is never done for nothing; there is always a cause" (my rendering) [C, S30].

**What it is: neither making nor transmitting, but discussing (*lun* 論).** Ch. 84 [C, S30]:
- 或曰：「聖人作，賢者述。以賢而作者，非也。」 … 〔非〕作也，亦非述也，論也。論者，述之次也. Forke: "Some say that the sages create, whereas the worthies relate, and that, if worthies create, it is wrong … These works are neither creations nor relations" [C, S31 vol. 1].
- The three tiers: the Five Classics are making (*zuo*); Sima Qian's history, Liu Xiang's *Xinxu* and Ban Biao's records are transmitting (*shu*); Huan Tan's *Xinlun* and Zou Boqi's are discussing (*lun*), "and discussing ranks after transmitting".
- Making is 造端更為，前始未有, "to produce something new that did not exist in the past" (Forke): Cang Jie inventing writing, Xi Zhong the cart [C, S30, S31].
- **Headline:** a third term after Confucius's 述而不作, "transmitting, not making" (*Analects* 7.1) [C, S33]. McLeod: works are to be judged "not on their faithfulness … to the tradition … but instead on their truthfulness, usefulness, and correspondence to reality" [C, S29].

**How it is done: evidence (*xiaoyan* 效驗) and the heart-mind.**
- Ch. 79 **知實**: 凡論事者，違實不引效驗，則雖甘義繁說，眾不見信. Forke: "Whenever people in their discussions depart from truth and do not bear out their propositions by evidence, their arguments may be never so pleasing, and their reasons never so abundant, yet nobody believes them" [C, S30, S31 vol. 2].
- Ch. 67 **薄葬** (Forke: "Simplicity of Funerals"): 事莫明於有效，論莫定於有証, "no matter is clearer than one with proof, no argument more settled than one with evidence" (my rendering) [C, S30].
- Evidence is not enough, and the same chapter takes the Mohists as its example: 墨議不以心而原物，苟信聞見，則雖效驗章明，猶為失實. "The Mohists do not use the heart-mind (*xin*) to trace things to their origin; they simply trust what is heard and seen, so even with plain evidence they still miss the reality" (my rendering) [C, S30]. **Headline:** Wang Chong's test answers the Mohist second standard (the eyes and ears of the people) directly.
- McLeod: his method subjects existing teachings to "questioning" (*wen*) and "challenging" (*nan*) [C, S29]. Ch. 28 is 問孔, Forke's "Criticisms on Confucius" [C, S31 vol. 1 table].

## 6. Qing evidential learning (*kaozheng* 考證) and Han against Song learning

**Labels and dates.**
- "Evidential learning" is *kaozhengxue* 考證學 [C, S22]. The Qing movement called itself Han Learning (*Hanxue* 漢學) and "applied the term, Sung Learning (*Sung hsüeh*), to the Neo-Confucianism of the Sung and Ming dynasties … to distinguish it from their own self-termed Han Learning" (Fung, *History* vol. 2) [C, S7].
- Fung quotes Ruan Yuan (1764–1849), preface to Jiang Fan's (1761–1831) *Guochao Hanxue shicheng ji*: Han classical learning must be followed because "it was extremely close in time to the sages and worthies … whereas the doctrines of the two other schools (Buddhism and religious Taoism) had not yet appeared" [C as quoted, S7].
- Dates: Gu Yanwu 1613–1682; Dai Zhen 1724–1777; Zhang Xuecheng 1738–1801 [C, S22]. Fung gives Dai 1723–77 [C, S7]; the difference is a reckoning detail.
- Gu Yanwu's slogan 經學即理學, "the proper study of patterns (*li*) is the study of the classics" [C as quoted, S22, citing Yu Yingshi]. Its primary locus is [U].

**Dai Zhen: from characters to words to the Way.**
- Letter to Shi Zhongming (與是仲明論學書, *Dai Dongyuan ji* juan 9): 經之至者道也，所以明道者其詞也，所以成詞者字也。由字以通其詞，由詞以通其道，必有漸. "What the classics reach to is the Way; what makes the Way clear is their words; what makes the words is the characters. Through the characters one gets through to the words, through the words to the Way; it must go step by step" (my rendering) [C, S34].
- The same letter then lists what that takes. The *Shuowen*; the commentaries on the Thirteen Classics, since "the sense of one character must run through all the classics"; astronomy for the "Canon of Yao"; ancient phonology for the *Odes*; ancient buildings and dress for the *Rites*; historical geography for the "Tribute of Yu"; mathematics for the "Kaogong ji"; the names of birds, beasts, insects and plants for the *Odes*' figures [C, S34]. **Headline: the method needs the sciences.**
- Letter to Duan Yucai: "without researching the import of words, the structure of institutions, and the terminology for various things there is no way to comprehend the language [of these texts]". And 宋儒譏訓詁之學，輕語言文字，是欲渡江而棄舟楫，欲登高而無階梯也. Chong and Fraser's translation: the Song scholars dismissing philology is "akin to wanting to cross a river without using a boat and paddle or to climbing a height without using a ladder" [C as quoted, S22].

**Han against Song in Dai's own voice: he takes neither side.**
- Letter to Fang Xiyuan (與方希原書, dated 乙亥, 1755, same juan): 古今學問之途，其大致有三：或事於理義，或事於制數，或事於文章. "The roads of learning, past and present, are broadly three: working at pattern and meaning (*liyi*), at institutions and numbers (*zhishu*), or at literary writing (*wenzhang*)" (my rendering) [C, S34].
- 聖人之道在六經。漢儒得其制數，失其義理；宋儒得其義理，失其制數. "The Way of the sages is in the Six Classics. The Han Ru got their institutions and numbers but lost their meaning and pattern; the Song Ru got their meaning and pattern but lost their institutions and numbers." His image: one man on the summit of Mount Tai can speak of mountains, one on the shore of the northern sea can speak of water, and neither takes in the whole (my rendering) [C, S34].
- Word test: Chong and Fraser render Dai's *yili* side as "philosophy" ("evidential learning is the means to discover the dao"; "Research in the first four areas serves as the foundation for work in the fifth (philosophy)") [C, S22]. "Philosophy" there is the translators' word; Dai's is 理義 / 義理.
- Chong and Fraser: his contemporaries took evidential learning as "solid learning (*shixue*)" and philosophy as "empty" (*xu*). For Dai, evidential learning was "necessary but not sufficient"; for his contemporaries it was "both … necessary and sufficient" [C, S22]. After the 1755 letter this is the headline Han/Song split.
- Feng Youlan (*Short History*, 1948, ch. 27) called the dispute one "between the philosophical and scholarly interpretation of the ancient texts" [U this round; not re-opened].

**Dai Zhen against the Song *li*** (*Mengzi ziyi shuzheng* 孟子字義疏證, completed 1777) [C, S22 for the date; text C, S35].
- **Method: define the word from its oldest uses.** 理者，察之而幾微必區以別之名也，是故謂之分理, "*li* is the name for what, examined, must be distinguished down to the finest point; that is why it is called the pattern of divisions (*fenli*)". He backs this with Zheng Xuan's gloss 理，分也 and Xu Shen's *Shuowen* preface. 古人所謂理，未有如後儒之所謂理者矣, "what the ancients called *li* was never what later Ru call *li*" (my renderings).
- 理也者，情之不爽失也；未有情不得而理得者也, "*li* is feelings without error; there has never been a case of getting *li* without getting the feelings" (my rendering).
- **The point: who suffers from the word.** 尊者以理責卑，長者以理責幼，貴者以理責賤，雖失，謂之順；卑者、幼者、賤者以理爭之，雖得，謂之逆 … 人死於法，猶有憐之者；死於理，其誰憐之！ … 其禍甚於申、韓如是也. "The honoured reproach the lowly with *li*, the old the young, the noble the base, and even when wrong they are called compliant; when the lowly, the young and the base argue with *li*, even when right they are called rebellious … When a man dies by the law, there are still those who pity him; when he dies by *li*, who will pity him? … The harm is worse than Shen Buhai's and Han Fei's" (my rendering) [C, S35]. It is in the section on *li* in juan 1; the section's number is [U].
- **Why he writes:** the preface takes Mencius's 3B9 stance. Mencius 不能已於與辯, "could not stop himself from disputing"; 辯惡可已哉, "how could disputation stop?"; 吾知之而不言，是不忠也, "to know it and not say it would be disloyal" [C, S35].

**Zhang Xuecheng: the Six Classics are all history.**
- *Wenshi tongyi*, "Yi jiao I" (易教上), opening: 六經皆史也。古人不著書，古人未嘗離事而言理，六經皆先王之政典也. "The Six Classics are all history. The ancients did not write books; the ancients never spoke of pattern (*li*) apart from affairs; the Six Classics are all the governing records of the former kings" (my rendering) [C, S36].
- Chong and Fraser: Zhang was "among the few who were sympathetic to and influenced by Dai's philosophical thought" [C, S22].
- **Headline:** Dai goes from words to the Way; Zhang puts the Way in the records of affairs. Both turn away from *li* spoken on its own. Zhang belongs in one paragraph on a Qing page or stage, not more.

## 7. The Yin-Yang school (optional)

- **Sima Tan:** 陰陽之術，大祥而眾忌諱，使人拘而多所畏；然其序四時之大順，不可失也, "the art of yin and yang dwells on omens and multiplies taboos, making people constrained and fearful; yet its ordering of the great sequence of the four seasons must not be lost" (my rendering). His gloss on the rules for each season: 未必然也, "not necessarily so" [C, S1].
- ***Hanshu*:** the school descends from the Xi-He astronomers, "reverently following august heaven, calculating and representing the sun, moon and stars". "When the narrow do it, they are tied to taboos, stuck on petty techniques, and leave human affairs to rely on ghosts and spirits" (my rendering). It lists 鄒子四十九篇 and 鄒子終始五十六篇 [C, S4].
- **Zou Yan** (*Shiji* 74). Fung's rendering: he saw the rulers "becoming ever more dissolute"; "Thereupon he examined deeply into the phenomena of increase and decrease of the yin and the yang … He had first to examine small objects, and extended this to large ones until he reached what was without limit" (必先驗小物，推而大之，至於無垠) [C, S7 vol. 1; text S37]. The *Shiji* adds 然要其歸，必止乎仁義節儉，君臣上下六親之施，始也濫耳, "yet in the end it always came to rest in humaneness, right and frugality and the relations of ruler, minister and kin; only the beginning was extravagant" (my rendering) [C, S37].
- Zou Yan's dates [U] (commonly c. 305–240 BCE). No writings survive [U]. **Headline only:** a method (from the small and verified to the boundless) and a point (to call rulers back to virtue). A landing-page or Sima Tan clause; no page.

## 8. Findings that bear on the grouping

1. ***Bian* runs through four groups.** The Mohists define it; the disputers practise it; Xunzi requires it of the gentleman but condemns useless disputing; the *fa* texts say it arises only when the ruler is unclear. Shang Yang lists 辯 among the ten things that ruin a state. Mencius and Dai Zhen dispute because they "cannot help it". The thread argues for putting the disputers with the Mohists, and gives the Legalists a natural place as the party that refuses disputation altogether.
2. **Every Han and later label in this round is a library or retrospective label:** *mingjia*, *fajia* (Sima Tan); *xuanxue* (fifth-century curriculum); "Neo-Daoism", "Legalism", "School of Names" (Western). The landing page already explains *jia*, so the school pages can say "filed by Sima Tan as…" in a clause.
3. **"Order" (*zhi*) as the shared point recurs:** in Sima Tan; in Han Feizi ("use as the target"); in Dark Learning ("a new blueprint for order", Chan); in Zongmi ("all returning together to order").
4. **Ranking by embodiment:** Wang Bi (the sage embodies nothingness, Laozi only talks about it) and Guo Xiang (Zhuangzi "knew the root" but "did not embody it") rank Confucius above the Daoist writers. The Dark Learning material sits on both sides of the Confucian–Daoist line, as Chan says.
5. ***Panjiao* is China sorting its own schools again,** now across a translated canon and then across all three teachings (Zongmi). It fits beside "Tianxia", Sima Tan and Liu Xin on the landing page as much as on a Buddhism page.
6. **Transmission and commentary:** Wang Chong's three tiers (making, transmitting, discussing), Wang Bi's "forget the words", Dai Zhen's "from characters to the Way" and Zhang's "the Six Classics are all history" are four answers to "a transmitter and not a maker". All four bear on the landing page's theme of transmission.
7. **Wang Chong and Dai Zhen both test** (*xiaoyan*; *kaozheng*). Both stand inside the Ru tradition, criticising its received readings.

- (b) With the Daoists. Against: Chan says it was "not a partisan 'Daoist' school", and Wang Bi ranks Confucius above Laozi.
- (c) As a bridge stage before Buddhism in China, since *xuanxue* vocabulary carried the early Buddhist translation. That claim is [U] and not researched here.

***Panjiao*.**
- (a) **On "Buddhism in China"**, with Chan. Zhiyi, Fazang and Zongmi as the sorting of the teachings; Xuanzang's journey as the other answer. *Proposed.*
- (b) Zongmi's three-teachings ranking also as a line on the landing page's "China sorting its own schools".

**Wang Chong.**
- (a) **With the Confucians**, as the Han stage that "discusses" rather than transmits. The page would then run Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi, Wang Chong.
- (b) **Landing page**, under transmission and commentary (the three tiers).
- (c) Own short page. Probably too thin for the brief.
- No strong preference. (a) keeps him with the tradition he criticises from within.

**Qing evidential learning.**

- (b) Own page, "Evidential learning". Possible if the Learning of the Way page grows too long.

**Yin-Yang school.** Landing page clause, with Sima Tan's six. No page.

**Person pages.** For discussion only; external links serve the brief. Possible importance cases:

## 12. Discard list (do not place)

- "Sophists" for the disputers and "like the Greek sophists" (comparison).
- "Totalitarians" for the *fa* thinkers (Creel and others, reported by Pines). It is a reception verdict, and Pines rejects it.
- "Realists" / Morgenthau's political realism for *fajia* (Western framing, discussed by Pines).
- Jones's "inference to the best explanation" for *panjiao*, and his Anaximander comparison for *qi* (Western analogies).
- "Proto-Zen" for Fazang's sudden teaching (Rothschild's gloss).
- The plagiarism charge against Guo Xiang beyond one clause.
- The scholarly dispute over what theory the "White Horse" assumes, and the "court entertainer" reading of Gongsun Long.
- Fazang's rain-making and the Wu Zhao coup (biography, not metaphilosophy).
- Liu Xin's derivation of the Names and *fa* schools from offices of state (detail).
- The Huiyuan–Chengguan argument over the sudden teaching (detail).
- The 21 disputers' paradoxes beyond one or two examples.
- Li Si's book-burning beyond a clause.
- The 1723/1724 dating difference for Dai Zhen.
