## 1. Sapolsky's standing

- **[A]** Robert Sapolsky is very important, more narrowly for the human biology approach.
- **[C, checked via OpenAlex 2026-10-13]** About 560 works, about 64,600 citations, h-index 120 — top tier for a working biologist.
- **[C, unchecked]** Stanford professor of biology and neurology; MacArthur Fellowship (1987). Research reputation: stress (glucocorticoids and the hippocampus, chronic stress and health); decades of baboon fieldwork in Kenya — rank shaping physiology, and the Forest Troop, where a culture outlasted its members.
- **[C, unchecked]** Public reach: *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (1994), *Behave* (2017), the Stanford course *Human Behavioral Biology* on YouTube, watched by millions.
- **[C, unchecked]** *Determined* (2023), the case against free will, reached a wide audience and drew strong criticism: compatibilists in Dennett's line say he attacks a libertarian free will few philosophers defend; Kevin Mitchell's *Free Agents* (2023) argues for genuine agency from biology itself.
- **[C]** So: the multi-layer method — no layer sufficient alone — is broadly accepted; the free-will conclusion is his own, and debated.

## 2. Which free will — "confusion confusion"

- **[A]** It is telling that they attack him for attacking the wrong free will — confusion, confusion.
- **[C]** The fight is about which free will: Sapolsky defines it as an uncaused cause and shows biology leaves no room for it; compatibilists define it as acting from one's own reasons without coercion, which survives every prior cause; everyday use means the experience of choosing. Each side defines the target so the other's shots miss. One word, many meanings, each cast into a local definition — and the local definitions fight as if they were rival answers to one question.
- **[C]** Read from experience and cognition rather than head-on: the experience of choosing is real as experience, had at the subject's seat; purpose and choice live at the agent level (in a complex adaptive system: the landscape and the agents). Sapolsky dissolves the agent level into the layers below; the libertarian lifts it above everything. Both lose the level itself — and both share the privileged-individual assumption that a real self would have to stand outside causation.

## 3. The strings we don't see

- **[A]** Free will is often the belief one has taken a decision without strings attached, and less that there are no strings.
- **[C, unchecked]** Spinoza says nearly the same in the appendix to Part I of the *Ethics* — people think themselves free because they are conscious of their actions and ignorant of the causes that determine them — and, in a letter, a falling stone that, if conscious, would think it fell of its own will.
- **[C]** A decision experienced from inside comes without the context of its causes; the strings are the part of the context that is missing from the experience. The sense of freedom is then neither an illusion to debunk nor a power to defend, but a feature of the seat: from inside, one sees the choosing, not the strings. The debate circles because one side shows the strings exist, the other says they don't matter, and neither speaks of the experience of not seeing them.

## 4. Punishment and harm

- **[A]** Many need free will to justify punishment of the individual, rather than take an approach of minimising harm by managing individuals. Sapolsky is not a proponent of letting all criminals loose.
- **[C]** Retributive punishment — because they deserve it — needs the uncaused self; take it away and desert loses its ground, while the need to protect people is untouched.
- **[C, unchecked]** Sapolsky's image (*Behave* / *Determined*): a car whose brakes have failed is taken off the road because it is dangerous, not because it is wicked. Close to the public-health or quarantine model of Derk Pereboom and Gregg Caruso: restrain as far as needed to prevent harm, without the retributive surplus.
- **[A]** There are a lot of examples: happy to spend money on prisons, but not on treating obvious mental illness at the same time. Taking a symptom out of action for a limited time instead of spending effort on addressing causes.
- **[C, unchecked]** Figures to check: the largest US jails (Cook County, Los Angeles County) often called the country's largest psychiatric institutions; high shares of prisoners with serious mental illness; rates of brain injury and childhood trauma far above the general population; reoffending after release.
- **[C]** Through Sapolsky's layers the causes sit before the act — brain, hormones, childhood, poverty, community; retribution acts only on the last link. Punishment targets the agent; the causes live in the landscape. The free-will belief keeps it in place: if the act came from an uncaused self, punishing the self *is* addressing the cause, and treating conditions looks like excuse-making.
