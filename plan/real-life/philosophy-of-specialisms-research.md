# The philosophies of the special sciences: research note

Purpose: backing for the section "The philosophies of the special sciences" on the metaphilosophy residue page, then for the paragraph of the draft post *The End of Philosophy, Really?* that dates the "philosophy of" fields. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Working framing tested: before about 1912 the "philosophy of" a science existed, but as a division inside a philosophical system or school; in the twentieth century it turned outward and became specialised fields, each facing its own science.

Marking: **[C, Sn]** = checked against a source I opened (Sources list at the end). **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). Quotations are given as found.

---

## 1. Is there a historian's account of the shift? (Q1)

**Short answer.** None of the sources I could open uses an internal/outward or system/field framing. Two historical surveys do describe the second half of the shift, the move from a general, physics-modelled philosophy of science to philosophies of particular sciences, and they date it to the 1960s–70s, after Kuhn (S4, S9, S16; sections 6–7 below). The first half (philosophy-of-X inside a system or school) is well documented for Hegel and the neo-Kantians, but with one qualification that changes the headline for the neo-Kantians (below). Joining the two halves into one story is this note's synthesis, not a source's.

**Hegel: a part of the system.**
- The *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817) is "a systematic work in which an abbreviated version of the earlier *Science of Logic* … was followed by the application of its principles to the philosophy of nature and the philosophy of spirit". The *Philosophy of Right* (1821) is "ultimately grounded in the section of the Philosophy of Spirit part of the *Encyclopaedia* dealing with objective spirit" [C, S2 §1]. The *Philosophy of Nature* is the second of the three parts [C, S2 §3.2].
- Headline dispute: the *Philosophy of Nature* "has often been damned by the contention that Hegel had simply dismissed the activity of the empirical sciences … and in their place had attempted to give an account of the natural world as entirely deduced from logical principles"; recent interpreters "have started to push back", stressing "his broad familiarity with the natural science of his time" [C, S2 §3.2.1]. So "answering to the system" fits Hegel; "ignoring the sciences" is contested.
- Date check: S2 gives 1821 for the *Philosophy of Right*; the residue page has 1820. The book carries the imprint 1821 but appeared in late 1820 [U]. Detail only; it does not change the headline.

**The neo-Kantians: inside a school, but facing the sciences.**
- The SEP organises each school's section as conception of philosophy, theory of knowledge, then "the philosophy of the special sciences most characteristic of each school (2.3 on Marburg philosophy of physics, and 3.3 on SW philosophy of history)" [C, S1 §1]. "Philosophy of the special sciences" is the SEP author's organising phrase, not a quoted neo-Kantian term.
- They "were systematic and comprehensive philosophers, writing on nearly every area of philosophy" [C, S1]. Cohen's work "culminated in a three part system of philosophy" (1902, 1904, 1912). Natorp's *The Logical Foundations of the Exact Sciences* (1910) gives "a systematic philosophy of logic, arithmetic, geometry, and physics" [C, S1 §2].
- **The qualification.** The neo-Kantian philosophy of a science did not answer to the system in Hegel's way. They denied that philosophy "could deduce facts of the special sciences from a priori principles … or that philosophy could even be done in ignorance of the results of the sciences". Philosophy of science was for them "the logic of the sciences", "a second order discipline", with "a non-revisionist attitude". Rickert: philosophy "does not claim to direct science onto the paths it should take … it proposes only to follow science with a view to understanding it" (1902: 340). Cassirer: "critical philosophy … does not seek to direct the sciences but to understand them" (1907b: 31) [C, S1 §1, points 2 and 5; both quotations as given in S1].
- So for the neo-Kantians the accurate contrast is not "system versus science". It is *one school's method applied to each science by the school's own philosophers*, against later *independent fields with their own journals, societies and specialist practitioners*. The first is what S1 documents. The second is documented in sections 2–5.

**The bridge between the two.** "Most of the German philosophers who came to prominence in Germany after the First World War were educated by Neo-Kantians", including Carnap and Reichenbach. After 1918 the neo-Kantians "became the primary targets" of those wanting "a completely fresh start", and the upheavals up to 1933 "nearly erased the institutional memory of Neo-Kantianism within the emerging analytic and continental traditions" [C, S1 §1]. Friedman and Richardson argue that "Kant's scientific legacy in the twentieth century philosophy was much more complex and subtle" than the logical empiricists' break with the synthetic a priori suggests [C, S18, as Milkov's summary of their thesis]. The Vienna Circle presented itself as "dedicated to ending the previous disputes of philosophical schools by dismissing them, controversially, as strictly speaking meaningless" [C, S3]. That is a stated turn *away from schools*, which fits the framing's first step.

**Candidates not opened.**
- Michael Friedman, *A Parting of the Ways: Carnap, Cassirer, and Heidegger* (2000). Its subject is the neo-Kantian common ground and the split that followed Davos 1929, not the rise of specialist fields [U, from memory and search summaries]. It probably answers a different question. The metaphilosophy subject has set the analytic–continental quarrel aside.
- Alan Richardson, "Scientific Philosophy as a Topic for History of Science", *Isis* 99 (2008): 88–96 [C for the record, search result; text blocked]. Richardson's work on how "analytic philosophy" replaced "scientific philosophy" as the name for logical empiricism in North America [U, search summary] is the most likely place for a disciplinary history.
- Don Howard, "Two Left Turns Make a Right" (2003), and George Reisch (2005): the de-politicisation of North American philosophy of science in the 1950s [U]. That is a different axis (social engagement, not the specialisms). One institutional trace I could open: in its 1957 by-laws the PSA became "more focused on philosophy of science for its own sake" [C, S5, Douglas's wording].
- Psillos, "Having Science in View: General Philosophy of Science and its Significance" (2015), philsci-archive 11430 [blocked].

## 2. The professionalisation of general philosophy of science (Q2)

- **Erkenntnis, 1930.** "In 1930, the Circle took over, again together with the Berlin Society, the journal *Annalen der Philosophie* and restarted it under the name of *Erkenntnis* with Carnap and Reichenbach as co-editors" [C, S3]. Machamer: "first published in 1930", following "the first congress on the Epistemology of the Exact Sciences held in Prague in September of 1929" [C, S4 p. 5]. Springer: "Founded in 1930 and revived in 1975" [C, S7]. First series 1930–1940 [C that Wikipedia says so, S8]. *The journal belongs to the Vienna Circle and the Berlin group jointly, not to the Circle alone.*
- **Philosophy of Science and the PSA, 1933–34.** The PSA "was first announced in the *Journal of Philosophy*, December 1933", which welcomed "the first number of a new philosophical quarterly, entitled *Philosophy of Science*. It is the 'chief external expression' of the Philosophy of Science Association". "The first issue of *Philosophy of Science* appeared in January 1934". William Malisoff was editor, and the first editorial board included Carnap, Feigl and Susan Stebbing [C, S5]. The association's formal life began later: first by-laws in 1946 (revised 1947, published 1948), first elected president Philipp Frank in 1947, and the first stand-alone biennial meeting under Grünbaum in Pittsburgh in 1968 [C, S5]. Machamer: "The Philosophy of Science Association was in existence in 1934" [C, S4 p. 5]. *This is an American journal founded by Malisoff, a biochemist (S4), with Vienna Circle members on its board. It is not the Circle's own journal.*
- **BSPS and BJPS, 1948–50.** In 1948 a provisional committee of Crombie, Dingle, Popper, Rawlins and Whitrow formed a Philosophy of Science Group. Its stated aim: "The purpose of the Group is to study the logic and method of science as well as of the various special sciences, including the social sciences. The main emphasis is upon an approach through the various special sciences to the philosophy of science". Its first annual meeting was on 15 November 1948 at UCL, and in January 1959 it was reconstituted as the British Society for the Philosophy of Science [C, S6]. The BSPS lists A. C. Crombie as the first BJPS editor, 1950–1955 [C, S6b]. Journal history "1950–present" [C that Wikipedia says so, S8]. The date of vol. 1 no. 1 was not opened (open check 4).
- **Earlier institutions.** At UCL a "Board of Studies in Principles, Methods and History of Science" was set up in 1922, an M.Sc. in 1924, and a chair in "History and Method of Science" (A. Wolf) followed. Popper was appointed to LSE in 1945, and Melbourne began teaching HPS in 1946 [C, S4 p. 5]. History of science had its own society (HSS, 1924) and journal (*Isis*, 1912) [C, S4 p. 5].
- **Physics as the model.** For the logical positivists "physics was the paradigmatic science", and that, with science as the best method of knowing, was "taken to be obvious" [C, S4 p. 2]. Mayr, as late as 1969, "could complain that books with 'philosophy of science' in the title were all misleading and should be re-titled 'philosophy of physics'" [C, S11 §1, citing Mayr, "Footnotes on the Philosophy of Biology", *Philosophy of Science* 36 (1969)].

## 3. Philosophy of physics as a field of its own (Q3)

- **A stated account.** F. A. Muller (2021): "Philosophy of physics is a young branch of the tree of philosophy." Its "grandfathers" were "philosophers of science who fully engaged with modern physics": Reichenbach, Grünbaum, Putnam, Stein, Shimony, Bunge, Feyerabend, "the 1950s–1970s". The "next generation of philosophers of physics were philosophers, some of them physicists who became philosophers (1970s onwards)" (Earman, Fine, Sklar, Redhead, van Fraassen and others). A third generation followed from the 1980s (Maudlin, Albert, Howard and others) [C, S9 §2].
- **Journals.** The grandfathers published "mostly in philosophy of science journals". "*Foundations of Physics* and the short-lived *Physics Essays* published philosophy of physics papers. In 1995, *Studies in the History and Philosophy of Modern Physics* was born, as an off-spring of *Studies in the History and Philosophy of Science*. Conferences, workshops and PhD theses devoted to philosophy of physics became routine … Philosophy of physics seems here to stay" [C, S9 §2]. *Foundations of Physics* has been published since 1970, with Henry Margenau and Wolfgang Yourgrau as founding editors [C that Wikipedia says so, S8; vol. 1 not opened]. *SHPS* was established in 1970 and split, with Part B (*Modern Physics*) from 1995 [C that Wikipedia says so, S8; consistent with S9].
- **Bell and the rehabilitation of foundations.** This is a story inside physics, and it should be kept apart from the philosophers' subfield. Jeffrey Bub, reviewing Freire's *The Quantum Dissidents* (Springer, 2015): Freire "shows how research on the foundations of quantum mechanics, which was not regarded seriously as 'real physics' in the 1950s and 1960s, achieved respectability … following Bell's seminal nonlocality result in 1964 and the subsequent tests … in the 1980s". Quantum information in the 1990s "finally changed the status of research on the conceptual foundations of quantum mechanics from 'philosophy' to 'real physics.'" Freire quotes Greenberger: before Bell "there was nothing, only the philosophical disputes between famous old men" (Freire p. 245) [C, S10, Bub's review; Freire's book not opened]. Freire's own wording and his 2004 *Foundations of Physics* paper on "foundations of quantum physics" as a field of research (1950–1970) are [U].
- **Headline.** The philosophers' subfield took its own shape from the 1970s, with a journal of its own in 1995. Foundations research inside physics was marginal until Bell (1964) and the experiments of the 1970s–80s. The two strands meet in *Foundations of Physics* and in figures like Shimony. Maudlin's "seamlessly integrated" line (2012) describes where they ended up.

## 4. Philosophy of mind as a named field (Q4)

- **The phrase is old.** Robert Blakey, *History of the Philosophy of Mind* (1848, 4 vols 1850); George Trumbull Ladd, *Philosophy of Mind* (1895); William Wallace's translation *Hegel's Philosophy of Mind* (Oxford, 1894) [C, S12, catalogue records]. Word test: Wallace's "Mind" renders Hegel's *Geist*, the translator's choice. In the nineteenth century the phrase covered what became psychology (Blakey's subtitle: "the opinions of all writers on mental science") [C, S12, title]. *So "philosophy of mind" is not a twentieth-century coinage. Like Russell's psychology example, the science left and the name stayed with philosophy.*
- **As the analytic field.** Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949) [C, site page]. The identity theory is dated "from the pioneering papers 'Is Consciousness a Brain Process?' by U.T. Place (Place 1956) and H. Feigl 'The "Mental" and the "Physical"' (Feigl 1958)", followed by Smart (1959) [C, S13]. The "machine state" theories "most firmly associated with Hilary Putnam (1960, 1967)", and "In the last part of the 20th century, functionalism stood as the dominant theory of mental states" [C, S14].
- **The standard narrative and its challenge.** "According to a standard narrative, philosophy of mind was officially kicked off as a full-blown research program in the 1950s with the rise of the mind-brain identity theory (Place, Feigl, Smart) and its subsequent functionalist criticism." The Liège project MIND argues that this "overlooks" a "Cambridge family" (Stout, Moore, Russell, Broad) writing on mind before 1950 [C, S15].
- **Field-name markers, 1960s.** V. C. Chappell (ed.), *The Philosophy of Mind* (Prentice-Hall, 1962); Stuart Hampshire (ed.), *Philosophy of Mind* (Harper & Row, 1966); Jerome Shaffer, *Philosophy of Mind* (Prentice-Hall, 1968), catalogued as part of the "Foundations of Philosophy" series [C, S12, catalogue records]. The same series published Hull's *Philosophy of Biological Science* (1974) [C, S11]. Hempel's *Philosophy of Natural Science* (Prentice-Hall, 1966) [C, S12] is in the same series [U]. A textbook series with one volume per "philosophy of X" is a fair sign that these had become teaching units.
- **Course names.** No source found for the first use of "philosophy of mind" as a course or department-area name. Open.
- **Bearing.** Philosophy of mind is not a philosophy *of a science* in the way biology and physics are. Its counterpart is philosophy of psychology, which Machamer says "used to be tied to philosophical psychology, to philosophy of mind" until the cognitive revolution redefined it [C, S4 p. 10]. Its dating (1949, 1950s) also comes before Kuhn and does not depend on him.

## 5. Philosophy of biology (Q5)

- "The first sign of philosophy of biology becoming a mainstream part of philosophy of science was the publication of David Hull's *Philosophy of Biological Science* in the prominent Prentice-Hall Foundations of Philosophy series (Hull 1974). From then on, the field developed rapidly." Robert Brandon on the late 1970s: "I knew five philosophers of biology: Marjorie Grene, David Hull, Michael Ruse, Mary Williams and William Wimsatt". "By 1986, however, there were more than enough to fill the pages of Michael Ruse's new journal *Biology and Philosophy*" [C, S11 §1].
- Prehistory: Woodger (1952) and Beckner (1959) "did not give rise to a subsequent philosophical literature". Factors S11 names: "the encouragement of prominent biologists such as Mayr and F. J. Ayala", and philosophers of science working at Harvard with Gould, Lewontin and Levins [C, S11 §1]. *S11 does not name Kuhn as a factor.*
- Ruse, *The Philosophy of Biology* (London: Hutchinson, 1973) [C, S12, catalogue record]. Hull's PhD (Indiana, 1964) [C, site page]. Wikipedia: "only emerged as an independent field of philosophy in the 1960s and 1970s" [C that Wikipedia says so, S8].
- **Bearing.** The residue page's "1960s and 1970s" rests on Wikipedia. The SEP supports "the 1970s" more exactly: Hull 1974, then a journal in 1986.

## 6. Kuhn's credit and Fodor's "special sciences" (Q6)

- **Machamer (2002)** gives the fullest statement, and credits the post-Kuhn situation, not Kuhn alone. "By the early 1960s, as the result of the work of Thomas Kuhn – and concurrently Norwood Russell Hanson and Paul Feyerabend – the big philosophical question had become: Were there revolutions in science?" [C, S4 p. 7]. "The laudable tendency to explore the nature of sciences other than physics and to examine in detail cases from the history of many sciences left philosophers without a 'paradigm'" [C, S4 pp. 8–9]. The consensus that followed: "Philosophers of science could no longer get along without knowing science and/or its history in considerable depth. They, hereafter, would have to work within science as actually practiced … This was a major shift in the nature of philosophy" [C, S4 p. 9]. Then: "This emphasis on the details of science led various practitioners into doing the philosophy of the special sciences" [C, S4 p. 9], followed by philosophers of space-time, quantum theory, chemistry, biology, medicine, economics and cognitive science [C, S4 p. 10]. Machamer also contrasts this with the positivists, whose scientific training "had led them to try to make philosophy scientific after the image of their own philosophical–logical model of science" [C, S4 p. 9]. *This is the nearest any opened source comes to an "outward" framing: philosophy turning "to science itself" (his phrase, p. 9).*
- **Muller (2021):** "After the great debates in general philosophy of science about the scientific method and the rationality of science in mid-twentieth century (Kuhn, Popper, Feyerabend, Lakatos, Laudan), philosophy of science grew new sub-branches, notably philosophy of physics, of biology, and of economics" [C, S9 §8]. This is sequence ("after"), not cause.
- **Cat, SEP "The Unity of Science":** "Since the 1930s, the focus was on a syntactic approach, with physics as the paradigm of science … The historicist turn in the 1960s, the semantic turn in philosophy of science in the 1970s and a renewed interest in special sciences has changed this focus." One consequence: "the strengthening of philosophical projects and communities devoting more sustained and sophisticated attention to special sciences, different from physics" [C, S16 §3.2].
- **Politi (2019):** after "the demise of the demarcation problem … philosophy of science began to fragment in a plethora of philosophies of the special sciences, each devoted to its own narrow field of investigation" [C, S17]. His text dates the emergence and institutionalisation of general philosophy of science to "the beginning of the XIX century", evidently meaning the twentieth (as found).
- **Fodor (1974).** Cat cites "Special sciences, or the disunity of science as a working hypothesis", *Synthese* 28 (S16 gives pp. 77–115; the usual range is 97–115 [U]) as a defence of "the autonomy of the special sciences from physics" through token-physicalism [C, S16 §3.2]. No source I found credits Fodor with the rise of the specialist fields. His paper is an argument within the anti-reductionist decades that S16 describes, not a cause. Word test: "special sciences" is older than Fodor. The British group used it in 1948 ("an approach through the various special sciences to the philosophy of science", S6).
- **Counter-cases to "after Kuhn" as cause.** S11 explains philosophy of biology by biologists' encouragement and Harvard contacts, with no mention of Kuhn. Philosophy of mind runs on its own timeline (section 4). The British group already proposed "an approach through the various special sciences" in 1948 (S6).

## 7. Word test

- "Philosophy of the special sciences" for the neo-Kantians is the SEP author's phrase (S1). The residue page quotes it as if it were a term.
- "Outward-facing" (residue page, on Kitcher) is the page's word. Kitcher's abstract says the renewal should "turn the contemporary conception inside out, attending to and developing further the valuable work being done on the supposed 'periphery' and attending to the 'core areas' only insofar as is necessary" [C, S19]. The abstract does not name the philosophies of the sciences. That Kitcher (2023, p. 23) counts the philosophy of the special sciences as an area "where valuable work does get done" is Williamson's summary [U this round].
- "General philosophy of science" is the field's own term for the non-specialised part (S17; S9 §8).
- "Philosophy of mind": Hegel's *Geist* in Wallace's rendering; Blakey's "mental science" (section 4).
- Frodeman and Briggle's "unity" is the unity of the university's older fields, not philosophy's own: "these older fields were widely believed to hang together in a grand unity of knowledge … But this unity shattered under the weight of increasing specialization by the turn of the 20th century" [C, S20]. "These older fields" are "philosophy, medicine, theology and law". The residue page's "its old unity" makes it philosophy's unity, which is a slight shift.

## 10. Placement (options, not decisions)

**Residue page, "The philosophies of the special sciences".** Neutral voice, every judgement attributed. Possible shape in four steps, each backed above:
- (a) **Inside the systems and schools.** Hegel's philosophy of nature is the second part of the *Encyclopaedia*, "the application of its principles" (SEP, Redding). The neo-Kantian schools had a philosophy of physics (Marburg) and of history (Southwest), as the SEP (Heis 2018) arranges them. Add in a clause what Rickert and Cassirer said: philosophy follows the sciences, it does not direct them. That keeps the page from implying the neo-Kantians imposed a system. Attribute the phrase "philosophy of the special sciences" to the SEP.
- (b) **A general philosophy of science, with its own journals.** *Erkenntnis* (1930, Carnap and Reichenbach), *Philosophy of Science* (first issue January 1934; association formalised 1946–47), the British group (1948) and BJPS (1950). Physics was the model (Machamer; Mayr's 1969 complaint).
- (c) **The philosophies of particular sciences.** Machamer: after Kuhn, Hanson and Feyerabend, philosophers had to "work within science as actually practiced", and "this emphasis on the details of science led various practitioners into doing the philosophy of the special sciences". Muller: philosophy of physics, biology and economics grew "after the great debates". Biology: Hull 1974, Ruse's journal 1986. Physics: "a young branch", 1970s onward, SHPMP 1995. Replace the Wikipedia-backed "1960s and 1970s" for biology with the SEP's 1974/1986 markers, or attribute it.
- (d) Fix "its old unity" to the unity of the older fields (Frodeman and Briggle). Consider quoting Kitcher's own "periphery" for "outward-facing", or attribute "outward-facing" as a gloss.
- Philosophy of mind, if named, needs its own clause: an old name, an analytic field from the 1950s, not a post-Kuhn development. It could be left out of this section.
- Backing that flows down first: `reichenbach` (the year 1930), `hegel` (the three parts are in Key works; add a body line only if the subject quotes S2), the `philosophy-of-science` subject (a short institutional paragraph: Erkenntnis, PSA, BJPS, the post-Kuhn turn to particular sciences, with Machamer). No new person pages proposed; Machamer and Muller are sources, not subjects.

**Post, the paragraph "This was not yet in view when Russell wrote".** Sentence by sentence:
1. "This was not yet in view when Russell wrote." The post's own reading; fine.
2. "In 1912 the subjects had gone, but the 'philosophy of' had not yet taken up its position." **Needs changing.** In 1912 the form existed (Hegel's philosophy of nature, neo-Kantian philosophy of physics: Natorp 1910). What did not yet exist was a field of its own, with journals and specialists. Possible line: the 'philosophy of' existed, but inside the philosophers' systems and schools, not yet as a field facing its science. The last clause is the post's reading, and is defensible from Machamer's "turn to science itself".
3. "A general philosophy of science came first, in the 1930s, with the Vienna Circle and its journals". **Backed with a correction.** *Erkenntnis* (1930) was the Vienna and Berlin groups' journal. *Philosophy of Science* (1934) was American, with Carnap on its board. "With the Vienna Circle and *Erkenntnis*, then *Philosophy of Science* in America" is accurate.
4. "Carnap's method made into a discipline." The post's own reading. It is consistent with Carnap's "logic of science" (S3). But Neurath "rejected philosophy as a separate discipline" and Schlick held a different view (S3), so it describes one strand.
5. "The philosophies of the separate sciences came later, from the 1960s on, after Kuhn". **Backed** as sequence (Muller) and as an account of the post-Kuhn situation (Machamer, Cat). "From the 1970s" is the tighter date for biology and physics.
6. "philosophy of mind, of biology, and of physics as a field of its own". **Mind needs changing.** Its name is nineteenth-century, its analytic field dates from 1949 and the 1950s, before Kuhn, and it is not a philosophy of a science. Options: "of biology, of physics as a field of its own, of economics" (Muller's list), or "of psychology" (Machamer). Biology and physics are backed.
7. "These are the years of Heidegger's end of philosophy and Derrida's closure of an epoch." The dates are backed on the site's person pages (Heidegger 1964, Derrida 1967). The juxtaposition is the post's own reading.
8. "By the time Hawking wrote, the philosophy of physics had been a specialism for a generation, and the replies came from inside it." "For a generation" is backed (Muller, 1970s onward; SHPMP 1995). "The replies came from inside it" fits Maudlin, but Rovelli, a physicist, also replied, as the post itself says earlier. "Many of the replies" or "some" would be exact.
- The preceding paragraph ("It keeps some at its foundations, a 'philosophy of'") is the post's own reading. Muller's note that *Foundations of Physics* published philosophy of physics, and Maudlin's "seamlessly integrated" (on the site), sit well beside it without stating it.
