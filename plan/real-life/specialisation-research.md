# Specialisation and the loss of the whole: research note

Purpose: to record who described the splitting of knowledge into specialisms, how each diagnosed it and what each proposed instead. Material for a blog post ("philosophy is dead?") and the planned Real Life philosophy page. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

The research is aimed at one question: when these authors look at specialisation, do they ask for **unity** (one picture, from above or by reduction) or for **communication** (the parts staying distinct but talking)? The answer sorts them, and it is the axis the post needs.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (Sources list at the end). **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). Quotations are given as found in the named edition or translation.

---

## 1. Ortega y Gasset, 1930: the "learned ignoramus"

- *The Revolt of the Masses*, ch. XII, "The Barbarism of 'Specialisation'" (Allen & Unwin, 1932, pp. 119ff.). The translation is authorised by Ortega, and the translator "remains anonymous at the translator's request" [C, S1, title page]. In the Spanish it is the twelfth chapter of Part One, "La barbarie del 'especialismo'" [C, S2, table of contents].
- **The specialist** (S1 p. 124): "He is not learned, for he is formally ignorant of all that does not enter into his speciality; but neither is he ignorant, because he is 'a scientist,' and 'knows' very well his own tiny portion of the universe. We shall have to say that he is a learned ignoramus, which is a very serious matter, as it implies that he is a person who is ignorant, not in the fashion of the ignorant man, but with all the petulance of one who is learned in his own special line" [C, S1].
- Spanish: "No es sabio, porque ignora formalmente cuanto no entra en su especialidad; pero tampoco es un ignorante, porque es 'un hombre de ciencia' y conoce muy bien su pequeña porción de universo. Habremos de decir que es un sabio-ignorante, cosa sobremanera grave, pues significa que es un señor el cual se comportará en todas las cuestiones que ignora no como un ignorante, sino con toda la petulancia de quien en su cuestión especial es un sabio" [C, S2, pp. 157–58 of that edition].
- **Science versus the scientist** (S1 p. 121): "In order to progress, science demanded specialisation, not in herself, but in men of science. Science is not specialist. If it were, it would ipso facto cease to be true." Specialisation cost the scientist "contact with other branches of science, with that integral interpretation of the universe which is the only thing deserving the names of science, culture, European civilisation" [C, S1].
- **"Not listening"** (S1 pp. 124–25): "That state of 'not listening,' of not submitting to higher courts of appeal which I have repeatedly put forward as characteristic of the mass-man, reaches its height precisely in these partially qualified men" [C, S1]. Spanish: "Esa condición de 'no escuchar', de no someterse a instancias superiores" [C, S2]. This is the closest of all the sources to the "non-communicating" point.
- **His remedy is unification.** Science needs "a labour of reconstitution … an effort towards unification" (*esfuerzo de unificación*). Physics "can only be saved by a new 'Encyclopaedia' more systematic than the first" (*una nueva enciclopedia más sistemática que la primera*) [C, S1 p. 125; S2].
- **Word test.** Ortega's word is *especialismo*, an -ism: a condition or ideology, not just the division of labour (*especialización*, which he also uses). The English title turns it into "Specialisation", in scare quotes. *Sabio-ignorante* is his coinage; "learned ignoramus" is the 1932 translator's rendering. *Especialismo no compensado* ("uncompensated specialism") becomes "unbalanced specialisation" [C, S1, S2; the comparison is mine].
- **Context.** The chapter is one case within the book's thesis about the "mass-man". The specialist is the mass-man's "prototype" [C, S1 p. 120]. A post that quotes "learned ignoramus" should say this in a clause.

## 2. C. P. Snow, 1959: two cultures that cannot talk

- Rede Lecture, Cambridge, 7 May 1959 [C, S4 for the date], published as *The Two Cultures and the Scientific Revolution* (CUP, 1959) [U, the printed book not seen]. Text used: a transcription marked "THE REDE LECTURE, 1959 © Cambridge University Press" [C, S3]. The book's page numbers are not available, so the sections are named instead.
- **The gap** (section 1, "The Two Cultures"): two groups "who had almost ceased to communicate at all"; "Between the two a gulf of mutual incomprehension—sometimes (particularly among the young) hostility and dislike, but most of all lack of understanding" [C, S3].
- **The test**: "Once or twice I have been provoked and have asked the company how many of them could describe the Second Law of Thermodynamics. The response was cold: it was also negative. Yet I was asking something which is about the scientific equivalent of: Have you read a work of Shakespeare's?" [C, S3]. Variant: the *Nature Physics* editorial quotes the 1998 Canto edition (pp. 14–15) as "which is the scientific equivalent of", without "about" [C, S4]. Open check.
- **Both sides are specialists.** The literary intellectuals "dismiss them as ignorant specialists. Yet their own ignorance and their own specialisation is just as startling" [C, S3].
- **Where the cultures meet, the new arises.** "The clashing point of two subjects, two disciplines, two cultures—of two galaxies, so far as that goes—ought to produce creative chances. In the history of mental activity that has been where some of the break-throughs came. The chances are there now. But they are there, as it were, in a vacuum, because those in the two cultures can't talk to each other" [C, S3]. This is close to the author's "the new arising where they meet". Snow's is a communication remedy, not a unification one.
- **Cause and cure.** In England the gap is sharpest because of "our fanatical belief in educational specialisation" and the tendency "to let our social forms crystallise". "There is only one way out of all this: it is, of course, by rethinking our education" [C, S3].
- **1963.** In *The Two Cultures: A Second Look*: "we can repair communications to some extent: but, as I have said before, we are not going to turn out men and women who understand as much of our world as Piero della Francesca did of his, or Pascal, or Goethe" [C, S4, quoting the 1998 edition pp. 53–100; the 1963 text not opened]. His "third culture" idea in the *Second Look* is U.
- **Leavis, headline only.** Richmond Lecture, Downing College, 28 February 1962, "Two Cultures? The Significance of C. P. Snow", printed in *The Spectator* [C, S5; the Spectator date of 9 March 1962 is U]. Phrases as quoted in S5: "intellectually as undistinguished as it is possible to be"; "He doesn't know what he means, and doesn't know he doesn't know" [C as quoted in S5; the Spectator text not opened]. The attack was on Snow's standing and on the idea of culture as two equal blocs, not a defence of the gap [U as a summary]. *Nature Physics* calls it "a famously vicious attack" [C, S4].

## 3. E. O. Wilson, 1998: unity by consilience, and Whewell's word

- **The definition.** In the *Atlantic Monthly* excerpt of *Consilience* ("Back from Chaos", March 1998, "taken from his book Consilience: The Unity of Knowledge, which is to be published next month by Knopf"): "The key to unification is consilience … William Whewell, in his 1840 synthesis The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences, was the first to speak of consilience -- literally a 'jumping together' of knowledge as a result of the linking of facts and fact-based theory across disciplines to create a common groundwork of explanation" [C, S6]. The book's wording, "by the linking of facts", is the one usually quoted [U, search summary; book is lending-only].
- **The diagnosis.** "The ongoing fragmentation of knowledge and the resulting chaos in philosophy are not reflections of the real world but artifacts of scholarship." "The same professional atomization afflicts the social sciences and the humanities. The faculties of higher education around the world are a congeries of experts" [C, S6].
- **Unity on the sciences' terms.** "Consilience can be established or refuted only by methods developed in the natural sciences" (though "not led by scientists, or frozen in mathematical abstraction"). Reductionism is the "key method" of the Enlightenment programme. Culture will "devolve into science … and the humanities", social science will split, part "folding into or becoming continuous with biology", and the humanities "will draw closer to the sciences and partly fuse with them" [C, S6]. He calls the belief in consilience across the branches of learning "a metaphysical world view, and a minority one at that" [C, S6]. The book's explicit reductionism (ch. 4, "The Natural Sciences") is U.
- **Wording variant.** The Atlantic has "The greatest enterprise of the mind always has been and always will be the attempt to link the sciences and the humanities" [C, S6]. Gould quotes the book, p. 8, as "has always been and always will be the attempted linkage of the sciences and the humanities" [C, S9]. Use the book's wording, with Gould's page.
- **Whewell's own term (1840).** *The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences*, vol. 2 (Parker, 1840), Book XI, ch. V, "Of Certain Characteristics of Scientific Induction", art. 11, p. 230: "the cases in which inductions from classes of facts altogether different have thus jumped together, belong only to the best established theories which the history of science contains … I will take the liberty of describing it by a particular phrase; and will term it the Consilience of Inductions." His examples are Newton's gravitation explaining all three of Kepler's laws, and the precession of the equinoxes [C, S7].
- The sentence Wilson quotes ("The Consilience of Inductions takes place when an Induction, obtained from one class of facts, coincides with an Induction, obtained from another different class. This Consilience is a test of the truth of the Theory in which it occurs") is Aphorism XIV "concerning Science" in the 1847 edition, p. 469 [C, S8]. Whether the 1840 edition has it is not checked.
- **Word test.** Whewell's consilience is about inductions inside one theory: a test of a theory's truth. Wilson stretches it to "across disciplines" and the "great branches of learning". "Jumping together" is Whewell's own image ("jumped together"), not Wilson's invention [C, S6 against S7; the comparison is mine].
- **Whewell also named the specialists.** 1840, vol. 1, aphorisms on the language of science (around p. cxiii): "As we cannot use physician for a cultivator of physics, I have called him a physicist. We need very much a name to describe a cultivator of science in general. I should incline to call him a Scientist" [C, S8b]. His earlier use (1834) is U. This is a dated fact for the natural-philosophy story, not a priority claim.
- **Gould's reply** (*The Hedgehog, the Fox, and the Magister's Pox*, Harmony, 2003 [U for the publisher of the first edition]):
  - He shares the goal and rejects the path: "Part III of this book will also explain my reasons for rejecting his favored path toward our common goal" [C, S9, preface].
  - Sciences and humanities "do different things, each equally essential to human wholeness. We need this wholeness above all, but cannot achieve the goal by shearing off the legitimate differences (I shall critique Wilson's notion of consilience on this basis)". They "cannot be morphed into one simple coherence"; they are "two different but nonconflicting truths" [C, S9].
  - Chapter 9 is titled "The False Path of Reductionism and the Consilience of Equal Regard" [C, S9, contents]. What "consilience of equal regard" says in the chapter is U (only search summaries; chapter not opened).
  - Gould still wants one goal. His model is *e pluribus unum*, a "diverse but common enterprise of unity and power" [C, S9]. So he stands between Wilson and the author: equal regard, but a shared end.
  - Rée's review says Gould had revived Whewell's word in 1986, was "a bit peeved" that Wilson took it without acknowledgement, and shows Wilson "muddling consilience with reduction" [C, S10, as Rée's reading].

## 4. Russell, 1914: philosophy "piecemeal" like the sciences

- *Our Knowledge of the External World as a Field for Scientific Method in Philosophy* (Open Court, 1914; text used is the Allen & Unwin reissue, 1922), Lecture I, "Current Tendencies". Logical atomism, "the one that I wish to advocate", brings "the same kind of advance as was introduced into physics by Galileo: the substitution of piecemeal, detailed, and verifiable results for large untested generalities recommended only by a certain appeal to imagination" [C, S11].
- "On Scientific Method in Philosophy", Herbert Spencer Lecture, Oxford, 1914 (Clarendon Press), reprinted in *Mysticism and Logic* [C, S12 preface]. Its section II: "By concentrating attention upon the investigation of logical forms, it becomes possible at last for philosophy to deal with its problems piecemeal". "Most philosophies hitherto have been constructed all in one block". "A scientific philosophy such as I wish to recommend will be piecemeal and tentative like other sciences" [C, S12].
- The essay "Mysticism and Logic" (*Hibbert Journal*, July 1914) ends the same way: "A truly scientific philosophy will be more humble, more piecemeal, more arduous" [C, S12].
- **Bearing on the post.** Russell offers piecemeal work as the cure for philosophy's lack of progress, not as a danger. This note reads him as an early programme for the specialised, problem-by-problem philosophy that the other sources lament. That is my reading, not a source's.

## 5. Sellars, 1962: how things hang together, and the specialist's neighbourhood

- "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man", in R. Colodny (ed.), *Frontiers of Science and Philosophy* (Pittsburgh, 1962), pp. 35–78; reprinted in *Science, Perception and Reality* (1963) [C, S13 header]. Quotations are from section I, "The Philosophical Quest". Page numbers within it are not available in S13.
- "The aim of philosophy, abstractly formulated, is to understand how things in the broadest possible sense of the term hang together in the broadest possible sense of the term." Success would be to "know one's way around" with respect to all these things, "in that reflective way which means that no intellectual holds are barred" [C, S13].
- Philosophy "has no special subject-matter … What is characteristic of philosophy is not a special subject-matter, but the aim of knowing one's way around with respect to the subject-matters of all the special disciplines" [C, S13].
- **The specialist** "knows his way around in his own neighbourhood, as his neighbourhood, but doesn't know his way around in it in the same way as a part of the landscape as a whole" [C, S13].
- **Specialisation inside philosophy is legitimate.** "the task of 'seeing all things together' has itself been (paradoxically) broken down into specialities. And there is a place for specialization in philosophy." What distinguishes philosophy is "the 'eye on the whole'". A specialist more concerned with "how his work as a specialist joins up with other intellectual pursuits" is "properly, … philosophically-minded" [C, S13].
- **Sellars still seeks unity.** The manifest and scientific images must be fused "into one vision", "one stereoscopic view" [C, S13]. The site's Sellars page has "reconcile them" (the page's voice), which is consistent with this.

## 6. Kitcher, 2011 and 2023: philosophy inside out

- "Philosophy Inside Out", *Metaphilosophy* 42 (April 2011) [C, S14; issue 3, pp. 248–60 is U]. Abstract as quoted by Leiter: the "core areas" conception (epistemology, metaphysics) is "a new version of the scholastic 'self-indulgence for the few' of which Dewey complained nearly a century ago. Philosophical questions evolve, and a first task for philosophers is to address issues that arise for their own times." Renewal "should turn the contemporary conception inside out, attending to and developing further the valuable work being done on the supposed 'periphery' and attending to the 'core areas' only insofar as is necessary to address genuinely significant questions" [C, S14]. The paper itself (Wiley, 403) was not opened.
- *What's the Use of Philosophy?* (Oxford UP, 2023) [C, S15]. As Williamson reads it, Kitcher holds that much analytic philosophy "has lost its audience" (43, 58). It is to be reformed by getting rid of "most of its metaphysics, epistemology, philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, and metaethics" (24, 117, 147). The philosophy of the special sciences is an area "where valuable work does get done" (23) [C, S15, Williamson's summary with page references; Kitcher's own wording not opened].
- **Williamson's reply, headline.** The complaint is an old genre, not new; Kitcher "treats his call for reform as novel" [C, S15].
- **This changes the headline for the post.** Kitcher's target is not specialisation as such but an inward-turned "core". The specialised philosophies facing outward (of the sciences, of social questions) are, for him, the living part. So "philosophy split into specialisms" and "philosophy turned inward" are two different complaints, and the sources do not run them together.

## 7. Frodeman and Briggle, 2016: philosophy disciplined by the research university

- "When Philosophy Lost Its Way", *New York Times*, The Stone, 11 January 2016 [C, S16]. The authors are at the University of North Texas, and the piece announces the forthcoming *Socrates Tenured: The Institutions of 21st-Century Philosophy* [C, S16]. The book's publisher and date (Rowman & Littlefield International, 2016) are U.
- **The turning point** is "the locating of philosophy within a modern institution (the research university) in the late 19th century", which "made it into a discipline that could be seriously pursued only in an academic setting". "Before then, scientists were comfortable thinking of themselves as 'natural philosophers' … and the predecessors of social scientists had thought of themselves as 'moral philosophers'." The older fields "were widely believed to hang together in a grand unity of knowledge … But this unity shattered under the weight of increasing specialization by the turn of the 20th century" [C, S16].
- **The roles left open**: philosophers as "1) synthesizers of academic knowledge production; 2) formalists …; 3) translators who brought the insights of the academy to the world at large; 4) disciplinary specialists …; or 5) as some combination". The university's structure left "no real choice": philosophy "needed its own discrete domain, its own arcane language, its own standards of success and its own specialized concerns" [C, S16].
- **Their claim**: "Philosophy should never have been purified" (they take "purified" from Latour). Philosophy is "present everywhere, often interstitial, essentially interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary in nature". The close: "Having become specialists, we have lost sight of the whole. The point of philosophy now is to be smart, not good" [C, S16]. Their second strand, knowledge divorced from virtue, is beyond this note's scope.

## 8. Campbell, 1969: ethnocentrism of disciplines, fish-scale omniscience

- "Ethnocentrism of Disciplines and the Fish-Scale Model of Omniscience", in M. Sherif and C. W. Sherif (eds), *Interdisciplinary Relationships in the Social Sciences* (Chicago: Aldine, 1969) [C, S19 for the volume's title, year and publisher]; pp. 328–48 [U, S18's reference]. Several secondary sources give the volume title as "*Relations*". The archive.org record has "*Relationships*".
- Campbell blamed disciplinary ethnocentrism for "a redundant piling up of highly similar specialties leaving interdisciplinary gaps" [C as quoted in S18; the essay not opened].
- **Remedy (headline).** Hodgson (2022), who reprints extracts: Campbell "identified disciplinary boundaries as enablers of specialization but often barriers to scientific innovation and shared knowledge. But instead of unbounded interdisciplinarity, Campbell argued for focused specialisms that cross disciplinary boundaries" [C, S17 abstract]. The fish-scale image is overlapping narrow specialties that together cover the whole, like scales on a fish. No one is meant to know it all [U at quotation level; the slogan "collective comprehensiveness through overlapping patterns of unique narrownesses" is from memory and was not found].
- **Bearing.** Of all the sources, this is the nearest to the author's answer: no unification from above, no polymath, but parts that overlap and so stay in contact. The site's Campbell page lists the 1972 *Ethnocentrism* book but not this essay.

## 9. Other headlines (short, all U)

- **The disunity of science** in philosophy of science: Dupré, *The Disorder of Things* (1993); Cartwright, *The Dappled World* (1999); Galison and Stump (eds), *The Disunity of Science* (1996). These argue that the sciences are irreducibly many, against the unity-of-science programme (Neurath, the *Encyclopedia of Unified Science*). This is the field's own "not one but many" [U]. The site's Anderson page ("More Is Different") already carries a physicist's anti-hierarchy case [C, site].
- **Ortega, *Mission of the University*** (1930), on the university's task of teaching "culture" against specialism [U].
- **Jerry A. Jacobs, *In Defense of Disciplines*** (2013), a counter-case: disciplines are less closed than the silo picture says [U].

## 10. Sorting the sources (the axis for the post)

| Source | Diagnosis | Remedy | Unity or communication? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ortega 1930 | specialist as *sabio-ignorante*, "not listening" | new, more systematic "Encyclopaedia" | unity (integral culture) |
| Snow 1959 | two cultures "can't talk to each other" | rethink education; the "clashing point" as creative | communication |
| Wilson 1998 | fragmentation as "artifact of scholarship" | consilience by natural-science methods | unity by reduction |
| Gould 2003 | Wilson shears off "legitimate differences" | "consilience of equal regard", *e pluribus unum* | communication with a shared goal |
| Russell 1914 | philosophy "all in one block", unprogressive | "piecemeal and tentative like other sciences" | neither: specialisation as cure |
| Sellars 1962 | specialist knows his "neighbourhood" only | the "eye on the whole", one stereoscopic view | unity (synoptic) |
| Kitcher 2011/2023 | inward "core areas" | turn philosophy "inside out" toward its times | outward-facing specialisms |
| Frodeman & Briggle 2016 | disciplining by the research university | philosophy "interstitial", undisciplined | the whole lost by specialists |
| Campbell 1969 | ethnocentrism of disciplines, gaps | overlapping narrow specialties | communication by overlap |

All rows are C as marked above, except Gould's "equal regard" content and Campbell's fish-scale wording (U). The sorting is my reading.

The finding for the post: the lament about specialisation is old and common (1930 onward), but most remedies ask for **unity**: an encyclopaedia, a synoptic eye, reduction to natural science. The ones closest to the author's answer, communication between parts that keep their standing, are **Snow's "clashing point"**, **Gould's "equal regard"** and **Campbell's overlap**. None of them uses "pluralism" for it [U as a negative: not searched in their texts].

## 13. Placement options (not decisions)

- **Post: "Philosophy is dead?"** Possible shapes:
  - (i) The split, not the death. Natural philosophers turned "scientists" (Whewell 1840 gives the dated word). Philosophy joined the research university as one discipline among many (Frodeman and Briggle's list of five roles is a ready frame). Philosophy became many specialisms (Sellars: "there is a place for specialization in philosophy").
  - (ii) The old lament. Ortega's *sabio-ignorante* and "not listening", Snow's two cultures, Wilson's "congeries of experts".
  - (iii) Two answers. Unity (Ortega's encyclopaedia, Wilson's consilience, Sellars's stereoscopic view) against communication (Snow's "clashing point", Gould's "equal regard", Campbell's overlapping scales). SPLectrum sits with the second, in the descriptive register: it is like Snow's clashing point, with the difference that no shared end is presupposed.
  - Colour: Wilson borrowing Whewell's "jumped together" and stretching it from one theory to all learning, with Gould's objection.
  - Kitcher's distinction (an inward core, not specialism, is the trouble) is worth a sentence, so that the post does not read every specialism as a silo.
  - Per posting rules: no headings, image template.
