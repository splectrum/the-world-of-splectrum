# (Modern) natural philosophy: research note

Purpose: to record what "natural philosophy" meant historically, how and when the sciences split from it, who calls for its revival today and what each means by it, what Rovelli actually said about philosophy and physics, and where "ivory tower" comes from. It also checks one sentence on the SPLectrum 101 landing. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). Quotations from OCR scans are given with obvious OCR errors corrected (e.g. "fett" to "felt"); nothing else is changed.

## 1. What natural philosophy meant

**Headline: natural philosophy was the study of nature, one branch of philosophy among others. It was never the whole of knowledge. "Philosophy" was the broad word that covered all learned knowledge; "natural philosophy" was the part of it that dealt with nature.** Until the nineteenth century that part included what later became the natural sciences. Several authors put God, and even the soul, inside it.

- **Aristotle: physics is one of three theoretical philosophies.** "There must, then, be three theoretical philosophies, mathematics, physics, and what we may call theology" (*Metaphysics* E.1, 1026a, Ross trans.) [C, S1]. Physics "will theorize about such being as admits of being moved"; theology, or "first philosophy", deals with what is "eternal and immovable and separable", and is prior to physics [C, S1]. One conditional matters here: "if there is no substance other than those which are formed by nature, natural science will be the first science" [C, S1]. The theoretical sciences are set apart from practical and productive thought [C, S1], which is where ethics and politics belong.
- **Nature, in Aristotle's word:** "nature is a source or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily" (*Physics* II.1, 192b, Hardie & Gaye trans.) [C, S2]. Word test: the Greek is *physis*, and the science is *physikē*. "Physics" kept the name while the content changed. The Latin *philosophia naturalis* is a later rendering [U as to first Latin use].
- **The soul falls partly inside it.** "It belongs to the student of nature to study even soul in a certain sense, i.e. so much of it as is not independent of matter" [C, S1]. Natural philosophy in this sense took in living and perceiving things, not just the inanimate world.
- **Medieval universities.** Arts faculties "became in effect Aristotelian Faculties, with the curriculum divided up according to his texts"; study there "was supposed to be limited to natural knowledge", while revealed truth was handled in the theology faculties [C, S3]. The usual triad of natural, moral and metaphysical philosophy in the arts course [U, from memory: Edward Grant, *A History of Natural Philosophy*, 2007, not opened]. The first chair dedicated to natural philosophy as such is said to be Zabarella's at Padua, 1577 [C that Wikipedia says so, S7; U on the fact].
- **Descartes: natural philosophy is the trunk, not the tree.** "Thus, all Philosophy is like a tree, of which Metaphysics is the root, Physics the trunk, and all the other sciences the branches that grow out of this trunk, which are reduced to three principal, namely, Medicine, Mechanics, and Ethics" (letter-preface to the French *Principles*, 1647, Veitch trans.) [C, S4; date of the preface C from search summary only, U]. This is the clearest early-modern statement of a philosophy that takes in "the whole", and it gives that role to philosophy as a whole, not to physics.
- **Newton.** The title is *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, first published 1687 (imprimatur 5 July 1686) [C, S7 and the Wikipedia *Principia* article]. Two of Newton's own lines settle scope. First, natural philosophy includes God as known from nature: "And thus much concerning God; to discourse of whom from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy" (General Scholium, Motte trans.) [C, S5; the scholium was added in the 2nd edition, 1713, U]. Second, natural philosophy is distinct from moral philosophy and enlarges it: "if natural Philosophy in all its Parts, by pursuing this Method, shall at length be perfected, the Bounds of Moral Philosophy will be also enlarged" (*Opticks*, 4th ed., end of Query 31) [C, S6].
- **Into the nineteenth century** the term narrowed toward physics. Thomson and Tait's *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1867) is a physics textbook [C that the title exists, archive.org; date C only via S7]. Chairs of natural philosophy survive, now held by physicists or mathematicians: Oxford's Sedleian chair dates from 1621 and today sits in the Mathematical Institute [C, S12]. The site's own `maxwell` and `born` pages carry such chairs.
- **Word test: German *Naturphilosophie* is a different thing.** Schelling, Goethe, Hegel and others used it for a speculative unity of nature and spirit, associated with Romanticism and an organismic view of nature [C that Wikipedia says so, S7]. It is not a translation of the English "natural philosophy" in Newton's sense, and a page should not run the two together.

**The one dispute that changes the headline.** Most accounts, and every revival author below, treat natural philosophy as early science: "Modern science began as natural philosophy" (Maxwell) [C, S13]. Andrew Cunningham argued the opposite. On his reading natural philosophy was a different enterprise, "about God" and "God's universe", and calling it science is anachronistic ("How the *Principia* got its name; or, taking natural philosophy seriously", *History of Science* 29, 1991, 377–392) [U, search summary only; PhilArchive blocked]. Newton's General Scholium line above is the kind of evidence this reading leans on. A page that says "natural philosophy was science before the name" is taking one side. A neutral phrasing would be: it held together what we now split into science, philosophy and (for many) theology.

## 2. The split

**Headline: the separation was gradual, from the eighteenth century on, and was settled in the nineteenth through specialisation, a new name and new institutions.** 1833/34 and 1840 are the dateable markers, and both are about naming.

- **Whewell, 1834: the first printed "scientist".** It appeared in an anonymous review of Mary Somerville's *On the Connexion of the Physical Sciences*, *Quarterly Review* 51, no. 101 (March 1834), art. III, from p. 54; the passage is on pp. 59–60 [C, S8]. The review is now attributed to Whewell [C that SEP and Wikipedia say so, S10, S11; the attribution evidence, Ross 1962, not opened]. His wording sets the coinage inside a lament about fragmentation:
  - "the disintegration goes on, like that of a great empire falling to pieces; physical science itself is endlessly subdivided, and the subdivisions insulated" … "And thus science, even mere physical science, loses all traces of unity." [C, S8]
  - On the name: members of the British Association, at "York, Oxford, and Cambridge, in the last three summers", had "no general term by which these gentlemen could describe themselves with reference to their pursuits. *Philosophers* was felt to be too wide and too lofty a term, and was very properly forbidden them by Mr. Coleridge, both in his capacity of philologer and metaphysician; *savans* was rather assuming, besides being French instead of English; some ingenious gentleman proposed that, by analogy with *artist*, they might form *scientist* … but this was not generally palatable" [C, S8]. He also mentions *Natur-forscher* and the rejected "nature-poker" and "nature-peeper" [C, S8].
  - Dating: the proposal was made at the 1833 Cambridge meeting of the British Association, in answer to Coleridge [C that SEP says so, S10]. The "ingenious gentleman" is Whewell himself [C that Wikipedia, citing Ross 1962, says so, S11]. So 1833 is the coinage and 1834 the first print. Both dates are fine on a page if worded that way.
- **Whewell, 1840: the coinage in his own name.** "As we cannot use *physician* for a cultivator of physics, I have called him a *physicist*. We need very much a name to describe a cultivator of science in general. I should incline to call him a *Scientist*. Thus we might say, that as an Artist is a Musician, Painter, or Poet, a Scientist is a Mathematician, Physicist, or Naturalist." (*The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences*, vol. 1, 1840, "Aphorisms concerning the Language of Science", p. cxiii) [C, S9].
- **Uptake was slow.** "Scientist" became common in the US in the late nineteenth century and in Britain around 1900 [C that Wikipedia, citing Ross 1962, says so, S11; U on the fact]. Until then "natural philosopher" and "man of science" were the usual terms [C, S10].
- **Institutions, headline only.** The University of Berlin (1810) is the usual model of the research university [C that Wikipedia says so, S12]; Cambridge's Natural Sciences Tripos began in 1851 [C that Wikipedia says so, S12]. The broader story of professionalisation (disciplinary societies and journals, the research PhD, salaried posts) is standard history, but I opened no primary source for it [U]. McGrath puts the "disintegration of natural philosophy" in the nineteenth century, with Whewell and Darwin/Huxley as markers [C that the interviewer reports this, S14]. Maxwell begins the decline right after Newton and has it "confirmed in the 19th century" [C, S13].

## 3. Calls to revive it, and what each means

Each author is reported in their own terms. The last line of each entry states, neutrally, where they differ from SPLectrum's reading (language as the medium in which reality is experienced). That contrast is mine, not theirs.

**Nicholas Maxwell (UCL philosopher of science).**
- The title is real twice over: an article, "In Praise of Natural Philosophy: A Revolution for Thought and Life", *Philosophia* 40(4), 2012 [C, preprint, S13], and a book with the same title, McGill-Queen's University Press, 2017 [U, search summaries; publisher page not opened]. *From Knowledge to Wisdom* is Blackwell 1984, 2nd ed. Pentire 2007 [C as cited by Maxwell himself, S13].
- His argument: in Newton's time, science and philosophy "formed one mutually interacting, integrated endeavour of natural philosophy: to improve our knowledge and understanding of the universe, and to improve our understanding of ourselves as a part of it" … "But then natural philosophy died. It split into science on the one hand, and philosophy on the other" [C, S13]. He gives two causes: Newton's method hardening into "standard empiricism", and philosophy's failure on the problems the new picture of the world raised [C, S13]. His cure is "aim-oriented empiricism", together with putting at the centre "our fundamental problem of both life and thought: How can we exist and best flourish embedded as we are in the physical universe?" [C, S13]. This connects to his wider programme of an inquiry whose aim "would be to help humanity acquire wisdom – wisdom being the capacity to realize (apprehend and create) what is of value in life" [C, S13]. "Wisdom-inquiry" is his label for that programme in other works; the phrase does not occur in this paper [U].
- Against "ivory tower" (his reasons, not that phrase): he aims at "academic reform", with philosophy alerting "academic colleagues and the public" [C, S13].
- Where it differs: Maxwell's centre is *physicalism plus value*, how the human world fits into the physical universe. Language does not figure as the medium. He is also explicitly reformist about science's method, and SPLectrum is not.

**Alister McGrath (Oxford, science and religion).**
- *Natural Philosophy: On Retrieving a Lost Disciplinary Imaginary*, Oxford University Press. Date: it was described as "his latest work" in an Oxford alumni interview dated 1 November 2022 [C, S14]. A 2024 review gives the bibliographic date as 2023, 256 pp., ISBN 978-0192865731 [C, S15]. Safest wording: "(OUP, 2022/23)", or the imprint year once checked [open check]. The book grew out of his 2015–18 Gresham College lectures [C, S14].
- His argument, through the reviewer: natural philosophy aimed at objective knowledge of nature "but did not stop there"; it understood nature in a wider context now assigned to anthropology, ethics, aesthetics and religion. Its retrieval is pictured as "a confederation of disciplinary territories", none giving up its identity, all collaborating on how humans interact with the natural world: cognitive, affective, aesthetic, moral and spiritual [C as the reviewer's Spanish paraphrase, S15; McGrath's own wording U]. The publisher blurb, as summarised by search, says natural philosophy enfolded "learning about and learning from nature" [U, OUP page blocked].
- Word test: "disciplinary imaginary" is McGrath's own term.
- Where it differs: the frame is theistic and focused on nature. The "whole" is a confederation of disciplines around nature, not a common underlying medium. Of the four, his is the closest to SPLectrum's "does not compete with the specialisms".

**Roberto Mangabeira Unger and Lee Smolin.**
- *The Singular Universe and the Reality of Time: A Proposal in Natural Philosophy*, Cambridge University Press. The imprint reads "First published 2015", © 2015 [C, S16]. It was released in December 2014 [U, search summary]. Use 2015, which matches the site's `smolin` page.
- Their definition: "The proximate subject matter of the philosophy of science is science. The proximate subject matter of natural philosophy is nature. Unlike the philosophy of science, natural philosophy shares its subject matter with science" [C, S16, preface]. Natural philosophy "has long ceased to exist as a recognized genre"; "The duo of popular science and philosophy of science has usurped the place of natural philosophy" [C, S16]. It is "revisionist": it intervenes in science's agenda, though without "metaphysical ideas outside or above science", not as "a super-science" [C, S16, Part I ch. 2]. And "Natural philosophy … cannot be at the beginning of the twenty-first century what it was at the beginning of the nineteenth. It must turn into something else" [C, S16].
- Where it differs: their natural philosophy is about *nature*, cosmology above all, and claims a right to redirect a science. SPLectrum's version takes up the medium shared by every voice and explicitly does not compete with the specialisms.

**Whitehead, *The Concept of Nature* (1920; Tarner lectures, Trinity College, autumn 1919)** [C, S17].
- He uses the term for his own project: "to lay the basis of a natural philosophy which is the necessary presupposition of a reorganised speculative physics" (preface) [C, S17]. He also says "The modern natural philosophy is shot through and through with the fallacy of bifurcation" [C, S17], so for him "modern natural philosophy" names what is to be corrected.
- **This bears most directly on SPLectrum's phrase.** Whitehead defines natural philosophy as *excluding* the knower. "We can think about nature without thinking about thought. I shall say that then we are thinking 'homogeneously' about nature … Natural science is exclusively concerned with homogeneous thoughts about nature" (ch. I) [C, S17]. "For natural philosophy everything perceived is in nature. We may not pick and choose. For us the red glow of the sunset should be as much part of nature as are the molecules and electric waves" … "Natural philosophy should never ask, what is in the mind and what is in nature" (ch. II) [C, S17].
- He keeps the *whole* as philosophy's far ideal, outside natural philosophy: "the attainment of some unifying concept which will set in assigned relationships within itself all that there is for knowledge, for feeling, and for emotion. That far off ideal is the motive power of philosophic research" (ch. I) [C, S17].
- Where it differs: in Whitehead's terms, a philosophy centred on the medium of experience is "heterogeneous" thought about nature, which he places outside natural philosophy. SPLectrum would be borrowing the name for what Whitehead set beyond it. This is worth knowing before the phrase goes on a philosophy page, and it is a strong hook for the post.

**Rovelli** (see section 4). He argues that physics and philosophy need each other. In the 2018 paper he does not call his project "natural philosophy" [C, S18, word searched]. Whether he uses the term elsewhere I did not check [U].

**Others, not researched (headline only):** a wider "philosophy of nature" revival is listed in the Wikipedia article: Thomist/Aristotelian (Oderberg), Husserlian (*Crisis*), Whiteheadian process thought, and Ellis, Cartwright, Dupré [C that Wikipedia lists them, S7]. None calls for "natural philosophy" in Maxwell's sense. I would not pursue them.

## 4. Rovelli, "Physics Needs Philosophy. Philosophy Needs Physics."

- *Foundations of Physics* 48 (2018) 481–491, doi 10.1007/s10701-018-0167-y; published online 3 May 2018; arXiv:1805.10602 carries the published version [C, S18].
- **The target:** Weinberg's "Against Philosophy" chapter (*Dreams of a Final Theory*), Hawking's "philosophy is dead" (*The Grand Design*), and Neil deGrasse Tyson [C, S18].
- **The frame:** Isocrates' charge that philosophy is useless, and Aristotle's reply in the *Protrepticus* [C, S18].
- **The arguments, quoted:**
  - Philosophy shaped physics, from Galileo's Platonism to Heisenberg and Einstein. It supplies "conceptual analysis, attention to ambiguity, accuracy of expression, the ability to detect gaps in standard arguments" [C, S18].
  - Rovelli, following Aristotle: "Those who deny the utility of philosophy, are doing philosophy." The anti-philosophers are "stating their adhesion to a particular philosophy of science", traceable to the Vienna Circle's "no metaphysics!" [C, S18].
  - He quotes Einstein's letter to Thornton (7 Dec 1944): philosophical insight is "the mark of distinction between a mere artisan or specialist and a real seeker after truth" [C as quoted by Rovelli, S18].
- **The reverse direction**, which bears most on "ivory tower":
  - He criticises "the post-Heideggerian atmosphere that dominates some philosophy departments on the 'continent', [where] ignorance of science is something to exhibit with pride", and quotes Heidegger and Pickering [C, S18].
  - "just as the best science listens keenly to philosophy, so the best philosophy will listen keenly to science" [C, S18].
  - "Our general knowledge is the result of the contributions from vastly different domains, from science to philosophy, all the way to literature and the arts, and our capacity to integrate them" [C, S18].
  - He closes on "two little old men on a park bench" who are sure scientists cannot understand consciousness, "But we do!" [C, S18].
- **"Ivory tower":** the phrase does not occur in the paper [C, S18, searched], and a web search found no Rovelli use of it [U, negative result]. A page should not attribute "ivory tower" to him. The park-bench image is his own version of the point.

## 5. "Ivory tower"

- **Confirmed.** Sainte-Beuve, "À M. Villemain", in *Pensées d'août*: "… et Vigny, plus secret, / Comme en sa tour d'ivoire, avant midi, rentrait." [C, S19, in a Brussels printing dated 1838 and in the 1863 *Poésies*]. My rendering: "and Vigny, more private, / as if into his ivory tower, went back in before noon." First edition 1837 [U; not opened, though an 1838 reprint makes 1837 plausible].
- **Sainte-Beuve on his own phrase**, in a later note (1863 edition): "Cette tour d'ivoire est devenue comme inséparable du nom de M. de Vigny ; le mot a couru, et il est resté." ("This ivory tower has become as if inseparable from M. de Vigny's name; the word got about, and it stayed.") [C, S19].
- **Older image, different sense.** Song of Songs: "collum tuum sicut turris eburnea" ("your neck is like an ivory tower"), 7:4, numbered 7:5 in the Nova Vulgata [C, S20]. It became a Marian title, *Turris eburnea*, in the Litany of Loreto [U]. In the Song it is praise of beauty. Sainte-Beuve turned it into the poet's withdrawal from the world. So "detached", the sense the brief wants, starts with Sainte-Beuve, applied to a poet, not an academic. The academic sense is later [U; when it came into English not checked].

## 6. The 101 sentence

Current: "It aims for a modern natural philosophy, in the old sense of the word: philosophy that takes in the whole of what we know about the world, science included."

Findings:
1. **"the whole of what we know about the world" overstates it.** In the old sense natural philosophy was the study of nature: one theoretical philosophy beside mathematics and theology (Aristotle), the trunk and not the tree (Descartes), and distinct from moral philosophy (Newton) [C, S1, S4, S6]. Read narrowly, "the world" can mean "nature", but the plain reader will hear "everything we know". The role of taking in the whole belonged to *philosophy* in the old broad sense (Descartes' tree; Whitehead's "far off ideal"), not to natural philosophy.
2. **"science included" is anachronistic.** Natural philosophy did not take in science as a separate thing. It was, on the majority reading, the enterprise that later took the name "science" (Whewell 1833/34) [C, S8, S10]. On Cunningham's reading it was not science at all [U]. Either way, "included" pictures science as something natural philosophy took in, which is the reverse of the historical relation.
3. **The ivory-tower sentence** that follows ("Though a specialised discipline, philosophy should not reside in an ivory tower") is consistent with the phrase's origin, which is about detachment [C, S19].

Rewording options (for discussion, not decisions):
- (a) Keep "natural philosophy" and describe the historical relation accurately: "… a modern natural philosophy, in the spirit of the old one, which held together what later split into science and philosophy."
- (b) Move "the whole" onto philosophy: "… a modern natural philosophy. In the old sense, philosophy took in all of learning, the study of nature included."
- (c) Keep the author's distinction visible: "… a modern natural philosophy: not the old study of nature, which the sciences have taken over, but philosophy that can again hold the whole, because it starts from what every voice shares."
- Whatever the wording, the Whitehead point (section 3) is the one thing a careful reader may raise: classical natural philosophy, and Whitehead's explicitly, leaves the knower and the medium out.

## Placement options (not decisions)

**Philosophy page (Real Life, sibling to the 101).**
- A short history in three moves:
  - natural philosophy as one branch, the study of nature: Aristotle's three theoretical philosophies, Descartes' tree, Newton's natural and moral philosophy;
  - the split, told in Whewell's own words (the "great empire falling to pieces" line and "scientist");
  - today's revival calls, each in a line: Maxwell, McGrath, Unger & Smolin.
- Then SPLectrum's own move, stated as its own: not reclaiming nature's content but taking up the medium.
- Whitehead's "homogeneous" definition is the one counter-case worth stating openly: the name traditionally leaves the knower out, which marks the limit of the likeness.
- The Cunningham dispute deserves one clause.

**Blog post.**
- Strongest material for a post:
  - Whewell 1834: the coinage appears inside a lament about lost unity, in a review of a book written to show the sciences' connexion;

  - Sainte-Beuve's poet going home "before noon".
- "Listening first" on the 101 lines up with Rovelli's "listen keenly", which makes him a natural voice for the post. Quote him, not the phrase "ivory tower".
