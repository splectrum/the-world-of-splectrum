# Metaphilosophy outside the Western line: research note

Purpose: to record how traditions outside the Western line answer the three general metaphilosophical questions (1. What is philosophy? 2. What is, or should be, its point? 3. How should one do it?), what each tradition's own words are, how the dispute over whether each is "philosophy" runs, and what this means for how the subject's cultural and historical overview could be organised. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary itself was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary I could not open). Renderings marked "my rendering" are mine from the fetched original.

## 1. India

**Q1: the words.**
- *Darśana* ("view", from *dṛś*, "see") is the word modern Indian languages and modern Sanskrit use to translate "philosophy". Halbfass: it serves both "as an indigenous linguistic receptacle for foreign conceptual contents" and as a site of "specifically Indian apologetic claims", namely that its sense of "vision", "intuition", "realization" marks something "characteristically different from the analytical, discursive, theoretically objectifying spirit of European philosophy" [C, S4, ch. 15 §1, p. 263].
- Word test on *darśana* [C, S4 §§3–4, pp. 264–266]. In the doxographies (Haribhadra, 8th c.; Mādhava, 14th c.) it means a school's "view". The philosophers themselves "hardly ever use it" for what they do; they say *tantra, mata, vāda, siddhānta, śāstra*. Reading the doxographic "(mere) view" in the normative sense of "right vision" or "realization" "is a symptomatic innovation of Neo-Hinduism". Halbfass also calls doxographic *darśana* "a fundamentally retrospective concept" with "hardly any methodological implications" [C, S4 p. 273].
- *Ānvīkṣikī* (investigation, "looking into"). Kauṭilya, *Arthaśāstra* 1.2, lists four *vidyā*s: *ānvīkṣikī*, *trayī* (the three Vedas), *vārttā* (economics) and *daṇḍanīti* (government) (1.2.1). He says "sāṃkhyaṃ yogo lokāyataṃ ca^ity ānvīkṣikī" (1.2.10): Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Lokāyata, the materialists, are *ānvīkṣikī*. It "investigates with reasons" (*hetubhir anvīkṣamāṇā*) what is right and wrong in the Vedas, profit and loss in economics, and good and bad policy in government (1.2.11). Then comes the verse: **"pradīpaḥ sarva.vidyānām upāyaḥ sarva.karmaṇām / āśrayaḥ sarva.dharmāṇāṃ śaśvad ānvīkṣikī matā" (1.2.12)** [C, S2, GRETIL, Kangle numbering].
  - Renderings: Shamasastry (1915) has "Light to all kinds of knowledge, easy means to accomplish all kinds of acts and receptacle of all kinds of virtues, is the Science of Anvikshaki ever held to be" [C, S3]. Halbfass has "The investigative science has always been considered as a source of light for all sciences, an instrument for all activities, a foundation for all religious and social duties" [C, S4 p. 275]. "Lamp" is the literal sense of *pradīpa*. The phrasing "lamp of all sciences" is common in English, but neither of the two translations I opened uses it; the commonly cited Kangle rendering ("the lamp of all sciences") is [U].
  - Word test: Shamasastry's "the Philosophy of Sankhya, Yoga, and Lokayata" puts in a word the Sanskrit does not have [C, S3 against S2]. Halbfass notes that Jacobi (soon after the 1909 publication) translated *ānvīkṣikī* as "philosophy", and that "several translations" followed him [C, S4 p. 273].
  - Halbfass on the verse: it shows that *ānvīkṣikī* is "not a special 'science' … but a methodology which these other sciences themselves can and should utilize" [C, S4 p. 275]. The same verse is reused in Vātsyāyana's *Nyāyabhāṣya* (c. 400) for Nyāya itself [C, S4 p. 275; S12a, Sanskrit Wikisource search hit].
- **The headline dispute (Jacobi, Hacker, Halbfass).** Paul Hacker (1958) rejected Jacobi's equation. He said Jacobi was "so impressed with the Occidental emancipation of philosophy from theology that he projected this conflict" onto India [C as quoted, S4 p. 274]. Hacker's conclusion was that Hindu culture "knew the concept of philosophy, but no word which expressed this concept fully and exclusively" [C as quoted, S4 p. 285]. Halbfass concludes that neither *darśana* nor *ānvīkṣikī* "has played a role in India which would be historically comparable to the role of 'philosophy' in Europe". He also writes: "Today, it is no longer necessary to argue that there was philosophy in India". India had "constructive metaphysics as well as critical epistemology, ontology as well as linguistic analysis", with a reflexivity "fully commensurable with what we find in Europe" [C, S4 p. 286]. He adds that Europe's own history applied the concept retrospectively ("Plato and Aristotle adopted and appropriated Pre-Socratic thought as philosophy") [C, S4 p. 285]. **This is the headline for India:** commensurable thought, but no counterpart to the role the word played in Europe.

**Q2: the point.** Nyāya's first sūtra makes knowledge of the sixteen categories, *pramāṇa* first among them, the way to the highest good: "…tattvajñānāt niḥśreyasādhigamaḥ" (NS 1.1.1). The next sūtra gives release (*apavarga*) as the end of a chain running from false knowledge to suffering (1.1.2) [C, S5; my rendering]. Vātsyāyana insists that Nyāya is *ātmavidyā* aimed at *apavarga* [C, S4 p. 275]. Whether *mokṣa* is *the* point of Indian philosophy is itself a headline dispute:
- Hegel: *mokṣa* is "only an abstract and negative liberation", and "The real philosophy begins only in the Occident" [C as quoted, S4 pp. 92–93, from the *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*].
- Neo-Hindu self-presentation embraced the spiritual reading [C, S4 p. 263].
- B. K. Matilal (*Perception*, 1986): "Too often the term 'Indian philosophy' is identified with a subject that is presented as mystical and non-argumentative … A corrective to this view is long overdue" [C as quoted, S7, Matilal 1986 pp. 4–5].
- Daya Krishna, "Three Myths about Indian Philosophy": the reduction of Indian philosophy to a "spiritual" or *mokṣa*-centred endeavour is the first of the myths [C, S6, secondary; the essay itself not opened; first publication date U].
- Counter-case inside the tradition: Kauṭilya counts the materialist Lokāyata as *ānvīkṣikī* [C, S2]. That the Cārvāka/Lokāyata rejected liberation is [U].

**Q3: method.** NS 1.1.3 names four *pramāṇa*s ("means of valid knowledge"): perception, inference, comparison and testimony (*pratyakṣānumānopamānaśabdāḥ pramāṇāni*) [C, S5]. Vātsyāyana defines *ānvīkṣikī* as "examination of objects by the *pramāṇa*s" (*pramāṇair arthaparīkṣaṇam*) [C, S4 p. 275]. The debate forms (*vāda, jalpa, vitaṇḍā*) are among the sixteen categories of NS 1.1.1 [C, S5]. For later development, Navya-Nyāya built "a precise technical language" (SEP, J. Ganeri) [C, S8]. Ganeri's *The Lost Age of Reason* (2011) on early modern India is [U, title only].

## 2. China

**Q1: the words.**
- *Zhexue* 哲學 is Nishi Amane's *tetsugaku*, "the 'study' of 'wisdom'", taken into Chinese through Japanese. Defoort points out that "already in the seventeenth century" missionaries labelled the Chinese masters as philosophy, "while their Chinese contemporaries described Aristotle's work in Chinese terms (*qiongli gewu*)". What was new at the end of the nineteenth century was "the disappearance of this cultural balance" [C, S10 pp. 394–395].
- Earlier word test: the Jesuit *Ming li tan* (1631) transliterated *philosophia* as 斐录琐费亚 (*fei-lu-suo-fei-ya*), "the name for all the knowledge concerned with principles". Chinese scholars in contact with the Jesuits treated it as matching *lixue* 理學, the Study of Principle [C, S13]. Defoort's caution about the vocabulary: the most confusing neologisms were built from older terms with their own long histories, e.g. *daoli* 道理 for "reason" or "truth", from *dao* ("way", "method", "doctrine") and *li* ("pattern", "principle") [C, S10 p. 399]. That *dao* and *xue* (learning) are the tradition's own nearest words for the enterprise is [U].
- Corpus: what became "Chinese philosophy" is the *zhuzi* (masters) literature plus parts of the classics (*jing*), "retrospectively branded" [C, S10 p. 395].
- Hu Shi, *Outline of the History of Chinese Philosophy* (1919), Introduction: "凡研究人生切要的問題，從根本上着想，要尋一個根本的解決：這種學問叫做哲學" ("any learning that studies the vital problems of human life, thinks from the root, and seeks a fundamental solution is called philosophy"; my rendering) [C as quoted, S9].
- Feng Youlan, *History of Chinese Philosophy* (1931/1934): "所謂中國哲學者，即中國之某種學問或某種學問之某部分之可以西洋所謂哲學名之者". His task is to select, from the kinds of learning in Chinese history, those "that can be named by what the West calls philosophy" (my rendering) [C as quoted, S9]. This is the plainest statement of the method the legitimacy debate later questioned.
- Feng, *A Short History of Chinese Philosophy* (1948): "what I call philosophy is systematic, reflective thinking on life" [C, S11 ch. 1, p. 2].

**Q2: the point.** Feng: the sage's character is "sageliness within and kingliness without" (*neisheng waiwang*), and "the task of philosophy is to enable man to develop this kind of character"; "Philosophy is not simply something to be known, but is also something to be experienced" [C, S11 ch. 1, pp. 8–10]. Its function "is not the increase of positive knowledge of matters of fact, but the elevation of the mind" [C, S11 ch. 28]. Feng himself compares this to Plato's philosopher-king [C, S11 p. 9]. **This is the Chinese counterpart to Hadot's "way of life"**, stated by the tradition's founding modern historian.

**Q3: method.** Feng: Chinese philosophers wrote in "aphorisms, apothegms, or allusions"; what they lack in articulateness "is compensated for … by their suggestiveness" [C, S11 ch. 1]. Defoort's third position (below) puts this kind of argument against the Western definition.

**Whether it is "philosophy".**
- Hegel on Confucius, *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*: "a moralist as such, not actually a philosopher" [C as quoted, S13].
- Carine Defoort, "Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Philosophy? Arguments of an Implicit Debate", *Philosophy East and West* 51.3 (2001) 393–413 [C, S10]. She says the question is "seldom explicitly stated" but implicit answers dominate institutions. Denial is "primarily, though not exclusively, implicit and Western"; affirmation is "most explicitly represented in contemporary China" [C, S10 pp. 393, 395–396]. She sorts four positions:
  1. Chinese philosophy does not exist (historical and theoretical arguments).
  2. It exists (Feng Youlan).
  3. Redefine the content of "philosophy" so that the masters fall within it.
  4. Question the value of the philosophical project: "why should China even have had a philosophy?" [C, S10 pp. 395–402].
  
  Her paradox: "Chinese philosophy" "seems to have died of its own birth". The masters' tradition largely ended just as the modern discipline named it [C, S10 p. 395]. **This four-way sort is the best available map of the dispute, and it generalises** (Defoort says the case "acts as a case study for a wider problematic") [C, S10 p. 394].
- **Zheng Jiadong** (CASS). By his own account he first raised the problem in 1999 ("'中国哲学'与'哲学在中国'", conference October 1999; *Zhexue dongtai* 2000 no. 5). The "legitimacy" (合法性) essay began as an address at a seminar in Korea in September 2000, was posted on the 世纪中国 website, and was printed as the lead article of the *Chinese Philosophy Yearbook 2000* (published 2001) [C, S12, Zheng's own introduction to 《"中国哲学"的牟宗三时代》]. He says his target was "the legitimacy of the interpretive method" of "Chinese philosophy" since Hu Shi, not the legitimacy of Chinese thought. He asks whether it involved "汉话胡说" (Chinese words, foreign talk) and "削足适履" (cutting the foot to fit the shoe) [C, S12]. An English version, "The Issue of the 'Legitimacy' of Chinese Philosophy", appeared in *Contemporary Chinese Thought* 37.1 (2005) 11–23 [U, search summary].
- **Derrida, 2001.** The remark exists in two places.
  - (a) In conversation with Wang Yuanhua during the 2001 China visit: "中国没有哲学，只有思想". Yijing Zhang notes that the French original "has not been preserved", and the Chinese can be read as "only has thought" (the version printed in Wang's writings) or "but does have thought" (what Zhang believes Derrida meant) [C, S13, *Radical Philosophy* 204 (2019)].
  - (b) In print, in the interview preface to the Chinese translation of *Writing and Difference* (tr. Zhang Ning, Sanlian 2001, pp. 9–10) and in *Derrida's China Lectures* (2003), p. 139. Zheng quotes Derrida there as agreeing with Heidegger that philosophy is "linked to a finite history, to a language, to an ancient Greek invention … a European form", and that outside Western Europe there are thoughts and knowledge "of equal dignity", but calling them philosophy "is not reasonable" (my rendering of Zheng's Chinese) [C as quoted, S12].
  
  Headline: the remark is real and in print. Its intent (valorisation, not devaluation) is a reading, and Zhang sets it against Hegel's devaluation as sharing one logocentric frame [C, S13]. Zheng and others call the remark the "Derrida question" and pair it with his own [C, S12].
- **Hu Shi** is mostly a term on the doorway (founding definition, 1919); the "philosophy as way/practice" thread is Feng's.

## 3. Africa

**Q1/Q3: what it is and how to do it are the axis of the debate itself.**
- **Placide Tempels**, *La philosophie bantoue* (1945): a Belgian missionary's reconstruction of a Bantu ontology of vital force. The date 1945 is [C, S16]; the place (Élisabethville) and the Dutch edition of 1946 are [U]. Hountondji: it set out to answer Lévy-Bruhl's "primitive mentality", but it is "not addressed to Africans but to Europeans … colonials and missionaries"; its last chapter is "Bantu philosophy and our mission to civilize" [C, S14].
- **Alexis Kagame**, *La philosophie bantu-rwandaise de l'être* (1956 [U for date]). In Kagame's words it is an attempt by "an autochthonous Bantu African to 'verify the validity of the theory advanced by this excellent missionary'". Unlike Tempels, it "deliberately emphasizes language and its grammatical structure" and speaks of "intuitive philosophy" [C, S14].
- **Paulin Hountondji**, *Sur la « philosophie africaine »* (Maspero 1976 [U]; the 1976/1977 dating is a detail, not a headline). English: *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality* (Hutchinson, June 1983) [C, S14 editorial note]. Hountondji: "By 'African philosophy' I mean a set of texts, specifically, the set of texts written by Africans and described as philosophical by their authors themselves". Tempels' book is "an ethnological work with philosophical pretensions, or more simply, if I may coin the word, a work of 'ethnophilosophy'" [C, S14]. Whether the word was older than Hountondji is [U]. His positive thesis: philosophy "consists essentially in the process of analysis itself", not "a collective and unconscious world-view". Kagame and the others "are certainly philosophers", but their discourse rests on "a myth disguised as a collective philosophy" [C, S14].
- **H. Odera Oruka**, "Four Trends in Current African Philosophy" (read at the William Amo symposium, Accra, 1978; published 1981). The four trends are ethnophilosophy, philosophic sagacity, nationalist-ideological philosophy and professional philosophy. About a decade later (in *Sage Philosophy*) he added hermeneutic and artistic/literary philosophy [C, S15]. Sage philosophy interviews named, living, non-academic sages. Oruka faulted Tempels, Mbiti and Abraham for presenting "philosophy without philosophers" [C, S16]. Towett and Oruka argued literacy is not a prerequisite, with Socrates as the oral philosopher [C, S16].
- **Kwasi Wiredu**, conceptual decolonisation: "the purging of African philosophical thinking of all uncritical assimilation of Western ways of thinking", together with "the careful study of our own traditional philosophies and the synthesising of any insights obtained from that source with any other insights" from the modern world [C as quoted, S17, IEP citing Oladipo 2002: 328]. The book is *Conceptual Decolonization in African Philosophy* (Ibadan, 1995) [U]. Wiredu also coined "folk philosophies" for "what 'our elders' said" [C, S16].

**Q2: the point.** The trends answer differently:
- Hountondji: liberation, "in the most political sense of the word", of an ideological discourse into "a truly theoretical discourse which will be indissolubly philosophical and scientific" [C, S14].
- The nationalist-ideological trend: nation-building [C, S15, by name only].
- Wiredu: "the flourishing of our peoples and, ultimately, all other peoples" [C as quoted, S17].
- Oruka: sages "embodying their philosophies" [C, S16].

**Word test.** The "philosophy" in Tempels' title is the missionary's word. The Bantu terms (*ntu*, the vital force) are the material it organises. The debate is over the label from the start. **Headline: in Africa the metaphilosophical question was the field's founding question**, and Oruka's trends are themselves answers to "what is philosophy?".

## 4. The Islamic world

**Q1: the words.**
- *Falsafa* is a loanword from *philosophía*, and *falāsifa* are "heirs to the late-antique tradition of understanding the works of Aristotle in Neoplatonic terms" [C, S20]. Falsafa "was a movement where Christians, Muslims, and even pagan authors participated" [C, S20].
- al-Kindī's *On Definitions* gives the etymology: "the love of wisdom (*ḥubb al-ḥikma*)", *faylasūf* from *filā* + *sūfā* [C, S18].
- *Kalām* (literally "speech") is Muslim dialectical theology [U for the gloss; SEP uses "kalām" for theology without glossing]. The native word *ḥikma* (wisdom) also covers philosophy [U].

**Q1/Q2: al-Kindī (d. c. 870).** *On Definitions* gives six definitions of *falsafa* [C, S18, Stefaniuk 2017, citing Abū Rīda's edition pp. 172–174]:
1. from the name, the love of wisdom;
2. from its act, imitating "the acts of God, in so far as it is in the power of man";
3. "concern with death" (*al-ʿināya bi-l-mawt*), death being both natural and "bringing death to the desires";
4. "the craft of crafts and the wisdom of wisdoms";
5. "the knowledge of man himself";
6. "the knowledge of all eternal things … in so far as it is in the power of man".

These are the late-antique Alexandrian prolegomena definitions (David, Elias) [U for the derivation; Stefaniuk only points to David's and Elias's commentaries for comparison]. That is a headline worth checking: **the Arabic tradition received philosophy's self-definition together with philosophy**, the same "way of life" definitions (assimilation to God, practice of death) Hadot finds in Greek antiquity. *On First Philosophy*: since philosophy is the study of truth, first philosophy is "the knowledge of the first truth who is the cause of all truth" [C, S19]. Against critics of Greek learning: "We must not be ashamed to admire the truth or to acquire it, from wherever it comes. Even if it should come from far-flung nations and foreign peoples…" [C, S19]. The authenticity of *On Definitions* is doubted; it is "almost certainly at least a production of al-Kindī's circle" [C, S19]. That is a detail; flag it in a clause.

**Q2/Q3: Ibn Rushd (1126–98), *Faṣl al-maqāl* (*The Decisive Treatise*).**
- Purpose: to examine "whether the study of philosophy and logic is allowed by the Law, or prohibited, or commanded, either by way of recommendation or as obligatory" [C, S21, tr. Hourani 1961].
- Philosophy is "nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan"; the Law makes such study "obligatory" and requires it to be done "by intellectual reasoning", the best kind being demonstration [C, S21]. "Truth does not oppose truth but accords with it and bears witness to it" [C, S21]. Philosophy is "the friend and milk-sister of religion" [C, S21].
- People are sorted into demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical classes, and allegorical interpretation is reserved to the demonstrative class [C, S21].
- Word test: the chapter heading "The Law makes philosophic studies obligatory" is in Hourani's square brackets, i.e. the translator's [C, S21].

**The counter-answer: al-Ghazālī (d. 1111), *Tahāfut al-falāsifa*.** He critiques twenty positions. Most are "innovations" (*bidʿa*). Three, from Avicenna, are unbelief: the world's beginninglessness, God's knowing only universals, and the denial of bodily resurrection. He adds a *fatwā* that whoever teaches them publicly is a *kāfir* [C, S20]. Equally headline: he accepted demonstration, and with him "philosophy and the Greek sciences were 'naturalized' into the discourse of kalām". Frank Griffel argues that the *Tahāfut*, "a book that belongs to the genre of kalām", is a philosophical book, with "wider repercussions for our understanding of the philosophical tradition in Islam" [C, S20]. **Headline: the falsafa/kalām boundary is the Islamic form of the "servant of theology" question, and current scholarship disputes whether "philosophy in Islam" stops at falsafa.** The older narrative that philosophy declined in Islam after al-Ghazālī, and its revision, are [U]. So is the naming dispute between "Arabic" and "Islamic" philosophy.

**Interlock with the Western line.** Averroes reached the Latin West (the site's Aquinas page already says Aristotle entered "through Arabic translations and commentaries (principally Averroes)"). The Ghazālī–Ibn Rushd exchange is contemporary with the Western servant-of-theology period. The Islamic strand is not parallel to the Western one; it is entwined with it.

## 5. Japan

- The coinage is on the site already (Nishi, 1874) and is not redone here.
- **Nakae Chōmin**, *Ichinen yūhan* (*A Year and a Half*, Hakubunkan, September 1901), written after a terminal diagnosis [C, S23]. Its line "我日本古より今に至る迄哲学無し" ("our Japan, from antiquity to the present, has had no philosophy") [C as quoted, S22]. A Philosophical Association of Japan workshop text sets Chōmin's question beside the same "pattern" of debate in Africa (Tempels 1945), Latin America (León-Portilla, *La filosofía náhuatl*, 1956) and China [C, S22]. **This is a published statement of the cross-cultural pattern this note identifies.**
- **Nishida**, closing of "場所の自己限定としての意識作用" in 『無の自覚的限定』 (1932): "哲学は我々の自己の自己矛盾の事実より始まるのである。哲学の動機は「驚き」ではなくして深い人生の悲哀でなければならない" ("Philosophy begins from the fact of the self-contradiction of our self. The motive of philosophy must be not 'wonder' but the deep sorrow of life"; my rendering) [C as quoted, S24]. The volume and page in the *Complete Works* are [U]. **It is a direct Q2 answer, set explicitly against the Greek "wonder" (Plato, *Theaetetus* 155d; Aristotle, *Metaphysics* A.2) [U for the Greek loci].**
- **The Kyoto School and Zen.** For most of its thinkers "a philosophy of religion is the ultimate *arche* and *telos* of their thought", though not their sole concern. Nishitani refused the label "natural theologian of Zen". SEP: the school is "neither … a body of Buddhist thought forced into the form of Western garb, nor … universal discourse … dressed up in Japanese garb" [C, S25]. The site's Nishida page already holds Tanabe's objection (logic or religious intuition?), which is the school's internal Q3 dispute.

## 6. Latin America (material adequate, not thin)

- The question of whether an autochthonous Latin American philosophy exists dominated the second quarter of the twentieth century, and SEP notes the controversy "still continues" [C, S26].
- **Augusto Salazar Bondy**, *¿Existe una filosofía de nuestra América?* (1968). The existence of an authentic Latin American philosophy is "inseparable from the concrete socioeconomic conditions" of dependence, which produce a "defective culture" in which "inauthentic intellectual works are mistaken for authentic philosophical productions". Authenticity requires liberation from dependence [C, S27]. IEP lists him under Argentina; he was Peruvian [U; IEP slip]. **Q2 answer: philosophy's point is tied to liberation, and inauthenticity follows from domination.**
- **Leopoldo Zea**, *La filosofía americana como filosofía sin más* (1969) [C, S28, title and the 1968–69 debate]. Zea sought a philosophy "capable of grasping Latin America's concrete history and present circumstances in an authentic, responsible, and ultimately universal way" [C, S27]. That authenticity lies in adapting what is received to answer the society's problems is [U, search summary].
- After them came the philosophies of liberation (Dussel, *Philosophy of Liberation*, 1980; his "analectical" method against Hegelian dialectic) [C, S27].
- Ortega's perspectivism fed the Latin American identity question [C, S26]. The site's hub already has Ortega, a useful hinge.

## 7. Comparative framing and the shared pattern

- **The canon was narrowed at the hinge.** Peter K. J. Park, *Africa, Asia, and the History of Philosophy: Racism in the Formation of the Philosophical Canon, 1780–1830* (SUNY 2013). Kant-influenced historians reformulated the history of philosophy "as a march of progress from the Greeks to Kant", supplanting accounts that began in Egypt or Western Asia, and "these traditions were ultimately deemed outside the scope of philosophy and relegated to the study of religion" [C, S29, publisher's description]. Halbfass on Hegel's place for India and the Orient as "beginning, introduction, preparation" [C, S4 p. 88]. **The "is it philosophy?" question is a product of the same period the meanings piece calls the hinge.**
- **Heidegger**, "Was ist das – die Philosophie?" (1955), already on the hub: "the often-heard phrase 'Western-European philosophy' is in truth a tautology" because philosophy is Greek in essence [U for wording; search summary only. This is the Western statement Derrida aligned himself with, per S12].
- **IEP's own scoping.** The IEP metaphilosophy article treats only "Western" metaphilosophy and refers readers "on Eastern meta-philosophy" to the entries on Chinese and Indian philosophy [C, S1]. **So the site's source for the three questions does not itself answer them for other traditions.** Any cross-cultural answer on the page comes from the tradition-specific literature above.
- **Garfield and Van Norden** (2016) are on the site. Van Norden, *Taking Back Philosophy* (2017) is [U].
- **Organisational precedents in the field:**
  - Peter Adamson's *History of Philosophy without any gaps* runs Greek → late antiquity → Islamic world → medieval → … as one chronology, with **India, Africana and China as separate series** [C, S30].
  - Julian Baggini, *How the World Thinks* (2018), is organised **by question** ("How the world knows", "What the world is", "Who in the world are we?", "How the world lives") [U, search summary].
  - Randall Collins, *The Sociology of Philosophies* (1998), sets regional networks in parallel [U].
  - Jaspers' Axial Age (1949) is the classic interleaved frame [U].
- **The shared pattern.** Japan 1901 (Chōmin), China 1919–31 (Hu Shi, Feng) and 1999–2001 (Zheng, Derrida), Africa 1945–78 (Tempels → Hountondji → Oruka), Latin America 1968–69 (Salazar Bondy, Zea), and the West's own 2016 (Garfield–Van Norden). Each is a version of Defoort's four positions [C for each item as cited above; the grouping is this note's arrangement, which S22 also makes for Japan, Africa, Latin America and China].

## 8. Organisation proposal (options for the overview)

Three findings shape the choice:
- **(i)** Before about 1800 the Indian and Chinese answers run on their own chronologies and barely touch the Western line, while the Islamic line is entwined with it.
- **(ii)** After about 1800 every tradition meets the same question, "is it philosophy?", produced by one event: canon formation and the export of the word.
- **(iii)** The answers fall into recurrent types across cultures: philosophy aimed at a transformation of life (Hadot's way of life; *mokṣa*/*apavarga*; Feng's sageliness and "elevation of the mind"; al-Kindī's "practice of death"; Nishida's sorrow); philosophy as method for all knowledge (*ānvīkṣikī* as lamp; Ibn Rushd's demonstration; Descartes' tree in the other direction); and philosophy relative to revelation (servant; companion and milk-sister; falsafa against kalām).

**Option A: one interleaved chronology.**
- For: shows contemporaneity (Ibn Rushd and Aquinas; Kauṭilya and the Hellenistic schools); makes the hinge visibly global.
- Against: fragments India and China; implies a single world-history scheme of the kind Halbfass and Park describe as the source of the exclusion; invites "X before Y" readings (priority); the Western stage-sequence is one tradition's story and the others do not share its stages.

**Option B: parallel strands by culture (the Adamson model).**
- For: each tradition's own terms and chronology stand; equal standing; easy to back page by page; matches how the field and IEP divide the material.
- Against: the Western strand still reads as the main one if it comes first and longest; loses the shared hinge; the legitimacy debate repeats in every strand.

**Option C: sorted by question (the Baggini model).**
- For: the three IEP questions become the visible spine; the recurrent answer-types in (iii) become visible.
- Against: lifts answers out of context and invites equivalences the word test forbids (*darśana* ≠ philosophy, *apavarga* ≠ *eudaimonia*, *ānvīkṣikī* ≠ philosophy per Hacker); the Western historical story, which the existing pieces tell chronologically, has to be broken up.

**Option D: the Western line plus a separate "other traditions" part.**
- For: least restructuring; the meanings piece stands as is.
- Against: reproduces the hierarchy the Garfield–Van Norden proposal names; other traditions read as an appendix.

**Option E: a hybrid in two phases, with the questions as a grid inside each strand.**
- *Before the hinge*, parallel strands (Greek and Latin; Islamic, placed beside the Western line as its entwined sibling; Indian; Chinese), each answering Q1–Q3 in its own words.
- *From the hinge*, one interleaved story: canon formation (1780–1830), the export of the word (*tetsugaku* 1874; *zhexue*; *darśana* as translation), the Western identity crisis, and the twentieth-century legitimacy debates as a single strand sorted by Defoort's four positions. Africa and Latin America enter here, since their debates are what the record holds.
- For: honours (i) and (ii) together; gives the "is it philosophy?" dispute one home; uses (iii) without flattening.
- Against: two organising principles on one page; the pivot is a European event (though the sources, Halbfass, Defoort and Park, put it there); the Islamic strand straddles the two phases; Africa and Latin America appear only after the hinge, which could read as "no thought before" unless the overview says the pre-colonial record is a separate question (e.g. Oruka's sages, León-Portilla's Nahua) [the León-Portilla point is C only as a title, S22].

The choice between B, C and E is the substantive one. A and D have the clearest costs.

## 12. Discard list (do not place)

- Any "India/China/Africa had philosophy first" or "X anticipated Y" framing.
- *Darśana* glossed as "vision" or "realization" as if it were the tradition's own normative sense (Halbfass: a Neo-Hindu innovation).
- "Indian philosophy is spiritual/mokṣa-centred" stated as fact rather than as one side of a dispute.
- Shamasastry's or Jacobi's "philosophy" for *ānvīkṣikī* used without the word test.
- Derrida's remark as a put-down without the two readings and the printed source.
- Griaule's Ogotemmêli as sage philosophy (Oruka himself denied it; Griaule's method is disputed) [C, S16].
