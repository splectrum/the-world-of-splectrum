# Metaphilosophy in India as a bundle: research note

Purpose: research for restructuring the India page of the metaphilosophy subject into a hub and pieces built on India's own terms: the hub (the thinkers' words for their activity, periods and setting), the end, the means of knowing, the practice (debate and commentary), the views surveyed (darśana, doxography, standpoints) and encounters (meetings that changed how Indian thinkers understood their own activity). This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (see Sources at the end). **[C as quoted]** = wording checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a paraphrase I could not open). "My rendering" = mine, from Sanskrit I fetched.

## 1. Hub: the thinkers' own words for the activity

**The list and its headline.** Halbfass: in "the older literature of the systems, i.e. in the sūtras and their immediate commentaries", *darśana* is generally absent. "Instead, we find — often within compounds — terms like *tantra, mata, vāda, siddhānta* or *śāstra*; these terms have obviously no specific reference to the method or subject-matter of philosophy" [C, H pp. 264–265]. Śaṅkara "normally uses the terms *tantra, siddhānta, śāstra, pakṣa, mata*, or *samaya* — mostly in compounds, such as *vedāntavāda, sāṃkhyatantra*, and *sugatasamaya*". He says "our *darśana*" only once, picking up an opponent's word [C, H p. 265]. **The headline for the hub:** the words name schools, doctrines, tenets and treatises, not a discipline called philosophy.

Term by term:
- ***siddhānta*, "established tenet", and *tantra*, "school".** The *Nyāyasūtra* defines both. *tantrādhikaraṇābhyupagamasaṃsthitiḥ siddhāntaḥ* (1.1.26). Vidyabhusana renders it: "An established tenet is a dogma resting on the authority of a certain school, hypothesis, or implication" [C, NS; VID]. There are four kinds (1.1.27–31), including *sarvatantra-siddhānta*, "a dogma of all the schools … not opposed by any school", and *pratitantra-siddhānta*, "a dogma peculiar to some school", which is "accepted by similar schools but rejected by opposite schools" (1.1.28–29) [C, NS; VID]. So the *sūtra* text itself carries a vocabulary for many coexisting schools. Halbfass also cites *pratitantrasiddhānta* for "system-specific teachings" [C, H p. 278].
- ***vāda*.** Two senses:
  - (a) A doctrine, in compounds (*vedāntavāda*; *anekāntavāda*) [C, H p. 265].
  - (b) Honest debate, in the *Nyāyasūtra* (1.2.1; section 4 below). Matilal: logic "developed in ancient India from the tradition of *vādavidyā*, a discipline dealing with the categories of debate" [C, MAT p. 2]. Dasti: Nyāya "is tied to ancient traditions of debate and rules of reasoning (*vāda–śāstra*)" [C, IEP-N].
- ***mata*, "opinion, view".** Haribhadra "uses both terms [*mata* and *darśana*] interchangeably", and his commentators "often use *mata* to paraphrase *darśana*" [C, H p. 265].
- ***śāstra*, "authoritative treatise, science".** Pollock: "*Śāstra* is one of the fundamental features and problems of Indian civilization in general and of Indian intellectual history in particular." In the relation of *śāstra* ("theory") to *prayoga* ("practical activity"), "Theory is held always and necessarily to precede and govern practice; there is no dialectical interaction between them" [C, POL85 p. 499]. Pollock's thesis is contested, for instance by McCrea on poetics [U, search summary]. Present it as his reading.
- ***vidyā*, "knowledge, science".** The doxographies set the schools inside traditional lists of "fourteen or eighteen 'sciences' (*vidyā*)" [C, H p. 353]. In Kauṭilya, *ānvīkṣikī*, "investigation", is "a source of light for all sciences (*pradīpaḥ sarvavidyānām*)", which for Halbfass makes it "not a special 'science' … but a methodology which these other sciences themselves can and should utilize" [C, H p. 275].
- ***darśana*, "view".** By Haribhadra's time (8th c.) it was "a well-known designation for 'philosophical' systems or doctrines", but it "plays no noticeable role in the self-characterization of the leading exponents" [C, H p. 264]. Reading the doxographic "(mere) view" as "right vision", "realization" "is a symptomatic innovation of Neo-Hinduism" [C, H p. 266].

**The modern words (for the hub or Encounters).** In modern Indian languages *darśana* and *darśanaśāstra* became "the most important terminological analogue to 'philosophy,' specifically in Hindi and Bengali". Works in Marathi "rarely use the word *darśana*, preferring *tattvajñāna* (or *tattvavidyā*, 'the knowledge of truth')"; Gujarati also uses *tattva* compounds [C, H pp. 287, 289–290].

**What the activity is for (overlaps with The end).** Halbfass: Plato's and Aristotle's "wonder" and "desire to know" as a natural distinction of man "are absent in Indian literature. Instead, Indian philosophers usually take it for granted, or even postulate explicitly, that the desire to know has to be motivated and guided by a goal or purpose (*prayojana*)". For most *darśanas* "the ultimate purpose is final liberation (*mokṣa, mukti, apavarga*)" [C, H p. 269]. He then warns that "we cannot simply contrast the Indian 'soteriological' commitment with the 'purely theoretical' orientation of the West" [C, H p. 271]. He reports Frauwallner's reading of old Vaiśeṣika as "fundamentally unsoteriological" ("it can hardly be justified in its entirety"), and judges that Nyāya "posits and emphasizes its soteriological commitment in a manner which appears somewhat forced", in response to Buddhism [C, H pp. 271–272].

## 2. Hub: *āstika* and *nāstika*

- **Pāṇini 4.4.60:** *asti-nāsti-diṣṭaṃ matiḥ* [C, PAN]. The suffix forms a word for one whose opinion (*mati*) is "there is" (*āstika*), "there is not" (*nāstika*) or "fate" (*daiṣṭika*). The sūtra does not say what is or is not.
- **The *Kāśikā* fills the gap:** "the suffix is not intended for the mere existence of an opinion; rather, he whose opinion is 'the other world exists' (*paraloko 'sti*) is *āstika*; the opposite is *nāstika*" [C, KAS; my rendering]. The *Kāśikā* is a 7th-century commentary [U].
- **Manu 2.11:** *yo 'vamanyeta te mūle hetuśāstrāśrayād dvijaḥ / sa sādhubhir bahiṣkāryo nāstiko vedanindakaḥ* [C, MANU-S]. Bühler: "Every twice-born man, who, relying on the Institutes of dialectics, treats with contempt those two sources (of the law), must be cast out by the virtuous, as an atheist and a scorner of the Veda". Bühler's note gives Medhātithi's gloss on the reasoning relied on: "the atheistic institutes of reasoning, such as those of the Bauddhas and Cārvākas" [C, BUH, 2.11]. **Word test:** "atheist" is Bühler's English for *nāstika*. Here *nāstika* marks a person who scorns the Veda by reasoning, not a school.
- **Haribhadra (Jaina, 8th c.):**
  - He calls the six views he has surveyed, Buddhist and Jaina included, *āstikavāda*: *evam āstikavādānāṃ kṛtaṃ saṃkṣepakīrtanam*, "thus a brief account of the *āstika* doctrines has been given" (*Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya* 77) [C, SDS; my rendering].
  - His commentator Guṇaratna glosses *āstikavāda* as those who affirm "soul, other world, merit, demerit and the like", namely "Buddhist, Naiyāyika, Sāṃkhya, Jaina, Vaiśeṣika, Jaiminīya" [C, SDS, Guṇaratna on 77; my rendering].
  - Only the Lokāyata, added to make up six for those who merge Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika, is *nāstika* (77–79) [C, SDS].
- **The Vedāntic doxographies:** "Many of the Hindu-Vedāntic doxographies arrange the systems they treat into two groups of three, the 'non-Vedic' or 'heterodox' (*avaidika, nāstika*), and the 'Vedic' or 'orthodox' (*vaidika, āstika*) traditions". Splitting Buddhism four ways and Mīmāṃsā, Sāṃkhya and Tarka two ways each "yields the six orthodox systems" [C, H p. 353]. Halbfass keeps "orthodox"/"heterodox" in scare quotes throughout.
- **Who says the English misleads:** Nicholson "critiques the way in which Eurocentric concepts like … orthodoxy and heterodoxy have come to dominate modern discourses on Indian philosophy" [C, CUP, publisher's description]. His ch. 9 is titled "Affirmers (Āstikas) and Deniers (Nāstikas) in Indian History" and proposes those renderings [C as listed, WIK-N; the chapter not opened].
- **When it became a sorting of schools:** the sources above show a shift from a person's opinion (Pāṇini, Manu) to a doxographic sorting. Haribhadra's sorting cuts across the later one: Jainas and Buddhists are *āstika* for him. Nicholson places the fixing of the categories in the late medieval period [U, search summary of a secondary citing him]. **Headline:** "orthodox/heterodox" is a late-doxographic sorting rendered in European church vocabulary. It is not the terms' constant meaning.

## 4. The practice: debate

- **The three forms (NS 1.2.1–3)** [C, NS; VID p. 16]:
  - *vāda*: "Discussion is the adoption of one of two opposing sides. What is adopted is analysed in the form of five members, and defended by the aid of any of the means of right knowledge, while its opposite is assailed by confutation, without deviation from the established tenets".
  - *jalpa*: "Wrangling, which aims at gaining victory, is the defence or attack of a proposition in the manner aforesaid by quibbles, futilities, and other processes which deserve rebuke".
  - *vitaṇḍā*: "Cavil is a kind of wrangling which consists in mere attacks on the opposite side".
- **Matilal's gloss** [C, MAT pp. 2–3]:
  - *vāda* is "an honest debate … where both sides … are seeking the truth"; it was held between teacher and student or students of one school.
  - *jalpa* is "a winner-takes-all situation", usually between teachers of different schools. It "used to take place before a board or jury called the *madhyastha* … and a chairman, usually a king or a man with power and money who would organize the debate".
  - *vitaṇḍā* is "explicitly destructive", which is why Vātsyāyana "denounced this form of debate".
- ***tarka* (NS 1.1.40).** Vidyabhusana: "Confutation, which is carried on for ascertaining the real character of a thing of which the character is not known, is reasoning which reveals the character by showing the absurdity of all contrary characters". He notes that *tarka* is "variously given as 'confutation,' 'argumentation,' 'reductio ad absurdum,' 'hypothetical reasoning'" [C, NS; VID]. **Word test:** the English renderings vary. The Sanskrit term is stable.
- **Debate inside the path to release (NS 4.2.47–50)** [C, NS; VID]:
  - To secure release one is to study the treatise "as well as to hold discussions with those learned in that treatise" (47, *tadvidyaiś ca saha saṃvādaḥ*).
  - These discussions are to be held "with unenvious persons, such as disciples, preceptors, fellow-students and seekers of the summum bonum" (48).
  - "Wranglings and cavils may be employed to keep up our zeal for truth just as fences of thorny boughs" protect young growth (50; *bījaprarohasaṃrakṣaṇārthaṃ kaṇṭakaśākhāvaraṇavat*). **Note:** Vidyabhusana's full wording ends on the word the site reserves. A page needs another rendering or a cut quotation.
- ***Carakasaṃhitā* (Vimānasthāna 8, "III.8.27 ff.")** [C, MAT pp. 38–39]:
  - The friendly debate (*sandhāya saṃbhāṣā*) is held with a learned person; "One need not be afraid of defeat in such a debate for one may learn the truth", and "above all, one should respect the opponent".
  - The hostile debate (*vigṛhya saṃbhāṣā*) is allowed "provided one can gain something". Before it one must weigh the opponent (superior, equal, inferior) and the assembly (learned or not; friendly, hostile, indifferent). Against a weak opponent one may use tricks, for instance "a long quotation from a well-known text".
  - Gillon gives the passage as CS 3.8.16–67 and dates the redaction conjecturally to the beginning of the 1st century CE [C, SEP-L].
- **How it worked in practice:**
  - Janaka's court in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, with Gārgī and Yājñavalkya, is Matilal's earliest instance [C, MAT p. 31].
  - "By the third and second century BC, monks and priests were required to have a training in the art of conducting a successful debate. Several debate manuals were written in different sectarian schools" [C, MAT p. 32].
  - Gillon: the early Buddhist exchanges "are not debates in the usual sense", with a fixed pattern: challenge, refutation, "a surrender and a reward" (after Manné). Public assemblies (*pariṣad*, *sabhā*) shared the vocabulary of debate with law (after Solomon) [C, SEP-L].
  - Manuals named by Gillon include Vasubandhu's *Vādavidhi*, *Vādavidhāna* and *Vādahṛdaya* (lost, fragments) and the *Upāyahṛdaya* [C, SEP-L]. Dharmakīrti's *Vādanyāya* is on GRETIL [C as listed, GRETIL index].
  - Later Jaina theory (Vādi Devasūri, Hemacandra) required a debate "with four components" (*caturaṅga*): proponent, opponent, judges (*sabhya*, *prāśnika*) and a president (*sabhāpati*). The president would "avert a quarrel between the debating parties (—by saying that he who is defeated shall become the disciple of the victor, and the like), and give away prizes" [C, SOL I pp. 341–342].
  - Stories of debates staked on conversion (Śaṅkara and Maṇḍana) belong to the later hagiographies [U].
- **The *Milindapañha* passage (moved here from Encounters; see section 10).** Nāgasena: "If your Majesty will discuss as a scholar (*paṇḍit*), well; but if you will discuss as a king, no." "When scholars talk a matter over one with another then is there a winding up, an unravelling; one or other is convicted of error, and he then acknowledges his mistake; distinctions are drawn, and contra-distinctions; and yet thereby they are not angered." Kings instead fine whoever differs. The king: "It is as a scholar, not as a king, that I will discuss" [C, RD, II.1.3, p. 46]. Matilal reads the lines as revealing "a world where scholars used to enter into a debate that was controlled by strictly defined rules" [C, MAT pp. 32–33].
- **Rege on how the practice is misjudged:** traditional discussions can "look like mock-discussions … like tournaments", producing "improvements in style, not in substance". But "this is certainly a mistaken view of the function which the institution of philosophical discussions, *śāstrārtha*, played in the development of Indian thought", for "There have been radical innovations … the most impressive example of which is the rise of the Navya-Nyāya movement" [C, SAM pp. xxiii–xxiv]. **Own term:** *śāstrārtha*.

## 5. The practice: commentary

- **The form.** Halbfass: classical and later works were mostly "commentaries, sub-commentaries, introductions, compendia, and polemic and dialectic works", which "do not present their subject matter as such … but instead adhere to the medium of commenting and explicating". The "aphoristic brevity and ambivalence of some of the basic texts were almost tantamount to an invitation to speculative and free interpretation" [C, H pp. 363–364].
- **What it was taken to be for, in an author's own words.** Jayanta (*Nyāyamañjarī*, 9th c.) says he cannot "expound new ideas or doctrines of his own" and asks to be judged only on "the variety and novelty of his manner of presentation (*vacovinyāsavaicitryamātra*)", "as if … it were a new type of floral arrangement". Since sciences like Nyāya have existed "since the world was created", "authors" can only mean "condensation or explication (*saṃkṣepavistaravivakṣā*)" [C as quoted, H pp. 362–363]. Halbfass adds that the remarks are "unusual" in their explicitness, were made "in response to the critical question of an opponent", and stand beside "a much more pronounced sense of achievement and originality" in Dharmakīrti and Abhinavagupta [C, H p. 363].
- **Legitimation.** "The tradition had to be reclaimed; it had to be rearticulated and made intelligible for the present", against the background of the declining *yugas* [C, H p. 364].
- **Pūrvapakṣa and uttarapakṣa.** "Presenting one's own teachings as answers (*uttarapakṣa*) to competing views (*pūrvapakṣa*) is a common procedure in Indian philosophical literature" [C, H p. 354]. This ties the practice to The views surveyed (section 8).
- **Ganeri's reading.** The layered commentary continues "the process of revision and adjustment until a state of reflective equilibrium is reached" (p. 103): "reading philosophically is a way of thinking philosophically", "a rejuvenation of the ancient in the present" (p. 115) [C as quoted, BRO].
- **The headline dispute: renewal or the new?**
  - Halbfass: "There is no research in the sense of a desire to know, which keeps itself methodically open for future answers … There are no notions of method, of progress, or of the accumulation of knowledge which keep the present explicitly open for the future". He immediately adds that this is not "an a priori given and invariably valid difference between Indian and European thought" [C, H pp. 361–362].
  - Pollock: in the seventeenth century, scholars in grammar, Mīmāṃsā, dharmaśāstra, epistemology and poetics "began to identify themselves or their opponents as *navya* or 'new' scholars … not just a different relationship with the past but a different way of thinking". He sees "a sharp line being drawn, for the first time in Indian history, that systematically separated present knowledge from its past forms" [C, POL01 pp. 5–6].
  - Ganeri: Indian philosophers "view the past as something to engage and develop further" rather than break from [C as quoted, BRO].
  - Rege, above. This dispute shapes how the practice is understood, so it is a headline.

## 6. The practice: Navya-Nyāya's method

- **Gaṅgeśa's stated aim (paraphrase only).** Matilal: "In the introductory verses, he said that his book was meant for the decoration of scholars, and opponents who would be refuted in his book would no longer be able to press their views cleverly in debates" [C, MAT p. 142]. The opening verses themselves were not opened [U].
- **Characterisations:**
  - Matilal: "Gaṅgeśa's style, precision, and uniformity, his logical ordering of thoughts and arguments, became the model for all later writers. Most of these later writers earned their fame by writing a commentary or a sub-commentary on any section … even a single sentence of his book was later developed and elaborated by his commentators into a separate work" [C, MAT p. 141].
  - Phillips: "Gaṅgeśa is lucid and precise, leading the reader into a preferred position by careful examination of alternatives" [C, SEP-G].
  - Ganeri: the Navya-Nyāya authors "develop a precise technical language through the employment of which many traditional philosophical problems could be clarified and resolved"; its techniques were "employed, not just by philosophers, but also in poetics, linguistics, legal theory" [C, SEP-EM].
- **An illustration of the method.** The "five definitions" of pervasion, each tried and rejected in turn (Goekoop's translation, quoted by Matilal p. 141) [C as quoted, MAT]. Too technical for a page, but it shows the method.

## 7. The views surveyed: Haribhadra and Mādhava

**Haribhadra, *Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya* (8th c.; "the oldest of the known doxographies")** [C, H p. 350]:
- **Opening.** *saddarśanaṃ jinaṃ natvā vīraṃ syādvādadeśakam / sarvadarśanavācyo 'rthaḥ saṃkṣepeṇa nigadyate*: "Having bowed to the Jina Vīra, of true vision, teacher of *syādvāda*, I state in brief what all the views (*darśana*) set out" (v. 1) [C, SDS; my rendering]. Halbfass notes the play: the Jina is *saddarśana*, "has true and complete insight", while the work describes "views" [C, H p. 265].
- ***darśanāni ṣaḍ evātra mūlabhedavyapekṣayā / devatātattvabhedena jñātavyāni manīṣibhiḥ*:** "Here the views are just six, counting by their root differences; the wise should know them by their differences of deity and principles (*tattva*)" (v. 2) [C, SDS; my rendering].
- **The six (v. 3):** *bauddhaṃ naiyāyikaṃ sāṃkhyaṃ jainaṃ vaiśeṣikaṃ tathā / jaiminīyaṃ ca*: Buddhist, Naiyāyika, Sāṃkhya, Jaina, Vaiśeṣika, Jaiminīya [C, SDS]. Vedānta is not among them, and Lokāyata is appended as a seventh (vv. 77–79, section 2) [C, SDS]. Halbfass: "None of these four Jaina doxographies presents the Vedānta as a separate doctrinal system" [C, H p. 352]. The edition's editor suggests that Vedānta had not yet gained standing as a separate *darśana* before Śaṅkara's commentary [C, SDS-ed., Hindi introduction; my rendering; the editor's view only].
- **The non-ranking stance:**
  - "the Jaina doxographies do not follow any recognizable schema in their presentation of the six systems" [C, H p. 351].
  - A verse from Haribhadra's *Lokatattvanirṇaya* (v. 38), quoted by Guṇaratna: *pakṣapāto na me vīre na dveṣaḥ kapilādiṣu / yuktimad vacanaṃ yasya tasya kāryaḥ parigrahaḥ*, "I have no partiality for Vīra, nor hostility toward Kapila and the rest; whoever's word is reasoned, it should be accepted" [C, SDS, Guṇaratna; my rendering].
  - Halbfass on the verse: "possibly based upon an older Buddhist verse … Such statements should certainly not be interpreted as programs for historical and critical research". Yet Jainism developed "a remarkable tradition of dealing with … other schools … to put them into a systematic order and framework", in which the Jainas "find their own perspectivism, the *anekāntavāda*, fulfilled and confirmed" [C, H pp. 266–267].
  - Haribhadra avoids the term *naya* "with its systematic and 'constructive' connotations" and uses *darśana* and *mata* [C, H p. 268].
- **Stated aim.** The commentary presents the work as written for learners who cannot master the great treatises of the six systems [C, SDS-ed., Hindi rendering of the commentary's preamble; not the verse text]. Detail.

**Mādhava, *Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* (14th c.)** [C, CG]:
- **Stated aim (prologue v. 4):** "Having thoroughly searched the Śāstras of former teachers, very hard to be crossed, the fortunate Sāyaṇa-Mādhava the lord has expounded them for the delight of the good. Let the virtuous listen with a mind from which all envy has been far banished; who finds not delight in a garland strung of various flowers?" (p. 1).
- **Order (contents):** Cārvāka, Bauddha, Ārhata (Jaina), Rāmānuja, Pūrṇaprajña (Madhva), Nakulīśa-Pāśupata, Śaiva, Pratyabhijñā, Raseśvara, Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya, Jaiminīya, Pāṇinīya (grammar), Sāṅkhya, Yoga, Vedānta.
- **The ascent.** Cowell: "The systems are arranged from the Vedanta point of view … The systems form a gradually ascending scale, the first, the Charvaka and Bauddha, being the lowest as the furthest removed from the Vedanta, and the last, the Sankhya and Yoga, being the highest as approaching most nearly to it" (pp. vii–viii).
- **The sixteenth is only named:** "The system of Śaṅkara, which comes next in succession, and which is the crest-gem of all systems, has been explained by us elsewhere; it is therefore left untouched here" (p. 273).
- **Cowell on its manner:** the author "holds, as it were, a temporary brief in behalf of opinions entirely at variance with his own" (p. vii).
- **Halbfass on its place:**
  - It "steers clear of the six-fold (as well as any other numerical) scheme" and includes doctrines "considered quite modern at the time" [C, H p. 353].
  - The Advaita doxographies are "usually based upon a hierarchical classification at whose apex stands the Vedanta" [C, H p. 351].
  - Deussen's history drew heavily on it [C, H p. 350].
- **Authorship.** The identity of the author with Vidyāraṇya is often questioned [U]; Cowell reports the author's election in 1331 as head of the Śṛṅgeri *maṭha* (p. viii). Detail; a page can say "attributed to Mādhava".

## 8. The views surveyed: doxography as reflection on plurality

- **Who wrote doxographies, and how.** They are "largely the work of two religious-philosophical groups — the Jainas and the Advaita Vedāntins". The Jainas present others "as partial truths within a context of comprehensive perspectivism"; the Vedāntins present them "as stages on the way to the absolute truth", which "was tantamount to their subordination". Halbfass calls the two "a perspectivistic or a hierarchically subsuming inclusivism" [C, H pp. 351, 355–356].
- **Neither side gives ground:** "In no case, however, does a presentation of the opinions of others imply a relativizing or abandoning of one's own position" [C, H p. 355]. **Word test:** "inclusivism" is Halbfass's term. *anekāntavāda* and *naya* are the Jaina terms; "relativism" is a later reader's word.
- **Other forms:**
  - Buddhism listed views "in order to refute them as doctrines, as mere theories (*dṛṣṭi, diṭṭhi*)" [C, H p. 355]; Nāgārjuna refutes "'mere views' per se" [C, H p. 267].
  - Rich doxographic material is found in Bhāviveka, Śāntarakṣita and Jayanta [C, H p. 355].
  - Udayana calls Nyāya the "ultimate, final Vedānta" (*caramavedānta*) [C, H p. 357].
  - Abhinavagupta (*Tantrāloka* 35) invokes "the union of the traditions" (*śāstrāṇāṃ melanam*) [C, H p. 360].
- **An early motto.** Bhartṛhari (5th c.): *prajñā vivekaṃ labhate bhinnair āgamadarśanaiḥ*, "insight gains distinctness from (the study of) different traditional views". Halbfass: it "sounds almost like a motto and a programmatic justification of the future doxographies" [C as quoted, H p. 268].
- **Harmonising.** The "agreement of all philosophical systems" (*sarvadarśanasamanvaya*) is a late motif (Vijñānabhikṣu, Madhusūdana Sarasvatī, Appaya Dīkṣita, 16th c.). In his *Prasthānabheda*, Madhusūdana treats the orthodox teachers as omniscient teachers of one truth, adapted to students' capacities (*adhikārabheda*). He refuses any place to the Buddhists "and other heterodoxies (not to mention the *mlecchas*)" [C, H p. 358]. "Such traditional Hindu programs of harmonization thus differ fundamentally from the Neo-Hindu universalizing of the concepts of *samanvaya* and *adhikārabheda*" [C, H p. 358].
- **Halbfass's overall judgement:** the doxographies "provide symptomatic testimony of a contextuality that is essential to Indian thought, a deeply rooted tendency to articulate one's own position by referring to, and trying to dispose of, other points of view" [C, H p. 356].

## 9. The views surveyed: when "the six darśanas" became standard

- Halbfass: "During the last centuries, the six-fold group of Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā, and Vedānta (which were also contained in some of the older doxographies) has gained increasing recognition in presentations of Indian philosophy, and this scheme of the systems is generally accepted today" [C, H p. 353].
- **A dated witness.** Abu'l-Faẓl's *Āʾīn-i Akbarī* (Akbar's reign) presents "the six 'orthodox' systems of Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, Sāṃkhya and Yoga" plus Jainas, Buddhists and materialists, following the later doxographies [C, H p. 33].
- **Nicholson:**
  - The idea of a unified Hinduism "can be traced back to the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, where it appears prominently in the works of what may be called doxographers", who divided Veda-accepting from Veda-rejecting schools in a hierarchy.
  - The supposed unity "does not bear up well under careful historical scrutiny, given that the Sāṃkhya school was every bit as critical of Vedic rituals as the Buddhists and Jains ever were"
  - [C as reported, HAY, Hayes's NDPR review; the book not opened].
- **Daya Krishna** is reported to have attacked the "six schools" picture as one of the textbook myths [U; see section 13].
- **Headline:** the fixed six is a late-doxographic and modern-textbook scheme. Haribhadra's six differ, and Mādhava avoids numbering.

## 10. Encounters: the scope test and its headline

**The headline (Halbfass, xenology chapter):** "There is no parallel or analogous development of Indian interest in or speculation about Europe … Traditional Hinduism has not reached out for the West … It has neither recognized the foreign, the other as a possible alternative, nor as a potential source of its own identity." India "discovered the West and begun to respond to it in being sought out, explored, overrun and objectified by it" [C, H p. 172]. On Islam: "In spite of various forms of factual symbiosis and occasional scholarly collaboration, we find no serious philosophical or religious debate with Islam in traditional Hindu literature … What Indian literature offers in this respect, is a tradition of silence and evasion" [C, H p. 182]. Buddhism had "a generally more open and universalistic attitude" [C, H p. 19]. Radhakrishnan held something similar from the other side: before the sea route, invasions (Alexander's excepted) did little "to promote spiritual intercourse", so "we may look upon Indian thought as a closed system or an autonomous growth" [C, RAD p. 24].

**Counter-currents (for balance; a headline dispute):**
- Pollock: in seventeenth-century Mughal north India there was "increased circulation in unfamiliar conceptual universes … where interactions with Persianate intellectuals or Europeans became more common and more cordial" [C, POL01 p. 6].
- Nicholson's hypothesis, as Hayes reports it: the late-medieval doxographers' "perceived need for unity may have been brought about by the political threat posed by Islam", though "there is … no mention at all of Islam in the writings of the doxographers" [C as reported, HAY].
- Truschke, *Culture of Encounters* (2016), on Sanskrit intellectuals at the Mughal court [U, not opened].

**The scope test, item by item (each with the source's judgement):**

|---|---|---|
| Greeks / *Milindapañha* | "little in the text which is Greek, aside from the name of the king"; Tarn's attempt to infer a Greek original "is not convincing" [C, H p. 19, n. 105]. Matilal uses the passage as evidence of Indian debate norms [C, MAT p. 32] | No, on these sources; the passage belongs in The practice |

## 11. Encounters: Dārā Shikōh

- ***Majmaʿ al-Baḥrayn*, completed 1065 AH, "the forty-second year" of his age (1655 CE)** [C, MAJ, colophon]. His own account of what it is for: having learned "the true religion of the Sufis", "he thirsted to know the tenets of the religion of the Indian monotheists". After "repeated intercourse and (continuous) discussion with the doctors and perfect divines of this (i.e. Indian) religion … he did not find any difference, except verbal, in the way in which they sought and comprehended Truth". Hence the title, "as it is a collection of the truth and wisdom of two Truth-knowing (*Ḥaqq-shinās*) groups". It is written "for the benefit of the members of my family and I have no concern with the common folk of either community" [C, MAJ, tr. Mahfuz-ul-Haq, preface]. **Word test:** "Indian monotheists" is the translator's English for the Persian.
- ***Sirr-i Akbar*:** fifty Upaniṣads, "completed in 1657", with commentary excerpts partly traceable to Śaṅkara. Who did the translating "has not yet been answered". Dārā took the Upaniṣads for the "first heavenly Book" referred to in the Qurʾān [C, H pp. 33–34]. Halbfass on the *Majmaʿ*: "without much attention to details or to questions of intercultural hermeneutics" [C, H p. 34]. Records survive of "seven dialogues with Bābā Lāl" [C, H p. 34]. Executed 1659 [C, H p. 34].
- **The European afterlife** (Anquetil-Duperron's *Oupnek'hat*, 1801–02; Schopenhauer) is Europe's reception, outside this piece's scope [C, H p. 35].

## 12. Encounters: Neo-Vedānta and "spiritual India"

- **Hacker's frame, via Halbfass:**
  - Modern Hindu thought splits into "Neo-Hinduism" and "surviving traditional Hinduism", a distinction "not based upon any particular teachings".
  - "Neo-Hinduism … always implies reinterpretation"; its adherents first adopt Western values, "afterwards they connect these values with and claim them as part of the Hindu tradition".
  - Rammohun Roy was only a "forerunner".
  - [C as quoted, H pp. 219–221]. Halbfass: "Hacker's scheme is a simplification, although a useful and convenient one" [C, H p. 221].
- **Rammohun Roy:**
  - The *Vedāntacandrikā* (1817) attacked his imageless monotheism as a "new Vedānta" (*abhinava vedānta*) [C, H p. 222].
  - He uses "philosophy" "only in a casual and unreflected sense" [C, H p. 292].
- **Keshab Chandra Sen:** "Europe, the Lord has blessed thee with scholarship and science and philosophy … Add to these the faith and intuition and spirituality of Asia". This model of complementarity "has become typical of Neo-Hindu self-awareness" [C as quoted, H p. 226].
- **Vivekananda:**
  - "Touch him on spirituality, on religion, on God, on the soul … the lowest peasant in India is better informed on these subjects than many a so-called philosopher in other lands" [C as quoted, H p. 231].
  - "When the Occident wants to learn about the spirit, about God, about the soul … he must sit at the feet of the Orient to learn" [C as quoted, H p. 233].
  - On philosophy itself: "In every religion there are three parts: philosophy, mythology, and ritual. Philosophy of course is the essence of every religion"; "Religion without philosophy runs into superstition; philosophy without religion becomes dry atheism" [C as quoted, H p. 293].
- **Radhakrishnan:** "Philosophy in India is essentially spiritual … The spiritual motive dominates life in India. Indian philosophy has its interest in the haunts of men" [C, RAD pp. 24–25]. Halbfass lists his formulas ("To the Indian mind, philosophy is essentially practical"; "Every philosophy is the exposition and justification of an experience") and names the common motifs: philosophy placed "entirely at the center of the Indian tradition", "the blurring of the distinction between what should be and what is" [C, H p. 299].
- **The surveys:** modern Indian surveys present "visionaries, reformers, and national founding figures" as "philosophers", and philosophy is "claimed as a domain for cultural and national self-affirmation". "Gandhi, never expressed any interest in 'philosophy'" [C, H p. 291].

## 13. Encounters: the academic answers (K. C. Bhattacharyya, Daya Krishna, *saṃvāda*)

**K. C. Bhattacharyya, "The Concept of Philosophy" (in Radhakrishnan and Muirhead, eds., *Contemporary Indian Philosophy*, 1936)** [C, CIP]:
- "An explication of the concept of philosophy appears to me more important than the discussion of any specific problem of philosophy. The possibility of philosophy as a body of knowledge distinct from science is nowadays called in question" (p. 105).
- "Science alone speaks in genuine judgments … Philosophy deals with contents that are not literally thinkable and are not actually known, but are believed as demanding to be known without being thought" (p. 113).
- "There is no question of philosophy progressing towards a single unanimously acceptable solution. All philosophy is systematic symbolism and symbolism necessarily admits of alternatives" (p. 123).
- Halbfass sets him apart from the apologists: "intense, almost passionate, yet highly reserved philosophizing about the concept of philosophy". His Vedānta "is not utilized as a national emblem" [C, H pp. 295, 297]. His son Kalidas Bhattacharyya's *Alternative Standpoints in Philosophy* (1953) opens: "In modern times the first business of Philosophy should be to justify its own existence" [C as quoted, H p. 298].

**"Svaraj in Ideas": date and venue.** The IPQ reprint's footnote: "This discourse was given at a meeting of the students of the Hooghly College of which the writer was Principal, during 1928-30 … Subsequently it was published in the *Visva Bharati Journal*, Vol. XX, 1954; pp. 103-114" [C, IPQ p. 383]. Halbfass: "originally presented between 1928 and 1930" [C, H n. 41 to ch. 16]. Against these, Wikipedia gives "an oration delivered in Candranagar in October 1931", citing Ganeri's essay "Freedom in Thinking" (2017) [C as Wikipedia states; Ganeri not opened]. Detail, not headline. A page can say "given c. 1928–31, published 1954".

**Daya Krishna, "Three Myths about Indian Philosophy".**
- **First publication:** *Diogenes* 14, no. 55 (September 1966), pp. 89–103, DOI 10.1177/039219216601405506. First sentence: "Indian Philosophy, like Indian culture, seems peculiarly prone to arouse either violent antipathy or violent enthusiasm" [C, DIO, publisher's page].
- **Collected** in *Indian Philosophy: A Counter Perspective* (1991; rev. ed.) [C as listed, RAV].
- **What the three myths are:** Raveh says "The reduction of Indian philosophy into a 'spiritual' or *mokṣa*-centered endeavour simply enraged him", pointing to this article [C, RAV]. The three myths themselves were not seen [U].
- **The book's preface,** as quoted by Raveh: "The interests of Western Indological studies, combined with the search for a spiritual self-identity in the face of overwhelming Western superiority in all fields of knowledge, seemed to have led to the creation of a certain picture of India's philosophical past … which render[s] it almost impossible to question the picture". Its aim is to "reestablish a living continuity with India's philosophical past" [C as quoted, RAV].

**The *saṃvāda* project.** *Saṃvāda: A Dialogue between Two Philosophical Traditions*, ed. Daya Krishna, M. P. Rege, R. C. Dwivedi, Mukund Lath (ICPR with Motilal Banarsidass, 1991) [C, SAM]:
- **The Pune dialogue** "between pandits and philosophers held in Pune from July 11th to the 16th, 1983" (Rege, p. xxv). Its topic was Russell's theory of the proposition, put into Sanskrit by E. R. Swaminathan: "probably … the first time when a western philosophical doctrine was presented through the medium of Sanskrit to an audience of pandits" (p. xxv).
- **Daya Krishna's preface:**
  - "perhaps, the first record of a dialogue between philosophers trained in the classical Indian tradition of philosophizing and those trained in the western tradition".
  - "few, even in India, are aware that the tradition of active philosophizing within the classical frameworks … is not dead".
  - "Sanskrit is still the living lingua franca of traditional scholarship in India" (p. xi).
  - "earlier experiments at Jaipur" had "not really clicked" (p. xii).
  - Follow-ups: Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā and Kashmir Śaiva meetings at Sarnath, Tirupati and Srinagar; a dialogue with the Ulema; schools in modern logic and Navya-Nyāya (pp. xii–xiv). The last session of each meeting asked "what are the deficiencies in the system as handed down to us and how should we try to develop it further. The very asking of these questions set the mind of the traditional scholars in a different direction … turned from the past to the future" (p. xiii).
- **Rege's aim:** the Indian schools must enter current debate "not merely as witnesses to be subjected to examination and cross-examination but as full participants"; "Indian schools have developed over centuries through continuous and incisive dialogue with one another. Surely the time has come for them to extend the scope of the dialogue by admitting western philosophical schools to it" (pp. xxiv–xxv).
- **This is an encounter that changed how traditional practitioners framed their own activity,** on the editors' own account.

**Optional (present-day).** Ganeri's "Why Philosophy Must Go Global: A Manifesto" (*Confluence* 4, 2016), with "re-emergence" and a polycentric discipline [U, search summary; not opened]. Not clear enough to place.
