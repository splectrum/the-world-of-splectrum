# "The End of Philosophy": research note

Purpose: to back a planned neutral subject page, "The End of Philosophy", on two facets. (A) Why then: the historical conditions the sources themselves name behind the end-of-philosophy claims. (B) A finite task? The debate over whether philosophy's problems are perennial or change with history. The verdicts themselves (who declared what ended) are not redone here. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (Sources list at the end). **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). Quotations are given as found in the named translation. "C secondary" = the wording is quoted, with a page, in a secondary source I opened, but the book itself was not opened.

## A. Why then: the conditions the sources name

### A1. Laplace's intelligence (1814)

- The passage, in the third edition (Paris: Courcier, 1816), section "De la Probabilité", pp. 3–4: "Nous devons donc envisager l'état présent de l'univers, comme l'effet de son état antérieur, et comme la cause de celui qui va suivre. Une intelligence qui pour un instant donné, connaîtrait toutes les forces dont la nature est animée, et la situation respective des êtres qui la composent, si d'ailleurs elle était assez vaste pour soumettre ces données à l'analyse, embrasserait dans la même formule les mouvemens des plus grands corps de l'univers et ceux du plus léger atome : rien ne serait incertain pour elle, et l'avenir comme le passé, serait présent à ses yeux" [C, S2]. The 1840 Bachelier edition (p. 4) has the same wording [C, S2a].
- First edition (Paris: Mme Vve Courcier, 1814): the scan's OCR loses p. 2. Page 3 opens with the same continuation ("de cette intelligence. Ses découvertes en mécanique et en géométrie …") [C, S1]. So the 1814 wording is very probably the same, but p. 2 itself is not read [U].
- The sentence that follows matters for the headline: the human mind "offre, dans la perfection qu'il a su donner à l'astronomie, une faible esquisse de cette intelligence". Its efforts "tendent à le rapprocher sans cesse de l'intelligence que nous venons de concevoir, mais dont il restera toujours infiniment éloigné" [C, S2; S1 for p. 3]. Laplace states the ideal and, in the same breath, humanity's infinite distance from it. The point of the passage is to introduce probability, which exists because of that distance.
- English (Truscott and Emory, from the sixth French edition, Wiley 1902), ch. II "Concerning Probability", pp. 3–4: "We ought then to regard the present state of the universe as the effect of its anterior state and as the cause of the one which is to follow. Given for one instant an intelligence which could comprehend all the forces by which nature is animated and the respective situation of the beings who compose it—an intelligence sufficiently vast to submit these data to analysis—it would embrace in the same formula the movements of the greatest bodies of the universe and those of the lightest atom; for it, nothing would be uncertain and the future, as the past, would be present to its eyes." The next sentences: "a feeble idea of this intelligence … from which it will always remain infinitely removed" [C, S3].
- Where it sits. The brief's "introduction" is right in a looser sense. In the *Essai* the passage opens the section "De la Probabilité", after a short untitled introduction. That introduction says the essay develops a 1795 lecture at the Écoles normales [C, S3, S2]. Bergson cites the passage to Laplace's "Introduction à la théorie analytique des probabilités" (*Œuvres complètes*, vol. VII, Paris 1886, p. vi) [C, S25 footnote]. The *Essai* served as the introduction to the *Théorie analytique* from its 1814 edition on [U].
- Word test. Laplace's word is *une intelligence*. "Demon" is a later label [U on who first used it]. Du Bois-Reymond's 1872 lecture *Über die Grenzen des Naturerkennens* spoke of a "Laplace'scher Geist" [U, search summary]. Bergson's English translator has "superhuman intellect" [C, S25].
- Reception as a driver. Bergson, *Creative Evolution* (Mitchell, 1911), ch. I, pp. 37–38. The scientists who believed "in the universality and perfect objectivity of mechanical explanations have, consciously or unconsciously, acted on a hypothesis of this kind. Laplace formulated it with the greatest precision". Bergson then quotes Laplace, Du Bois-Reymond and Huxley in sequence [C, S25]. Bergson's own reading of the scheme is under B8.

### A2. Heidegger, "The Age of the World Picture" (1938; *Holzwege* 1950)

- Occasion, from Heidegger's note at the end of *Holzwege* as translated by Lovitt: "The lecture was given on June 9, 1938, under the title 'The Establishing by Metaphysics of the Modern World Picture,' as the last of a series … arranged by the Society for Aesthetics, Natural Philosophy, and Medicine at Freiburg im Breisgau … The appendixes were written at the same time but were not delivered". The pieces "have been repeatedly revised" since [C, S4, Preface p. x]. Lovitt took the text from *Holzwege* (Klostermann, 1952) [C, S4]. That is a later printing; the first edition is 1950 [U; the archive.org catalogue record dates a *Holzwege* copy 1950].
- Metaphysics grounds an age: "Metaphysics grounds an age, in that through a specific interpretation of what is and through a specific comprehension of truth it gives to that age the basis upon which it is essentially formed" (p. 115) [C, S4].
- Science and technology are named at once as the age's phenomena, and technology is tied to metaphysics: "Machine technology remains up to now the most visible outgrowth of the essence of modern technology, which is identical with the essence of modern metaphysics" (p. 116) [C, S4].
- Man as *subiectum*: "What is decisive is not that man frees himself to himself from previous obligations, but that the very essence of man itself changes, in that man becomes subject. We must understand this word *subiectum*, however, as the translation of the Greek *hypokeimenon* … Man becomes the relational center of that which is as such" (p. 128) [C, S4]. Descartes is the turning point: "What it is to be is for the first time defined as the objectiveness of representing, and truth is first defined as the certainty of representing, in the metaphysics of Descartes" (p. 127) [C, S4].
- World as picture: "Hence world picture, when understood essentially, does not mean a picture of the world but the world conceived and grasped as picture" (p. 129). "The world picture does not change from an earlier medieval one into a modern one, but rather the fact that the world becomes picture at all is what distinguishes the essence of the modern age [der Neuzeit]" (p. 130). "That the world becomes picture is one and the same event with the event of man's becoming *subiectum* in the midst of that which is" (p. 132) [C, S4].
- The link to an end of philosophy is made in the essay itself (Appendix 4, p. 140): "With Descartes begins the completion and consummation of Western metaphysics … With the interpretation of man as *subiectum*, Descartes creates the metaphysical presupposition for future anthropology of every kind and tendency … Through anthropology the transition of metaphysics into the event of the simple stopping and setting aside of all philosophy is introduced" [C, S4]. Young and Haynes render the same sentences: "With Descartes, there begins the completion of Western metaphysics … Through anthropology, the transition of metaphysics into the event of the simple cessation and suspension of all philosophy is inaugurated". They add Heidegger's next line: Dilthey's "philosophy of philosophy" is "a leading example of anthropology's doing away with—as opposed to overcoming—philosophy" (*Off the Beaten Track*, p. 75) [C, S5].
- The link to technology (Appendix 9, p. 152): "In the planetary imperialism of technologically organized man, the subjectivism of man attains its acme, from which point it will descend to the level of organized uniformity … This uniformity becomes the surest instrument of total, i.e., technological, rule over the earth" [C, S4]. Lovitt's note 38 reads this as "a close foreshadowing" of the later account of *Ge-stell* [C, S4]. That reading is the translator's.
- The sciences: as research becomes institutionalised "ongoing activity", "the more irresistibly do the sciences achieve the consummation of their modern essence" (p. 126) [C, S4].
- Word test. *Weltbild*: Lovitt notes the conventional rendering would be "conception of the world" or "philosophy of life", and that "world picture" is chosen to follow the argument (n. 12) [C, S4]. German terms in Lovitt's brackets: *Weltbild*, *subiectum*, *vorstellen* (represent, set before), *die Neuzeit*, *Besinnung* (reflection), *das Seiende* (what is), *Entgötterung* (loss of the gods; "degodization") [C, S4]. Whether "completion and consummation" renders one German word (likely *Vollendung*) is not checked [U]. The phrase "metaphysics of subjectivity" was **not found** in Lovitt's text of this essay (OCR search of the whole essay and appendices) [C, S4, as a negative check]. The essay speaks of "subjectivism", the "subjectivity of the *subiectum*" and "the certainty of subjectivity". "Metaphysics of subjectivity" as Heidegger's phrase belongs, as far as I know, to the Nietzsche lectures [U]. A page should not put it in quotation marks as this essay's wording.

### A3. Schnädelbach: the identity crisis after Hegel

- Only the Spanish translation was open: *Filosofía en Alemania (1831–1933)*, trans. Pepa Linares (Cátedra, 1991), translated from *Philosophy in Germany 1831–1933* (CUP, 1984), "© Syndicate of the Press of the University of Cambridge" [C, S6]. The English wording below is my rendering, not the CUP text [U at wording level].
- The book's guiding question (Introduction, p. 15): "¿Qué es la filosofía en una época posidealista, y hasta qué punto es posible mantener ese nombre?" For post-Hegelian philosophers the proclamation of the "fin de la filosofía" is "una burda maniobra". The "desplome del idealismo" has led philosophy into "una profunda crisis de identidad, aún actual". The sign is that "¿adónde va la filosofía?" is the recurring theme of every inaugural lecture. The study aims to investigate the crisis's causes "y en la posibilidad de superarla" [C, S6].
- The cause, ch. 3 "La Ciencia" (pp. 87–88). The age "abandonó la ecuación ciencia-filosofía, tradicional desde Aristóteles hasta Hegel: la filosofía dejó de ser el modelo de la ciencia". Philosophers lost "el secular monopolio en la definición de lo 'científico'". After Hegel science "se consideró autosuficiente", and philosophy was "obligada a probar en cada momento no ya su necesidad, sino su propio derecho a existir. La continua exigencia de legitimidad sumió a la filosofía en una evidente crisis de identidad" [C, S6]. In English, roughly: philosophy ceased to be the model of science, and had to prove not its necessity but its very right to exist.
- The ways out he names (ch. 3, pp. 120–121). First, "historización": making philosophy a science by the methods of the *Geisteswissenschaften*, with its status resting on the interpretation of texts, and the revivals of old systems (neo-Aristotelians, neo-Thomists, neo-Kantians …). Second, "la ciencia como filosofía", likened to Freud's "identificación con el agresor": scientism, or recasting philosophical questions as logical or empirical problems, or adopting the methods of science [C, S6].
- Headline: Schnädelbach treats the "end of philosophy" talk as a reaction to a real loss of role after 1831, and dates the crisis to the collapse of idealism [C, S6]. The English term "identity crisis" is reported as his in the secondary literature [U, not seen in the CUP text].

### A4. The linguistic turn

- Coinage. Bergmann, "Two Types of Linguistic Philosophy", *Review of Metaphysics* 5 (1952), p. 417, as quoted: "Of late philosophy has taken a linguistic turn. At least this is true of a large and, by general agreement, significant part of all philosophical activity that went on in the English-speaking countries during the last one or two generations. (...) The causes of this linguistic turn or, if you please, the roots of the movement (...) are many … Yet the influence of three men, Moore, Russel[l], and Wittgenstein, stands out" [C secondary, S7]. By 1959 Bergmann writes of it as an established label, in the preface to *Meaning and Existence* (Wisconsin, 1959): "At the beginning of this century analytical philosophy took what has been called the linguistic turn. The issue, and it still is an issue, is one of method" [C secondary, S7a]. The 1952 article itself was not opened (JSTOR not tried; the archive.org Bergmann volumes are lending-only).
- Rorty's attribution, *The Linguistic Turn* (Chicago, 1967), Introduction, p. 9 n.: "The phrase 'the linguistic turn' which Bergmann uses [in *Logic and Reality*, 1964, p. 177] and which I have used as the title of this anthology is, to the best of my knowledge, Bergmann's own coinage" [C secondary, S7]. SEP: the volume "did much to cement the idea of a linguistic turn … as a sea change in the history of philosophy" [C, S10].
- Rorty's 1967 Introduction is titled "Metaphilosophical Difficulties of Linguistic Philosophy" [C secondary, S7, S8]. As quoted with pages by Mom [C secondary, S8]:
  - linguistic philosophy holds that "philosophical problems are problems which may be solved (or dissolved) either by reforming language, or by understanding more about the language we presently use" (p. 3);
  - its core assumption reduces to "a single plausible claim: that we should not ask questions unless we can offer criteria for satisfactory answers to those questions" (p. 14);
  - it has left philosophy a "merely critical, essentially dialectical, function";
  - Rorty "envisages at least six possibilities for the future of philosophy, after the dissolution of the traditional problems" (pp. 33 f.), one of them a "'post-philosophical' culture" (p. 34).
  Note: in 1967 Rorty already has "post-philosophical" in scare quotes, and Mom says Rorty there questions its conceivability [C secondary, S8].
- The later reading, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (Princeton, 1979), Introduction, p. 8, as quoted by Brandom: "'Analytic' philosophy is one more variant of Kantian philosophy, a variant marked principally by thinking of representation as linguistic rather than mental, and of philosophy of language rather than 'transcendental critique,' or psychology, as the discipline which exhibits the 'foundations of knowledge.' This emphasis on language … does not essentially change the Cartesian-Kantian problematic, and thus does not really give philosophy a new self-image. For analytic philosophy is still committed to the construction of a permanent, neutral framework for inquiry, and thus for all of culture" [C secondary, S9]. Also p. 6: the aim "to assert the possibility of a post-Kantian culture, one in which there is no all-encompassing discipline which legitimizes or grounds the others" [C secondary, S9]. SEP gives PMN's key claim at p. 168: "the Kantian picture of concepts and intuitions getting together to produce knowledge is needed to give sense to the idea of 'theory of knowledge' as a specifically philosophical discipline, distinct from psychology" [C, S10].
- Headline: for Rorty in 1979, the linguistic turn changed the medium (language for mind) but not the foundational project [C secondary, S9]. Elsewhere (CP, 1982) he hopes for a culture without capital-P Philosophy [U here].

### A5. Quantum foundations and philosophy (short)

- Wüthrich (arXiv 1406.5737, an essay for a Putnam volume): "By the 1960s, most of the people working in fundamental physics had turned away from the foundational problems of quantum mechanics … Only a few intrepid souls such as John S Bell, Abner Shimony, and Hilary Putnam stubbornly continued to puzzle over these 'philosophical' problems … Today … most philosophers and many physicists recognize that there remains a central foundational problem". He quotes Gell-Mann at the 1976 Nobel Conference: "Niels Bohr brainwashed the whole generation of theorists into thinking that the job was done 50 years ago" (proceedings 1979, p. 29) [C, S11].
- The fuller historical account is Freire. See "The Historical Roots of 'Foundations of Quantum Physics' as a Field of Research (1950–1970)", *Foundations of Physics* 34 (2004): 1741–1760 [C, S12, bibliographic record], and *The Quantum Dissidents: Rebuilding the Foundations of Quantum Mechanics (1950–1990)* (Springer, 2015) [C, S12, record of a chapter]. Their content is [U] (search summaries only). That includes the reported claim that philosophy of quantum mechanics became a field of its own in the late 1970s, with dedicated journals and meetings.
- Headline that can be carried: the measurement problem and interpretations were kept open by a few physicists and philosophers through a period when most physicists treated them as settled [C, S11]. "Where philosophy of physics re-established itself" is stronger than S11 says; it needs Freire opened [U].

## B. A finite task? Perennial problems against historical change

### B1. Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912)

- No explicit "perennial problems" claim was found. Chapter XV, "The Value of Philosophy", gives a shrinking-residue view instead: "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science … those questions which are already capable of definite answers are placed in the sciences, while those only to which, at present, no definite answer can be given, remain to form the residue which is called philosophy" [C, S13].
- He also keeps a core he expects to stay open: "There are many questions … which, so far as we can see, must remain insoluble to the human intellect unless its powers become of quite a different order from what they are now". It is "part of the business of philosophy to continue the consideration of such questions" [C, S13].
- The close: "Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions, since no definite answers can, as a rule, be known to be true, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves" [C, S13].
- Headline: Russell is not a clean "perennialist". He holds both that philosophy hands its answerable parts to the sciences and that some questions stay [C, S13].

### B2. Hegel: philosophy as its own history

- The *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*, Introduction A, open on the perennial/historical problem itself: "Philosophy aims at understanding what is unchangeable, eternal, in and for itself: its end is Truth. But history tells us of that which has at one time existed, at another time has vanished … Truth is eternal; it does not fall within the sphere of the transient, and has no history" [C, S14].
- His resolution: the diversity of philosophies "is, and has been, absolutely necessary to the existence of a science of Philosophy". "It must be shown from the succession in philosophies how that succession is the systematization of the science of Philosophy itself". And: "the latest, most modern and newest philosophy is the most developed, richest and deepest. In that philosophy everything which at first seems to be past and gone must be preserved and retained, and it must itself be a mirror of the whole history" [C, S14].
- The close of the lectures: "throughout all time there has been only one Philosophy, the contemporary differences of which constitute the necessary aspects of the one principle"; "the succession of philosophic systems is not due to chance, but represents the necessary succession of stages in the development of this science"; "We can now, for example, be Platonists no longer" [C, S14].
- *Philosophy of Right*, Preface (Dyde, 1896): "every one is a son of his time; so philosophy also is its time apprehended in thoughts". And: "philosophy at least always comes too late … When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old, and by means of grey it cannot be rejuvenated, but only known. The owl of Minerva, takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering" [C, S15].
- *Phenomenology*, ch. VIII (Baillie): "The goal, which is Absolute Knowledge or Spirit knowing itself as Spirit, finds its pathway in the recollection of spiritual forms (*Geister*) as they are in themselves". History "(intellectually) comprehended" forms "the recollection and the Golgotha of Absolute Spirit" [C, S16].
- Headline: Hegel holds the problem and the history together. The truth is one, and its history is its development. That makes him the reference point for both sides of B [C, S14]. The translator of the marxists.org *Lectures* is not named on the pages fetched (probably Haldane, 1892) [U].

### B3. Collingwood: absolute presuppositions and "eternal problems"

- *An Essay on Metaphysics* (Clarendon, 1940; photographic reprint 1948):
  - "Metaphysics is the attempt to find out what absolute presuppositions have been made by this or that person or group of persons, on this or that occasion or group of occasions, in the course of this or that piece of thinking" (p. 47) [C, S17].
  - Ch. VI, "Metaphysics an Historical Science": "All metaphysical questions are historical questions, and all metaphysical propositions are historical propositions" (p. 49) [C, S17].
  - How presuppositions change, in a note to ch. V (p. 48): such a change "cannot be a matter of choice … It is the most radical change a man can undergo". The absolute presuppositions of a society "form a structure which is subject to 'strains' … If the strains are too great, the structure collapses and is replaced by another, which will be a modification of the old with the destructive strain removed; a modification not consciously devised but created by a process of unconscious thought" [C, S17].
  - Ch. VII (p. 73): honest comparison "will convince the metaphysician … that there are no 'eternal' or 'crucial' or 'central' problems in metaphysics. It will rid him of the parish-pump idea that the metaphysical problems of his own generation … are the problems that all metaphysicians have been worrying about ever since the world began" [C, S17].
  - Ch. VIII, p. 86 n.: "The metaphysicians who believe in eternal problems are bad historians, or they would not believe in eternal problems" [C, S17].
- *An Autobiography* (1939), ch. VII "The History of Philosophy". The English text was not legible in the copy opened, so the wording is checked in the Spanish (*Autobiografía*, trans. Jorge Hernández Campos, FCE, pp. 66–72) [C, S18]:
  - The question he put to the "realists": "¿Era realmente verdad … que los problemas de la filosofía eran, aun en el sentido más lato de la palabra, eternos? ¿Era realmente verdad que las diferentes filosofías eran diferentes intentos por contestar a las mismas preguntas?" His answer: "no era verdad" (p. 66).
  - The Plato/Hobbes case and the conclusion: "la historia de la teoría política no es la historia de distintas respuestas dadas a una y la misma pregunta, sino la historia de un problema más o menos cambiante, cuya solución cambia con él" (pp. 67–68).
  - Generalised: "La concepción de los 'problemas eternos' desaparecía completamente", except in the sense that any historical fact is "eternal" for having happened once (p. 72, with a footnote on the loose sense of "eternal problem" as a series of problems connected by historical change).
  The English wording usually quoted is "the history of political theory is not the history of different answers given to one and the same question, but the history of a problem more or less constantly changing, whose solution was changing with it" [U, English not seen]. The OUP 2013 edition's commentaries cite the passage in English: "the nature of the state in Plato's time was genuinely different from the nature of the state in Hobbes's" (A, 61) [C secondary, S19, Haddock's essay].
- **A dispute that changes the headline.** Whether Collingwood really rejected eternal problems is contested in the literature. Bertoldi (*Dialogue* 24, 1985) reads the late works as denying them and asks whether this reverses his earlier *philosophia perennis*. Vanheeswijck (*Dialogue* 40, 2001) answers no: the late terminology is "un peu curieuse et même parfois trompeuse", and for Collingwood philosophical questions are "à la fois historiques et éternelles" [C, S20, the abstracts]. So a page can state what the 1939–40 texts say, but not flatly that "Collingwood rejected eternal problems".

### B4. MacIntyre: traditions of enquiry

- *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), ch. 1, p. 9, as quoted in IEP: "So rationality itself, whether theoretical or practical, is a concept with a history: indeed, since there are also a diversity of traditions of enquiry, with histories, there are, so it will turn out, rationalities rather than rationality, just as it will also turn out that there are justices rather than justice" [C secondary, S21].
- IEP summary: WJWR follows "three distinct traditions" (p. 326) plus liberalism as a fourth (p. 349). Epistemological crises are resolved "by the construction of a new narrative which enables the agent to understand both how he or she could intelligibly have held his or her original beliefs and how he or she could have been so drastically misled by them" (from "Epistemological Crises, Dramatic Narrative and the Philosophy of Science", 1977). "There is no alternative ahistorical, non-traditional way to make progress in human enquiry" (IEP's wording) [C, S21].
- Headline: MacIntyre is historicist about rationality but not relativist about progress. Traditions can make progress, and can fail, by their own lights [C, S21]. The tradition definition often quoted ("an argument extended through time…", WJWR p. 12) and "The relationship of philosophy to its past" (in *Philosophy in History*, CUP 1984) were not opened [U].

### B5. Rorty: contingency and the historiography of philosophy

- *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989), as quoted in SEP: the ironist "has radical and continuing doubts about the final vocabulary she currently uses" (CIS, 73). She believes "there is nothing beyond vocabularies which serves as a criterion of choice between them" (CIS, 80) [C secondary, S10]. SEP sums up the stance: "any vocabulary is optional and mutable is the basic conviction behind Rorty's attack on representationalist epistemology", a "historicist conviction" [C, S10]. Rorty's own definition of "final vocabulary" and the ch. 1 line "The world does not speak. Only we do" are not checked [U].
- "The historiography of philosophy: four genres". Cambridge Core lists it in Rorty, *Truth and Progress* (Philosophical Papers vol. 3, CUP 1998), pp. 247–273. Its opening, as given there: rational reconstructions treat dead philosophers "as contemporaries"; they have "led to charges of anachronism"; the apparent dilemma is "either we anachronistically impose enough of our problems and vocabulary on the dead to make them conversational partners, or we confine our interpretive activity to making their falsehoods look less silly by placing them in the context of the benighted times in which they were written. Those alternatives, however, do not constitute a dilemma. We should do both of these things, but do them separately" [C, S22]. First publication in *Philosophy in History*, eds. Rorty, Schneewind and Skinner (CUP, 1984) [U, page range]. The four genres are reported as rational reconstruction, historical reconstruction, *Geistesgeschichte* and doxography, with doxography rejected [U, search summaries]. Note: the 1998 reprint listing is Cambridge Core's; I have not reconciled it with the usual citation to the 1984 volume (open check).

### B6. Dewey, "The Influence of Darwinism on Philosophy" (1909)

- Text: *The Influence of Darwin on Philosophy and Other Essays in Contemporary Thought* (Henry Holt, 1910). The essay's own heading there is "The Influence of Darwinism on Philosophy". It was "A lecture in a course of public lectures on 'Charles Darwin and His Influence on Science,' given at Columbia University in the winter and spring of 1909. Reprinted from the *Popular Science Monthly* for July, 1909" [C, S23].
- The passage (p. 19): "the conviction persists—though history shows it to be a hallucination—that all the questions that the human mind has asked are questions that can be answered in terms of the alternatives that the questions themselves present. But in fact intellectual progress usually occurs through sheer abandonment of questions together with both of the alternatives they assume—an abandonment that results from their decreasing vitality and a change of urgent interest. We do not solve them: we get over them. Old questions are solved by disappearing, evaporating, while new questions corresponding to the changed attitude of endeavor and preference take their place" [C, S23].
- Also (p. 13): "the new logic outlaws, flanks, dismisses—what you will—one type of problems and substitutes for it another type. Philosophy forswears inquiry after absolute origins and absolute finalities in order to explore specific values and the specific conditions that generate them" [C, S23; OCR damaged, wording confirmed by context].
- Word test: the book title says "Darwin", the essay title "Darwinism" [C, S23].

### B7. The debate named as such (secondary)

- IEP, "Metaphilosophy" (N. Joll, 2010, rev. 2017) lists among the field's questions: "Is philosophy historical in some special or deep way?" and "Is philosophy, in some sense, over – or should it be?" [C, S24]. Its section on the history of philosophy: for a long time most analytic philosophers held that the history of philosophy "had little to do with doing philosophy". "In the 1970s a more positive attitude … began to emerge". It quotes Charles Taylor (1984: 17): "'Philosophy and the history of philosophy are one'; 'we cannot do the first without also doing the second'", a view "associated with Hegel" and taken as "axiomatic" by many Continental philosophers [C, S24].
- The Collingwood dispute (B3) is itself framed in the literature as "eternal philosophical problems" [C, S20, article titles].
- Further accounts not opened: Glock, "Analytic Philosophy and History: A Mismatch?", *Mind* 117 (2008): 867–897 [C, S27, record only; content U]; Lærke, Smith and Schliesser (eds.), *Philosophy and Its History* (OUP, 2013) [U]; Rorty, Schneewind and Skinner (eds.), *Philosophy in History* (1984), which holds Rorty, MacIntyre and Taylor [U, contents].

### B8. Bergson: "tout est donné"

- *L'Évolution créatrice*, ch. I (Alcan, 1908 printing on Wikisource): "L'essence des explications mécaniques est en effet de considérer l'avenir et le passé comme calculables en fonction du présent, et de prétendre ainsi que tout est donné. Dans cette hypothèse, passé, présent et avenir seraient visibles d'un seul coup pour une intelligence surhumaine, capable d'effectuer le calcul" [C, S26].
- On finalism: "Mais le finalisme radical nous paraît tout aussi inacceptable, et pour la même raison. La doctrine de la finalité, sous sa forme extrême, telle que nous la trouvons chez Leibniz par exemple, implique que les choses et les êtres ne font que réaliser un programme une fois tracé … Comme dans l'hypothèse mécanistique, on suppose encore ici que tout est donné. Le finalisme ainsi entendu n'est qu'un mécanisme à rebours" [C, S26].
- Mitchell (Holt, 1911), pp. 37–39: "The essence of mechanical explanation, in fact, is to regard the future and the past as calculable functions of the present, and thus to claim that *all is given*"; "radical finalism is quite as unacceptable, and for the same reason … here again it is supposed that all is given. Finalism thus understood is only inverted mechanism" [C, S25; scan OCR damaged, wording confirmed against S26].
- Later in ch. I: "Parler d'un but est penser à un modèle préexistant qui n'a plus qu'à se réaliser. C'est donc supposer, au fond, que tout est donné, que l'avenir pourrait se lire dans le présent" [C, S26].
- Relevance to B: the passage is about evolution and time, not about philosophy's history. Applying it to the "finite task" question is the page's arrangement, not Bergson's claim.
