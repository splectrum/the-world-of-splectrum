# "What is philosophy, and how did it change?": research note

Purpose: to give the historical frame for the planned Real Life philosophy page and later posts. The note sets out what "philosophy" meant at each stage, where the hinge lies (philosophy becoming secondary to the sciences), and how it fractured twice: into specialisms, and into analytic and continental. The author's working position is tested against the sources, with support and counter-evidence. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (Sources list at the end). **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). Quotations are given as found in the named edition or translation.

## 1. Ancient: a way of life, with a discourse that covered everything

- **Hadot's thesis.** "Philosophical discourse, then, originates in a choice of life and an existential option—not vice versa" (*What Is Ancient Philosophy?*, Introduction) [C, S1]. And: "Philosophy then appears in its original aspect: not as a theoretical construct, but as a method for training people to live and to look at the world in a new way. It is an attempt to transform mankind" ("Spiritual Exercises", in *Philosophy as a Way of Life*) [C, S2]. Dates: *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (Études Augustiniennes, 2nd ed. 1987), translated by Chase and edited by Davidson as *Philosophy as a Way of Life* (Blackwell, 1995); *Qu'est-ce que la philosophie antique?* (Gallimard, 1995), translated by Chase as *What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (Belknap/Harvard, 2002) [C, S1, S2 imprints].
- **The Stoic division, as discourse and as life.** Hadot: the Stoics distinguished philosophy, "the lived practice of the virtues of logic, physics, and ethics", from "discourse according to philosophy", which "was in turn divided into the theory of physics, the theory of logic, and the theory of ethics" (ch. 9, opening) [C, S1]. What is taught as three parts "must now be lived and practiced inseparably" (ch. 7) [C, S1].
- **The similes.** Diogenes Laertius VII.39–40 (Hicks): "Philosophic doctrine, say the Stoics, falls into three parts: one physical, another ethical, and the third logical". Zeno was the first to make the division. Philosophy is "like an animal, Logic corresponding to the bones and sinews, Ethics to the fleshy parts, Physics to the soul"; or an egg (shell logic, white ethics, yolk physics); or "a fertile field: Logic being the encircling fence, Ethics the crop, Physics the soil or the trees". "No single part, some Stoics declare, is independent of any other part, but all blend together." Cleanthes made six parts [C, S3].
- **The division is older than the Stoics.** Sextus (*Against the Logicians* I = *M* VII.16): Plato is "virtually, the pioneer", but "those who most expressly adopt this division are Xenocrates and the Peripatetics, and also the Stoics" [C, S4]. Sextus' similes assign the parts differently (in his egg, ethics is the yolk; in Posidonius' animal, ethics is the soul) [C, S4, Greek text]. Which part sits at the core varied by teacher. That is a detail; one clause at most.
- **"Covering everything".** The three parts took in the whole of learning, and the Stoic definition of wisdom reaches as far: Clement, drawing on this tradition, writes "philosophy is the study of wisdom, and wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human" (*Stromata* I.5, ANF trans.) [C, S6].
- **"Philosophos": the Pythagoras story is reception.** Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* V.3 (§§8–9), cites "Heraclides Ponticus, a very learned man, and a pupil of Plato": Pythagoras told Leon of Phlius that he "was a philosopher", and compared life to the festival games, where the best come "merely as spectators … these men call themselves studious of wisdom, that is, philosophers" (Yonge) [C, S5]. Diogenes Laertius I.12: "the first to use the term, and to call himself a philosopher or lover of wisdom, was Pythagoras; for, said he, no man is wise, but God alone", again from Heraclides [C, S3]. Hadot: "I agree with Burkert that the anecdote told by Heraclides Ponticus … is an instance in which the Platonic notion of philosophia was projected upon Pythagoras" (citing Burkert, *Hermes* 88, 1960) [C, S1, notes]. So a page should say "the story, told by Plato's pupil Heraclides, credits Pythagoras", not "Pythagoras coined the word".

## 2. Christian antiquity and the Middle Ages: the servant

- **Origin of the figure.** Clement (c. 200) uses the mistress–handmaid image one step down: "as the encyclical branches of study contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom" (*Stromata* I.5) [C, S6]. Word test: the chapter heading "Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology" belongs to the ANF edition, not to Clement. Hadot names Philo and Origen for the same ladder ("the liberal arts were a propaedeutic for Greek philosophy, and Greek philosophy was a propaedeutic for revealed philosophy") [C, S1, ch. 11]. Philo's own text is not checked (open check 7).
- **Peter Damian** (*De divina omnipotentia*, 11th c.): when dialectic and other human arts are applied to theology, "they must serve as a maid (ancilla) serves her mistress; they must not try to adopt the leading position" (PL 145, 603B–D) [C, S7 paraphrase]. The Latin *velut ancilla dominae* is [U, search summary].
- **Aquinas, ST I q.1 a.5.** Sed contra: "aliae scientiae dicuntur ancillae huius, Prov. IX, misit ancillas suas vocare ad arcem". Ad 2: sacred doctrine "accipere potest aliquid a philosophicis disciplinis … utitur eis tanquam inferioribus et ancillis" [C, S8]. English (Dominican Fathers): "Other sciences are called the handmaidens of this one" [C, S8]. Word test: Aquinas says "other sciences" and "philosophical disciplines", in the plural. The formula *philosophia ancilla theologiae* is not his wording. Hadot cites histories of the formula (Baudoux, "Philosophia ancilla theologiae", *Antonianum* 12, 1937; Gilson, "La servante de la théologie", 1921) [C, S1, notes]. Who first wrote the exact formula is not established here (open check 8).
- **Hadot's causal account (the headline).** "Philosophy was the servant of theology from Christian antiquity onward." The spiritual exercises passed into Christian spirituality; in the thirteenth-century Faculty of Arts, philosophy "became identified with Aristotelianism", and the professor's career "consisted of commenting on the works of Aristotle". "The idea of a philosophy reduced to its conceptual content has survived to our own time … the classical, scholastic, university conception of philosophy" (ch. 11) [C, S1].
- **Hadot on the modern university, bearing on the hinge:** "University philosophy therefore remains in the same position it occupied in the Middle Ages: it is still a servant, sometimes of theology, sometimes of science" (ch. 11) [C, S1].

## 3. Early modern: the system, and the first under-labourer

- **Descartes' definition of philosophy:** "the word PHILOSOPHY signifies the study of wisdom, and … by wisdom is to be understood not merely prudence in the management of affairs, but a perfect knowledge of all that man can know" [C, S9]. **The tree:** "all Philosophy is like a tree, of which Metaphysics is the root, Physics the trunk, and all the other sciences the branches … Medicine, Mechanics, and Ethics" [C, S9]. Both come from the letter to the French translator, "serving for a preface" (Veitch). The date, 1647 for the French *Principes*, is [U].
- **Locke, Epistle to the Reader:** "The commonwealth of learning is not at this time without master-builders, whose mighty designs, in advancing the sciences, will leave lasting monuments to the admiration of posterity: but every one must not hope to be a Boyle or a Sydenham; and in an age that produces such masters as the great Huygenius and the incomparable Mr. Newton, with some others of that strain, it is ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge" [C, S10; title page MDCXC; the text's edition basis is not stated by Gutenberg, so the first-edition wording is open check 9].
- **Bearing.** Within half a century the two self-images stand side by side: philosophy as the whole tree (1647) and philosophy as the under-labourer to the natural philosophers (1690). This is a dated contrast, not a priority claim.

## 4. The residue view (Russell 1912)

- *The Problems of Philosophy*, ch. XV, "The Value of Philosophy": "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science. The whole study of the heavens, which now belongs to astronomy, was once included in philosophy; Newton's great work was called 'the mathematical principles of natural philosophy'. Similarly, the study of the human mind, which was a part of philosophy, has now been separated from philosophy and has become the science of psychology. Thus, to a great extent, the uncertainty of philosophy is more apparent than real: those questions which are already capable of definite answers are placed in the sciences, while those only to which, at present, no definite answer can be given, remain to form the residue which is called philosophy" [C, S11].
- He does not leave it there: "This is, however, only a part of the truth". Some questions "must remain insoluble", and "The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty" [C, S11].
- **The psychology example, institutionally:** the *Journal of Philosophy* was founded at Columbia in 1904 as *The Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods* and took its present title in 1921. *Mind* (1876) opened by asking whether psychology "would in time pass with general consent into the company of the sciences" [C that Wikipedia says so, S27; primary not opened]. *Philosophical Review* 1892; APA 1900 [C that Wikipedia says so, S27].

## 5. Husserl, *Crisis* (1936/1954)

- **Headline.** The crisis is not in the sciences' results but in "what science in general had meant and can mean for human existence". In the second half of the nineteenth century, the world-view of modern man let itself be determined, and blinded, by the positive sciences and the "prosperity" they produced. This meant a turning-away from the questions "decisive for a genuine humanity". "Merely fact-minded sciences make merely fact-minded people" (§2) [C in Iribarne's Spanish, S12: "Meras ciencias de hechos hacen meros seres humanos de hechos"; Carr's English (Northwestern 1970, p. 6) C only as quoted in S12b]. The German (*Bloße Tatsachenwissenschaften machen bloße Tatsachenmenschen*) is [U].
- **Publication.** Lectures in Vienna and Prague, 1935 [U]. Parts I–II were published in 1936; the full text came out in 1954 (Husserliana VI, Nijhoff); Carr's translation in 1970 [C that Wikipedia says so, S27].
- **Bearing.** Husserl's charge is the reverse of the residue view: philosophy is not left over when the sciences leave; the sciences have lost the meaning that philosophy is meant to supply. He wanted philosophy to be a rigorous science (*Philosophie als strenge Wissenschaft*, 1911 [U]), so he is not anti-science. That matters for §8.

## 6. Unity of Science

- **The *Encyclopedia*.** *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science*, vol. I no. 1 (University of Chicago Press, copyright 1938), eds Neurath, Carnap and Morris. Contributors to no. 1 included Neurath, Bohr, Dewey, Russell, Carnap and Morris. The 1955 combined edition calls it "begun in 1938 under the editorship of the late Otto Neurath" [C, S13]. Kuhn's *Structure* (1962) first appeared as a monograph in the series [C that Wikipedia says so, S27].
- **Neurath's own version is anti-system:** "An encyclopedia and not a system is the genuine model of science as a whole. An encyclopedic integration of scientific statements, with all the discrepancies and difficulties which appear, is the maximum of integration which we can achieve" ("Unified Science as Encyclopedic Integration", §VII, p. 20) [C, S13]. His §III sets "Metaphysical Comprehensiveness" (Gnostic and Scholastic systems, the *Summae* of Aquinas) against "Empiristic Synthesis" [C, S13]. The unity he wanted was meant to replace philosophy's old claim to the whole.
- **The fading.** Reisch, *How the Cold War Transformed Philosophy of Science: To the Icy Slopes of Logic* (Cambridge UP, 2005). The book "documents the political vitality of logical empiricism and Otto Neurath's Unity of Science Movement when these projects emigrated to the US in the 1930s and follows their de-politicization by a convergence of intellectual, cultural and political forces in the 1950s" [C, S14, publisher blurb; the book not opened]. Neurath died in 1945 [U on the date; C that he was dead by 1955, S13].
- **Bearing.** Reisch's account is a political and institutional cause of how philosophy of science took its technical form. It is not a case of the discipline simply following the sciences (§11).

## 7. The question's own literature

- **Ortega y Gasset, *¿Qué es filosofía?*** These were lectures in Madrid in 1929, moved from the University to the Teatro Infanta Beatriz (the text names "los oyentes del salón Rex y del teatro Infanta Beatriz") [C, S15]. They were published posthumously (©1957; the Alianza *Obras* 5 text was used) [C, S15 imprint; the English *What Is Philosophy?*, trans. Adams, Norton 1960, U].
  - One-line answer: "definir la filosofía como conocimiento del Universo", where Universe means "todo cuanto hay" ("all there is"), and the philosopher cares for each thing only as it stands "frente y junto a las demás" (facing and alongside the others) (Lección I–II) [C, S15]. The course ends by finding a new "realidad radical": "nuestra vida" [C, S15].
  - **On the hinge, in his own words** (Lección II, "Reducción y expansión de la filosofía … Imperialismo de la física"): for nearly a century Europe suffered "el «terrorismo de los laboratorios»". The philosopher "se avergonzó de no ser físico", gave up the genuinely philosophical problems, "contrayéndola a un mínimum, poniéndola humildemente al servicio de la física", and decided "que filosofía era sólo teoría del conocimiento", Kant being the first to take this stance radically. "La filosofía había quedado reducida, o poco menos, a la teoría del conocimiento. Así se titulaban la mayor parte de los libros filosóficos publicados entre 1860 y 1920" [C, S15]. His cause is the prestige of physics as the source of machines and medicine in an industrial bourgeois age [C, S15]. This is the closest primary statement of the author's "secondary, as method", made in 1929 by a philosopher arguing against it.
- **Heidegger, "Was ist das – die Philosophie?"** Lecture at Cerisy-la-Salle, Sunday 28 August 1955, which "confined [itself] resolutely to a meditation on the Greek origin of philosophy", with the starting point "The Greek word philosophia indicates the direction" [C as reported by Janicaud, S16]. Published by Neske in 1956; Kluback and Wilde's translation 1958 [C that Wikipedia says so, S27]. One-line answer: philosophy is a "corresponding" (*Entsprechen*, rendered by him as *correspondance*) to the call of the Being of beings, and philosophy is in its essence Greek [U: no primary text reachable; open check 3].
- **Deleuze and Guattari, *Qu'est-ce que la philosophie?*** (Minuit, 1991; Tomlinson and Burchell trans., Columbia 1994). SEP: they "place philosophy in relation to science and art, all three being modes of thought, with no subordination among them … Philosophy is the creation or construction of concepts … Science creates functions on a plane of reference. Art creates 'a bloc of sensation'" [C, S17]. Their own sentence ("the art of forming, inventing, and fabricating concepts") is [U]. Bearing: theirs is the late answer that explicitly refuses philosophy's subordination to science.

## 8. The analytic–continental divide

- **Dummett, *Origins of Analytical Philosophy* (Duckworth, 1993).** "The roots of analytical philosophy are the same roots as those of the phenomenological school." The two traditions "may be compared with the Rhine and the Danube, which rise quite close to one another and for a time pursue roughly parallel courses, only to diverge in utterly different directions and flow to different seas". "Analytical philosophy was born when the linguistic turn was taken" [C as quoted by Ray Monk, S18; the book itself was blocked]. The regret, as quoted by Glendinning: only by going back before the division can we hope to "re-establish communication"; "it is no use now shouting across the gulf". He also found Frege and Husserl "remarkably close in orientation despite some divergence of interests". In an interview: "we have reached a point at which it is as if we're working in different subjects" [C as quoted in S19, citing Dummett via Critchley 2001, p. 15; primary wording open check 1].
- **Glendinning, *The Idea of Continental Philosophy: A Philosophical Chronicle* (Edinburgh UP, 2006)** [C, S19 imprint].
  - Thesis: there is no "internal glue" for a Continental tradition ("doomed … for anyone: there is none"). "Continental philosophy" functions as analytic philosophy's "Other" (Figure 2.1: argument/rhetoric, clear/obscure, rigorous/vague) [C, S19].
  - **Directly on the author's "two stances toward science":** "the scientism/anti-scientism contrast does specify the most profound philosophical division in the sensibility of the contemporary philosophical culture in general, but … it cuts orthogonally across the standard analytic/Continental distinction. The reverence for the outlook, style and method of science is a regular target of some of the most influential work in analytic philosophy — and a desire to achieve the kind of status of a science is evident in a number of important texts in the Continental collection" (endnote, against David Cooper's 1996 thesis that Continental philosophy is marked by its attack on "scientism") [C, S19]. Elsewhere he locates the deepest divisions "around the relationship of philosophy to science", which "cannot be held within the frame of the analytic/Continental difference" [C, S19].
  - Derrida (1978), quoted by Glendinning: the breakdown can "make one doubt the unity of the philosophical, of the concept or project behind the word philosophy, which then constantly risks being but a homonymic lure" [C as quoted, S19].
- **The 1992 Cambridge letter.** *The Times*, Saturday 9 May 1992, "From Professor Barry Smith and others". "The University of Cambridge is to ballot on May 16". Derrida's influence "has been to a striking degree almost entirely in fields outside philosophy – in departments of film studies, for example, or of French and English literature. In the eyes of philosophers … M. Derrida's work does not meet accepted standards of clarity and rigour". There are 19 signatories, Barry Smith and eighteen others [C, 2026-10-18, the letter's text at ontology.buffalo.edu/smith/derridaletter.htm], including Quine (Harvard), Barcan Marcus (Yale), Armstrong (Sydney), Simons, Mulligan, Schuhmann, Willard and René Thom [C, S20]. Four Cambridge dons had forced the ballot with *non placet*, and the vote on 16 May went 336–204 for the degree [U, search summaries].
- **The APA "pluralist revolt", December 1979** (Eastern Division meeting, Rorty presiding). The Committee for Pluralism in Philosophy grew out of the "Saturday Group" (formed January 1978) and worked against the "domínio da filosofia analítica". The members nominated their own slate; John E. Smith (Yale) beat Adolf Grünbaum 198–165, and Rorty recognised the result, making Smith president-elect of the Eastern Division for 1980–81. The *New York Times* (Thomas Lask, 30 December 1979) reported the complaint that the APA had become "monolithic" and "intolerant" [C, S21, Toledo's Portuguese account, quoting Wilshire 2002 and the *NYT*; the *NYT* itself not opened]. The meeting's city is not checked.
- **Bearing.** The sources support a common root (Frege/Husserl) and an institutional split (1979, 1992). They do **not** support reading the split as two stances toward science without challenge. Dummett places the fork at the linguistic turn; Glendinning argues that the science question cuts across the line. The popular version of "continental = against science" has an advocate in Cooper (as Glendinning reports him), and Rovelli's 2018 complaint about "the post-Heideggerian atmosphere that dominates some philosophy departments on the 'continent', [where] ignorance of science is something to exhibit with pride" voices it from the scientists' side [C, S32; restated, not reopened this round]. A page can carry the two-stances reading only as one reading, with Glendinning's objection.

## 9. Word test: "philosophy" outside the West

- **Japanese *tetsugaku*.** Nishi Amane coined it. Its first published use was in *Hyakuichi shinron* (1874), and the term "began to spread" from that work. In 1873 Nishi explained that he had made a new word so that Western philosophy would not be confused with Confucianism. For him "philosophy" in the broad sense "included all disciplines of science" (Comte's system) [C, S23]. SEP: *-gaku* was the suffix of the new university disciplines, the equivalent of *Wissenschaft*, so academic philosophers became *tetsugakusha*, "scholars who specialize in the Wissenschaft of wisdom", and not a traditional word for the wise [C, S22]. The earlier form *kitetsugaku* and the dating of the coinage to 1862 are [U, search summary]. Chinese *zhexue* and Korean *cheolhak* use the same characters, taken from Japanese [U].
- **Indian *darśana*** ("view", "seeing"). Halbfass, *India and Europe* (SUNY, 1988), chs 15–16, is reported to argue that *darśana* traditionally names a developed system of doctrine and came to be used as the equivalent of "philosophy" only by modern Indian scholars, while *ānvīkṣikī* (investigative reasoning) is the methodological candidate [U, review summary].
- **Garfield and Van Norden**, "If Philosophy Won't Diversify, Let's Call It What It Really Is", *New York Times*, The Stone, 11 May 2016: "any department that regularly offers courses only on Western philosophy should rename itself 'Department of European and American Philosophy'" [C, S24]. Van Norden, *Taking Back Philosophy: A Multicultural Manifesto* (Columbia UP, 2017) [U, catalogue only].
- **Bearing (neutral).** The word itself travelled in the nineteenth century as the name of a Western academic discipline, coined in the very period when that discipline was being redefined against the sciences. Whether to call Indian or Chinese traditions "philosophy" is a live dispute, and the site should report it, not settle it.

## 10. Leibniz and the "last person to know everything"

- The trope has many claimants: Leibniz, Thomas Young, Joseph Leidy, Kircher. Peter Burke (*The Polymath*, Yale, 2020) "immediately dismisses such questions as an exercise in facile myth-making". He defines a polymath as someone who contributed to several distinct fields, not someone who knew everything [C as reported by Chappell, *Prospect*, 9 Dec 2020, S25]. Book titles using the phrase: Robinson, *The Last Man Who Knew Everything* (Thomas Young, 2006); Findlen (ed.), *Athanasius Kircher: The Last Man Who Knew Everything* (2004) [U].
- **Headline.** The trope is reception. A page can use it only as a phrase others apply, with Burke's objection. The `leibniz` page states it in its own voice.

## 11. Causal accounts: did philosophy follow the sciences into specialisms?

**Support.**
- *Identity crisis, mid-nineteenth century.* SEP ("Neo-Kantianism", Heis 2018) cites Schnädelbach (*Philosophy in Germany 1831–1933*, CUP 1984, p. 5) for the "identity crisis" philosophy faced: "Given the collapse of the conception of philosophy shared by post-Kantian speculative idealists, and the concurrent spectacular advances made in the sciences, philosophers were left questioning what (if anything) was left as the distinctive subject matter and method of philosophy" (citing Beiser 2014, ch. 1) [C, S26].
- *Philosophy as method on the fact of science.* For the Marburg school, "philosophy begins with a fact — paradigmatically, the fact of science — and investigates the conditions that make that fact possible". Neo-Kantian philosophy of science "is the logic of the sciences", and each school had its "philosophy of the special sciences" (physics for Marburg, history for the Southwest school) [C, S26]. This is the earliest well-attested programme of philosophy-of-X as a reply to the sciences.
- *Ortega 1929* on philosophy "reduced … to theory of knowledge" and "humbly in the service of physics" (§7) [C, S15]. *Hadot*: university philosophy "still a servant … sometimes of science" (§2) [C, S1]. *Carnap 1932*: "What remains is not statements, nor a theory, nor a system, but only a method: the method of logical analysis" [C, S28; restated, not reopened this round].
- *Institutions.* The research university of the late nineteenth century made philosophy "a discipline", and the old unity "shattered under the weight of increasing specialization by the turn of the 20th century" (Frodeman and Briggle, *NYT* 2016) [C, S29; restated, not reopened this round]. Kuklick (*A History of Philosophy in America 1720–2000*, OUP 2001) is reported to date a "professional" period from 1912 to 2000. Psychology declared independence, the social sciences took over philosophy's advisory role, and philosophers "had to discover a new identity", becoming more refined but "more fragmented and remote from the public" [U, search summary of reviews; book and review blocked].
- *Recency of the specialisms.* Philosophy of biology "only emerged as an independent field of philosophy in the 1960s and 1970s" [C that Wikipedia says so, S27]. The journal *Philosophy of Science* dates from 1934 [C, same]. Post-1945 university expansion as the driver is [U].

**Counter-evidence.**
- *The genitive form is old and comes from the system.* Voltaire coined "philosophie de l'histoire" (1765); Hegel's *Philosophy of Right* appeared in 1820 [C that Wikipedia says so, S27]. In German idealism, philosophy-of-X names a part of one system, not a reply to a separate science. The form did not wait for the sciences to split.
- *Internal division is ancient.* Logic, physics and ethics (Xenocrates, the Stoics; six parts for Cleanthes) [C, S3, S4]. What is new is institutional separation (departments, journals, careers), not division as such.
- *The lag.* Biology was a separate science by the mid-nineteenth century, but philosophy of biology became a field only in the 1960s–70s. A lag of about a century points to academic expansion and professional incentives at least as much as to philosophy "following" each science. This is my inference from dated items, not a source's claim.
- *Politics, not only science.* Reisch shows the form of US philosophy of science shaped by Cold War pressures (§6) [C, S14 blurb].
- *Two complaints, not one.* Kitcher (2011, 2023) objects to an inward-turned "core" and counts the outward-facing philosophies of the sciences as the living part. So "specialised" and "turned inward" are separate charges [C, S30; restated, not reopened this round].

**Finding.** The literature supports the hinge: in the nineteenth century philosophy redefined itself as theory of knowledge or method in answer to the sciences (Schnädelbach via SEP, the neo-Kantians, Ortega, Russell). It supports institutional specialisation from the late nineteenth century, and more strongly after 1945. It supports "philosophy followed the sciences into specialisms" only in part. The genitive form is older, internal division is ancient, the timing points to institutions, and one specialism (philosophy of science) was shaped by politics. "Derived and recent" fits the institutional fracture. It does not fit the form.

## 12. Synthesis (proposal; built only from [C] items above; the arrangement is mine)

The meanings of "philosophy", in sequence:
1. **Love of wisdom as a way of life**, whose taught discourse (logic, physics, ethics) covered everything there was to know (Hadot; Diogenes Laertius; Sextus). The coinage story is Platonic reception projected onto Pythagoras.
2. **Servant**: of revealed wisdom, then of theology (Clement's ladder, Damian's *ancilla*, Aquinas' "philosophical disciplines" as handmaids). The way of life passed to Christian spirituality, and philosophy became theoretical commentary in the university (Hadot).
3. **The whole system** again: "perfect knowledge of all that man can know", a tree with physics for its trunk (Descartes 1647). In the same half-century came the first under-labourer to the natural philosophers (Locke 1690).
4. **The hinge (nineteenth century).** The natural philosophers took a new name, "scientist" (Whewell: proposed 1833, first printed 1834; S31, restated). Philosophy faced an "identity crisis" and narrowed to theory of knowledge and method on "the fact of science" (Schnädelbach, the neo-Kantians, Ortega's 1860–1920 "teoría del conocimiento"). Russell (1912) wrote the residue view in so many words.
5. **Two fractures (twentieth century).**
   - (a) **Specialisation** inside the research university. It was institutional, picked up after 1945, and philosophy of X became fields in its own right (philosophy of biology 1960s–70s). The genitive form was older.
   - (b) **Analytic and continental**, from a common root (Frege/Husserl, Dummett's two rivers) to institutional quarrels (1979, 1992). The science question runs through this divide but, on Glendinning's reading, across it, not along it.
6. **Counter-answers** to the residue view: Husserl's "fact-minded" sciences (1936), Ortega's "todo cuanto hay" (1929), and Deleuze and Guattari's three modes of thought "with no subordination" (1991). The non-Western word test shows "philosophy" itself travelling as the name of a nineteenth-century Western discipline (Nishi 1874).

Of the working position: the meaning changed (strongly supported). Philosophy went secondary, as method, with the sciences' rise (supported, and stated by Locke, Ortega, the neo-Kantians, Russell, Carnap and Hadot). The fracture into specialisms is derived and recent (supported for the institutional fracture, not for the form). The analytic–continental divide as two stances toward science is contested: hold it as one reading, with Glendinning against it.
