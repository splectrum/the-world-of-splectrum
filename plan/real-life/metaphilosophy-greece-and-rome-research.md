# Metaphilosophy, Philosophy in Greece and Rome: research note

Purpose: to source the section "Philosophy in Greece and Rome" (a landing page and eight pages, closing on Byzantium), which replaces the present single page `docs/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/ancient.md`. For each page it gathers the Greek and Roman texts' own answers to the section's three questions: what the activity of philosophy is, what its point is, and how it is done. Modern scholars are used for facts only (dates, word history, which text says what, translations). Pierre Hadot's thesis "philosophy as a way of life" does not frame anything here. The ancients' own lines make that case, and Hadot appears only where he gives a fact. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified. "My rendering" = my own English from the Greek or Latin I fetched.

**Access.** Perseus, Wikisource (English, Latin, Greek), the Latin Library, Gutenberg, archive.org, tertullian.org, New Advent, Fordham's Medieval Sourcebook, the MIT Internet Classics Archive and the SEP were all reachable. Perseus was intermittently "busy" on large pages. érudit.org (Chroust 1972) is behind a bot wall. Bury's Loeb Sextus vol. III is access-restricted on archive.org. Greek OCR of the Google-scanned *CAG* volumes is unusable. The University of Toronto scan of *CAG* 18.1 (Elias) is readable, with OCR errors.

## 1. Landing page: the words and the Greeks' own sortings

### The word *philosophia* and how late it came

- **Hadot, for the fact only:** "The words belonging to the philosophia family did not in fact appear until the fifth century B.C., and the term philosophia itself was not defined until the fourth century B.C., by Plato. Aristotle, however, and with him the entire tradition of the history of philosophy, applied the word 'philosophers' to the first Greek thinkers" [C, S50 p. 9]. On the coinage story Hadot agrees with Burkert that the Heraclides anecdote "is an instance in which the Platonic notion of philosophia was projected upon Pythagoras" [C, S50 notes]. Hadot glosses *philo-* compounds as "the disposition of a person who found his interest, pleasure, or raison de vivre in devoting himself to a given activity" [C, S50]. Use that as word history, not as framing.
- **Herodotus 1.30**, Croesus to Solon: ὡς φιλοσοφέων γῆν πολλὴν θεωρίης εἵνεκεν ἐπελήλυθας [C, S1]. Godley renders it "how as one who loves learning you have traveled much of the world for the sake of seeing it" [C, S1]. Solon also travels "to see the world", τῆς θεωρίης … εἵνεκεν, in the same chapter [C, S1]. **Word test:** *philosopheōn* is "loving learning" (Godley), and *theōriē* is seeing the world. The verb and *theōria* share their earliest attestation here.
- **Thucydides 2.40.1**, Pericles: φιλοκαλοῦμέν τε γὰρ μετ᾽ εὐτελείας καὶ φιλοσοφοῦμεν ἄνευ μαλακίας [C, S2]. Crawley renders it "We cultivate refinement without extravagance and knowledge without effeminacy" [C, S2], and Hobbes "and to philosophy, and yet without mollification of the mind" [C, S2]. **Word test:** Crawley puts "knowledge" for the verb *philosophoumen*; Hobbes keeps "philosophy". Hadot notes that both verbs are *philo-* compounds [C, S50].
- **The coinage story, as the ancients told it.**
  - Cicero, *Tusc.* 5.8–9, from "Ponticus Heraclides … auditor Platonis": Leon of Phlius asks what art Pythagoras professes. He answers "artem quidem se scire nullam, sed esse philosophum". He explains with the festival: some come for the crown, some to buy and sell, "and they were by far the best, whose aim was neither applause nor profit, but who came merely as spectators" (Yonge). So in life some "earnestly look into the nature of things; and these men call themselves studious of wisdom, that is, philosophers" (*sapientiae studiosos — id est enim philosophos*) [C, S30 Latin; S31 Yonge].
  - Diogenes Laertius 1.12: "the first to use the term, and to call himself a philosopher or lover of wisdom, was Pythagoras; for, said he, no man is wise, but God alone". He cites Heraclides' *On the Woman without Breath* [C, S10 Hicks; S11 Greek: μηδένα γὰρ εἶναι σοφὸν … ἀλλ᾽ ἢ θεόν]. In DL the conversation is "at Sicyon … with Leon, who was the prince of that city or of Phlius"; Cicero puts it at Phlius [C, S10, S30]. That is a detail; leave it out. DL 8.8 gives the festival comparison again, from Sosicrates [C, S10].
  - The same thought in Plato's dialogues, Socrates in *Phaedrus* 278d: "the epithet 'wise' is too great and befits God alone; but the name 'philosopher,' that is, 'lover of wisdom,' or something of the sort would be more fitting" (Fowler) [C, S3]. And *Symposium* 204a: θεῶν οὐδεὶς φιλοσοφεῖ οὐδ᾽ ἐπιθυμεῖ σοφὸς γενέσθαι, "no god philosophises or desires to become wise", nor do the ignorant; Eros is "a friend of wisdom … between wise and ignorant" (204b; Perseus credits Fowler, see open checks) [C, S3, S4].
- **DL claims the word for Greece**, 1.4: "And thus it was from the Greeks that philosophy took its rise: its very name refuses to be translated into foreign speech" (Hicks). The Greek is ἧς καὶ αὐτὸ τὸ ὄνομα τὴν βάρβαρον ἀπέστραπται προσηγορίαν [C, S10, S11]. He has just reported (1.1) those who said philosophy began "among the barbarians", the Magi, Chaldaeans, Gymnosophists and Druids [C, S10]. That is the Greeks' own dispute about where philosophy began.
- **The word contested in its own century.** Isocrates, *Antidosis* 270: "I hold that what some people call philosophy is not entitled to that name". At 271: "since it is not in the nature of man to attain a science by the possession of which we can know positively what we should do or what we should say … I hold that man to be wise who is able by his powers of conjecture to arrive generally at the best course, and I hold that man to be a philosopher who occupies himself with the studies from which he will most quickly gain that kind of insight" (Norlin) [C, S5]. **Headline:** in the fourth century *philosophia* was a contested name, and Isocrates claimed it for his own education against Plato's school. Hadot notes Isocrates' *Panegyricus*: Athens revealed philosophy to the world [C, S50]. The *Panegyricus* passage itself is not opened [U].

### *Historia peri physeos*, *sophia*, *theōria*, *scholē*

- **historia peri physeos.** *Phaedo* 96a: "When I was young, Cebes, I was tremendously eager for the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature" (Fowler) [C, S3]. **Word test:** the Greek has *sophia* ("wisdom") called *peri physeos historia*; Jowett's "department of philosophy" adds the word "philosophy" [U for Jowett here; not reopened]. SEP: "lovers of wisdom must be inquirers into many things" (Heraclitus B35); there "philosophos, does not have the special sense that it acquires in the works of Plato and Aristotle" [C, S51].
- **sophia** in the Greeks' own definitions:
  - Aristotle: "what is called Wisdom is concerned with the primary causes and principles" (*Met.* A.1 981b28, Tredennick) [C, S6].
  - Seneca, Ep. 89.4–7: "Wisdom is the perfect good of the human mind; philosophy is the love of wisdom, and the endeavour to attain it … it acknowledges by its very name the object of its love". The Latin is *Sapientia perfectum bonum est mentis humanae; philosophia sapientiae amor est et adfectatio*. "Wisdom is that which the Greeks call σοφία", and the Romans once used *sapientia* where they now say "philosophy" (Gummere) [C, S40 English; S41 Latin].
  - The old definition of wisdom, "the knowledge of things divine and human", is ascribed to the Stoics by the *Placita* (§6) [C, S27]. Cicero gives it as defined "by the ancient philosophers" (*De officiis* 2.5: *Sapientia autem est, ut a veteribus philosophis definitum est, rerum divinarum et humanarum causarumque, quibus eae res continentur, scientia*; *philosophia … studium sapientiae*) [C, S36]. This replaces the present page's Clement line.
- **theōria** (spectating):
  - Herodotus 1.30 (above).
  - Pythagoras's spectators at the festival (Cicero, DL).
  - *Republic* 475e, the true philosophers are τοὺς τῆς ἀληθείας … φιλοθεάμονας, "those for whom the truth is the spectacle of which they are enamored" (Shorey) [C, S4].
  - The *Protrepticus* via Iamblichus: Pythagoras, asked what we are born for, said "to be an observer of the sky", and called himself "an observer of nature" (θεωρὸν … τῆς φύσεως) "and it was for the sake of this that he had passed into his way of life" (Hutchinson–Johnson) [C, S8].
- **scholē** (leisure):
  - Aristotle, *Met.* A.1 981b20–25: the sciences not aimed at pleasure or necessity arose "first in those places where men had leisure", the mathematical arts in Egypt "because there the priestly class was allowed leisure" (ἐσχόλασαν) [C, S6 Tredennick, Greek].
  - *Theaetetus* 172c–d: philosophers "always have … leisure (σχολή), and they talk at their leisure in peace … they do not care at all whether their talk is long or short, if only they attain the truth", while the lawcourt men speak against the water-clock (Fowler) [C, S3].
  - *NE* X.7 1177b4: ἀσχολούμεθα γὰρ ἵνα σχολάζωμεν, "we do business in order that we may have leisure" (Rackham) [C, S7].
  - Epictetus calls his school τὸ τοῦ φιλοσόφου σχολεῖον (*Disc.* 3.23.30) [C, S19]. That *scholē* gave Latin *schola* and "school" is [U] (dictionary fact, not opened).

### The Greeks' own sortings of philosophy

- **Aristotle's three kinds of knowledge.**
  - *Topics* VI.6: knowledge "is classed as speculative, practical and productive; and each of these denotes a relation: for it speculates upon something, and produces something and does something" (Pickard-Cambridge) [C, S9]. The Bekker line (145a15) is [U].
  - *Met.* E.1 1025b25: "if every intellectual activity is either practical or productive or speculative, physics will be a speculative science". 1026a18–19: "there will be three speculative philosophies: mathematics, physics, and theology", and "the speculative sciences … are to be preferred to the other sciences, and 'theology' to the other speculative sciences" (Tredennick) [C, S6].
- **The Stoic three parts and their images.**
  - DL 7.39: "Philosophic doctrine, say the Stoics, falls into three parts: one physical, another ethical, and the third logical", first so divided by Zeno of Citium (Hicks) [C, S10]. The Greek is Τριμερῆ φασὶν εἶναι τὸν κατὰ φιλοσοφίαν λόγον [C, S11]. **Word test:** what is divided is "the account according to philosophy" (*ho kata philosophian logos*); "doctrine" is Hicks's word.
  - DL 7.40: "Philosophy, they say, is like an animal, Logic corresponding to the bones and sinews, Ethics to the fleshy parts, Physics to the soul"; an egg (shell logic, white ethics, yolk physics); "a fertile field: Logic being the encircling fence, Ethics the crop, Physics the soil or the trees"; "a city strongly walled and governed by reason". "No single part … is independent of any other part, but all blend together" [C, S10].
  - **DL 7.41, the ancients' own line for what the present page takes from Hadot:** "others say that these are divisions not of philosophic exposition, but of philosophy itself: so, for instance, Zeno of Tarsus" (Hicks). The Greek is ἄλλοι δ᾽ οὐ τοῦ λόγου ταῦτα μέρη φασίν, ἀλλ᾽ αὐτῆς τῆς φιλοσοφίας [C, S10, S11]. The same passage reports the order of teaching as disputed (logic → physics → ethics for Zeno and Chrysippus; physics first for Panaetius and Posidonius), and Cleanthes' six parts [C, S10].
  - Sextus, *Adv. Math.* 7.16–19: of those dividing into physics, ethics and logic, "Plato is, virtually, the pioneer", "but those who most expressly adopt this division are Xenocrates and the Peripatetics, and also the Stoics". He gives the garden, the egg, and Posidonius' animal, chosen "on the ground that the parts of Philosophy are inseparable one from another" (Bury) [C, S24]. Sextus and DL assign the parts of the egg and the animal differently; that is a detail, so give one version.
  - Earlier sorting: some made philosophy one part or two (7.2–15) [C, S24]. Some begin with physics because "even up till now the earliest philosophers have been called 'physicists'", others with ethics, others with logic (the Epicureans begin with the canonic) (7.20–22) [C, S24].
- **Diogenes Laertius's survey, Greece's own map of its schools** (DL 1.13–21, Hicks):
  - The sages (1.13).
  - "philosophy, the pursuit of wisdom, has had a twofold origin; it started with Anaximander on the one hand, with Pythagoras on the other" (Φιλοσοφίας δὲ δύο γεγόνασιν ἀρχαί). The Ionian and Italian schools follow, with their successions down to Clitomachus, Chrysippus and Theophrastus, and to Epicurus (1.13–15).
  - "dogmatists and sceptics", ἐφεκτικοί, "those who suspend" (1.16).
  - Schools named from cities, places, circumstances, nicknames, temperaments, conceits and teachers (1.17).
  - Three parts, physics, ethics and dialectic. "Physics flourished down to the time of Archelaus; ethics … started with Socrates; while dialectic goes as far back as Zeno of Elea". There are ten ethical schools (1.18). Hippobotus counts nine sects (1.19).
  - Whether the Pyrrhonians are a sect at all (1.20). Potamo's Eclectic school (1.21).
  - The summary at 1.20 is αἵδε μὲν ἀρχαὶ καὶ διαδοχαὶ καὶ τοσαῦτα μέρη καὶ τόσαι φιλοσοφίας αἱρέσεις [C, S10, S11]. **Word test:** *diadochai* is "successions", which Hicks renders "subsequent developments"; *haireseis* is "sects/choices", Hicks's "sects". DL cites the genre's titles: Sotion's *Succession of Philosophers* (1.1), and Alexander's *Successions* (9.61) [C, S10].
  - DL 3.56: philosophy grew like tragedy. "in early times it discoursed on one subject only, namely physics, then Socrates added the second subject, ethics, and Plato the third, dialectics, and so brought philosophy to perfection" [C, S10].
- **Other ancient sortings to note on the landing page.**
  - Sextus's three kinds of philosopher (PH 1.1–4; §6).
  - Sextus's Academies, "three … some, however, add as a fourth" (PH 1.220) [C, S23].
  - Cicero's *Academica* 1 (Varro's history of the Old Academy, §7).
  - Varro's 288 possible sects, reported by Augustine (*City of God* 19.1, §7).
  - Aristotle's *Metaphysics* A as a review of predecessors (§2).
  - The Stoic *Placita* proem (§6).
  - Longinus's list of philosophers who wrote and who did not, quoted in Porphyry's *Life of Plotinus* 20 [C, S45].
  - That Diels coined "doxography" (*Doxographi Graeci*, 1879) is [U].

## 2. The inquiry into nature

- **Aristotle's review as the first history.** *Met.* A.3 983b1–3: "let us avail ourselves of the evidence of those who have before us approached the investigation of reality and philosophized about Truth … we shall either discover some other kind of cause, or have more confidence in those which we have just described" (Tredennick) [C, S6]. Ross: "Of the first philosophers, then, most thought the principles which were of the nature of matter were the only principles of all things"; Thales "the founder of this type of philosophy" [C, S6 Ross]. Wonder: "It is through wonder that men now begin and originally began to philosophize; wondering in the first place at obvious perplexities, and then … about the changes of the moon and of the sun, about the stars and about the origin of the universe" (982b12–17, Tredennick; Greek διὰ γὰρ τὸ θαυμάζειν … ἤρξαντο φιλοσοφεῖν at 982b12–13) [C, S6].
- **Heraclitus** (Burnet; DK numbers checked against the Wikisource concordance):
  - B35, "Men that love wisdom must be acquainted with very many things indeed"; the word *philosophous* is doubted (see §0).
  - B40, "The learning of many things teacheth not understanding, else would it have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, and again Xenophanes and Hekataios".
  - B101, "I have sought for myself" [C, S52, S53].
  - B107 (eyes and ears) is not reopened [U].
- **Xenophanes** B18: "The gods have not revealed all things to men from the beginning, but by seeking they find in time what is better". B34: "There never was nor will be a man who has certain knowledge about the gods and about all the things I speak of" (Burnet) [C, S52].
- **Parmenides** B7: "nor let habit by its much experience force thee to cast upon this way a wandering eye or sounding ear or tongue; but judge by argument the much disputed proof uttered by me" (Burnet) [C, S52].
- **The atomists.** Democritus, in Dionysius of Alexandria quoted by Eusebius, *PE* 14.27: "he would rather discover one single law of causation than receive the kingdom of Persia" (Gifford) [C, S48]. The DK number (68B118) is [U]. The quoting author is hostile, "as the story goes". For the later atomist answer to why study nature, see Epicurus's KD 11 (§6): physics is for freedom from fear.
- **The point, in anecdote.** Pythagoras and Anaxagoras on what we are born for: "to be an observer of the sky", "the sky and the stars around it, as well as moon and sun" (Iamblichus, *Protr.* IX, 51.6–15 Pistelli, Hutchinson–Johnson) [C, S8]. The *EE* 1216a version is already on the site.
- **The label.** SEP: "Presocratic" was "coined in the eighteenth century, was made current by Hermann Diels in the nineteenth", meant to contrast Socrates' moral interest with his predecessors. "Some scholars now deliberately avoid the term"; "philosophers" "is almost certainly not how they could have described themselves" [C, S51]. Eberhard 1788 and Laks–Most's avoidance of the term are [U] (not reopened in this round).

## 3. The sophists and Socrates

- **The two names.**
  - DL 1.12: "Sophists was another name for the wise men, and not only for philosophers but for the poets also" [C, S10].
  - Protagoras in Plato: "sophistry is an ancient art", earlier practitioners disguised it as poetry, rites, or athletics (*Prot.* 316d, Lamb) [C, S3].
  - Aristotle: "sophistry is Wisdom in appearance only"; "sophistry and dialectic are concerned with the same class of subjects as philosophy, but this differs from dialectic in the nature of the faculty required and from sophistic in respect of the purpose of the philosophic life" (*Met.* Γ.2 1004b17–26, Ross). The Greek is τῆς δὲ τοῦ βίου τῇ προαιρέσει [C, S6 Greek, Ross]. **Word test:** *prohairesis tou biou* is "the choice of life" (my rendering). Ross gives "purpose of the philosophic life" and Tredennick "outlook on life" [C, S6]. This is Aristotle's own line separating philosopher from sophist by the life chosen. It does the work the Hadot frame did, in Aristotle's words.
  - *Soph. El.* 1, 165a21–23: "the art of the sophist is the semblance of wisdom without the reality, and the sophist is one who makes money from an apparent but unreal wisdom" (Pickard-Cambridge) [C, S9]. The Bekker lines are [U].
- **Protagoras's profession**, *Prot.* 318e–319a: "That learning consists of good judgement in his own affairs, showing how best to order his own home; and in the affairs of his city, showing how he may have most influence on public affairs both in speech and in action". Socrates: "you appear to be speaking of the civic science, and undertaking to make men good citizens". "That, Socrates … is exactly the purport of what I profess" (Lamb). The Greek of 318e is εὐβουλία περὶ τῶν οἰκείων [C, S3]. The Greek of "civic science" (πολιτικὴ τέχνη) is [U].
- **Isocrates' *philosophia*** (§1) belongs here as the third claimant to the name.
- **Socrates turns philosophy to life.**
  - Cicero, *Tusc.* 5.10: *Socrates autem primus philosophiam devocavit e caelo et in urbibus conlocavit et in domus etiam introduxit et coegit de vita et moribus rebusque bonis et malis quaerere*. Yonge: "Socrates was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil" [C, S30, S31].
  - Varro in Cicero, *Acad.* 1.15: Socrates "avocavisse philosophiam" from hidden things "et ad vitam communem adduxisse"; the heavens are beyond our knowledge or, if known, "nihil tamen ad bene vivendum" [C, S34].
  - Xenophon, *Mem.* 1.1.11, 16: "He did not even discuss that topic so favoured by other talkers, 'the Nature of the Universe' … His own conversation was ever of human things. The problems he discussed were, What is godly, what is ungodly; what is beautiful, what is ugly; what is just, what is unjust …" (Marchant) [C, S12].
  - Aristotle: Socrates was "disregarding the physical universe and confining his study to moral questions", "the first to concentrate upon definition" (*Met.* A.6 987b1–4). "There are two innovations which may fairly be ascribed to Socrates: inductive reasoning and general definition" (M.4 1078b27–29, Tredennick) [C, S6].
- **Plato's Socrates on the activity.**
  - *Apology* 21d: "what I do not know I do not think I know either". 29d: "I shall never give up philosophy or stop exhorting you". 29e: "I shall question and examine and cross-examine him". 30a: "the god commands me to do this". 38a: "to talk every day about virtue … is the greatest good to man, and … the unexamined life is not worth living" (Fowler) [C, S3].
  - *Gorgias* 500c, the dispute stated as a choice of lives: "what course of life is best (ὅντινα χρὴ τρόπον ζῆν); whether it should be that to which you invite me … speaking in Assembly and practicing rhetoric … or this life of philosophy (τὸν βίον τὸν ἐν φιλοσοφίᾳ); and what makes the difference between these two" (Lamb) [C, S3 English, Greek].
  - Callicles' attack, 484c–485a: "philosophy … is a charming thing, if a man has to do with it moderately in his younger days; but if he continues to spend his time on it too long, it is ruin to any man" (Lamb) [C, S3]. That is the opposing voice, and it is part of the subject.
- **The elenchus as the method.** *Apology* 29e above, and Cicero's account of Socrates' way (Acad. 1.16): "nihil affirmet ipse refellat alios, nihil se scire dicat nisi id ipsum" [C, S34]. The scholarly term "elenchus" for the method is modern usage of Plato's verb ἐλέγχειν [U].

## 4. Plato and the Academy

- **Wonder as the beginning.** *Theaetetus* 155d: μάλα γὰρ φιλοσόφου τοῦτο τὸ πάθος, τὸ θαυμάζειν· οὐ γὰρ ἄλλη ἀρχὴ φιλοσοφίας ἢ αὕτη. Fowler: "this feeling of wonder shows that you are a philosopher, since wonder is the only beginning of philosophy" [C, S3].
- **Practice for dying.** *Phaedo* 64a: "those who pursue philosophy aright study nothing but dying and being dead" (οὐδὲν ἄλλο αὐτοὶ ἐπιτηδεύουσιν ἢ ἀποθνῄσκειν τε καὶ τεθνάναι). 67e: "the true philosophers practice dying (ἀποθνῄσκειν μελετῶσι), and death is less terrible to them than to any other men" (Fowler) [C, S3]. Cicero turns it into Latin: *Tota enim philosophorum vita … commentatio mortis est* (*Tusc.* 1.74) [C, S30].
- **Becoming like god.** *Theaetetus* 176b: φυγὴ δὲ ὁμοίωσις θεῷ κατὰ τὸ δυνατόν· ὁμοίωσις δὲ δίκαιον καὶ ὅσιον μετὰ φρονήσεως γενέσθαι. Fowler: "to escape is to become like God, so far as this is possible; and to become like God is to become righteous and holy and wise" [C, S3]. The passage closes the leisure digression that begins at 172c, where the philosopher is a free man with leisure [C, S3 for 172c–d and 176b; the digression's end-point is U]. Both 64a and 176b later become two of the six definitions (§8).
- **The lover of the whole of wisdom.** *Republic* 475b: "the lover of wisdom … desires all wisdom, not a part and a part not". 475e: the true philosophers are lovers of the spectacle of truth (Shorey) [C, S4].
- **Philosopher-rulers.** *Republic* 473d: "unless … our kings and rulers take to the pursuit of philosophy seriously and adequately, and there is a conjunction of these two things, political power and philosophic intelligence … there can be no cessation of troubles … for our states, nor, I fancy, for the human race either" (Shorey) [C, S4].
- **Dialectic.** *Republic* 533c: "is not dialectics the only process of inquiry that advances in this manner, doing away with hypotheses, up to the first principle itself in order to find confirmation there?" (Shorey) [C, S4]. The site's present Jowett wording is [U here].
- **What cannot be written.** *Seventh Letter* 341c–d: "it does not at all admit of verbal expression like other studies, but, as a result of continued application to the subject itself and communion therewith, it is brought to birth in the soul on a sudden, as light that is kindled by a leaping spark" (Bury) [C, S4]. The letter's authenticity is disputed [U as a fact; widely known]. That is a headline-level caveat: one clause.
- **The Academy's later history, as the ancients sorted it.**
  - Plato's successors and the Academies (DL 1.14, 1.19; Sextus PH 1.220).
  - Arcesilaus: "With him begins the Middle Academy; he was the first to suspend his judgement owing to the contradictions of opposing arguments. He was also the first to argue on both sides of a question" (DL 4.28) [C, S10].
  - Cicero (Varro speaking), *Acad.* 1.17: the Academy and the Peripatos were one teaching "qui rebus congruentes nominibus differebant", agreeing in substance and differing in name [C, S34].

## 5. Aristotle and the Lyceum

- **The desire to know.** *Met.* A.1 980a21: "All men naturally desire knowledge. An indication of this is our esteem for the senses; for apart from their use we esteem them for their own sake" (Tredennick) [C, S6].
- **For its own sake; free.** *Met.* A.2 982b19–28: "if it was to escape ignorance that men studied philosophy, it is obvious that they pursued science for the sake of knowledge, and not for any practical utility … speculation of this kind began with a view to recreation and pastime, at a time when practically all the necessities of life were already supplied … just as we call a man independent who exists for himself and not for another, so we call this the only independent science, since it alone exists for itself". 983a10–11: "although all other sciences are more necessary than this, none is more excellent" (Tredennick) [C, S6]. Ross's "in order to know, and not for any utilitarian end" [C, S6 Ross]. Also from A.2: "the myth-lover is in a sense a philosopher, since myths are composed of wonders" (982b18–19) [C, S6].
- **Contemplation as the highest life.** *NE* X.7 (Rackham) [C, S7]:
  - "it is the activity of this part of us in accordance with the virtue proper to it that will constitute perfect happiness; and … this activity is the activity of contemplation" (1177a16–18).
  - "philosophy or the pursuit of wisdom contains pleasures of marvellous purity and permanence" (1177a25–26).
  - Contemplation "may be held to be the only activity that is loved for its own sake" (1177b1–2).
  - "Nor ought we to obey those who enjoin that a man should have man's thoughts and a mortal the thoughts of mortality, but we ought so far as possible to achieve immortality, and do all that man may to live in accordance with the highest thing in him" (1177b31–34).
- **But ethics is for acting.** *NE* II.2 1103b26–29: "we are not investigating the nature of virtue for the sake of knowing what it is, but in order that we may become good, without which result our investigation would be of no use" (Rackham) [C, S7]. **Word test:** Rackham's "unlike the other branches of philosophy" renders οὐ θεωρίας ἕνεκά ἐστιν ὥσπερ αἱ ἄλλαι, "is not for the sake of *theōria* as the others are" [C, S7 Greek]. The word "philosophy" is the translator's.
- **The *Protrepticus*** (exhortation to philosophy; lost, reconstructed) (Hutchinson–Johnson) [C, S8]:
  - "philosophy is, as we think, both a possession and a use of wisdom, and wisdom is among the greatest goods" (Iamblichus, *Protr.* VI, 40.1–3).
  - The argument preserved by later commentators: "if you should do philosophy, you should do philosophy, and if you should not do philosophy, then you should do philosophy. Therefore in every case you should do philosophy … since to investigate is the cause of philosophy". This is from Elias, *Prolegomena* p. 3.17–23 Busse; Alexander of Aphrodisias, *in Top.* 149.9–15, is the earlier witness.
  - Lactantius reports Cicero's *Hortensius* borrowing it: "it is up to philosophers to discuss what should be done, or not done, in life".
  - The editors date the work "soon after 353 BCE, probably" [C as S8 editors]. That it is a reconstruction is a headline-level caveat.
- **Method from what is believed** (*NE* VII.1, *Topics* I.1–2) is already on the site; not reopened [U here].
- **The Lyceum and the name "Peripatetic":** "Peripatetici dicti sunt, quia disputabant inambulantes in Lycio" (Cicero, *Acad.* 1.17) [C, S34]; and DL 1.17 "from incidental circumstances, as the Peripatetics" [C, S10].

## 6. The Hellenistic schools

**Epicurus.**
- *Letter to Menoeceus*, DL 10.122: Μήτε νέος τις ὢν μελλέτω φιλοσοφεῖν, μήτε γέρων ὑπάρχων κοπιάτω φιλοσοφῶν [C, S13]. Hicks: "Let no one be slow to seek wisdom when he is young nor weary in the search thereof when he is grown old. For no age is too early or too late for the health of the soul. And to say that the season for studying philosophy has not yet come, or that it is past and gone, is like saying that the season for happiness is not yet or that it is now no more" [C, S10]. **Word test:** the verb is *philosophein*, "to philosophise"; "seek wisdom" is Hicks's.
- Physics for peace of mind:
  - *Letter to Pythocles*, DL 10.85: "knowledge of celestial phenomena … has no other end in view than peace of mind and firm conviction" (Hicks) [C, S10].
  - KD 11, DL 10.142–143: "If we had never been molested by alarms at celestial and atmospheric phenomena, nor by the misgiving that death somehow affects us, nor by neglect of the proper limits of pains and desires, we should have had no need to study natural science" (Hicks). The Greek ends οὐκ ἂν προσεδεόμεθα φυσιολογίας [C, S10, S14]. **Word test:** *physiologia* is "natural science" in Hicks.
- The four first maxims, KD 1–4 (DL 10.139–140): the blessed being has no trouble; "Death is nothing to us"; the limit of pleasure is the removal of pain; pain does not last (Hicks) [C, S10]. **The "fourfold remedy"** (*tetrapharmakos*) as a name is Philodemus's (PHerc. 1005, col. 4) [U, not opened]. Give the four maxims from KD 1–4 and the name only as Philodemus's.
- Vatican Saying 54: "We must not pretend to study philosophy, but study it in reality: for it is not the appearance of health that we need, but real health" (Bailey) [C, S15].
- **The "empty word" saying.** Porphyry, *Ad Marcellam* 31: "Vain is the word of that philosopher who can ease no mortal trouble. As there is no profit in the physician's art unless it cure the diseases of the body, so there is none in philosophy, unless it expel the troubles of the soul" (Zimmern 1896) [C, S47]. Bailey: Κενὸς ἐκείνου φιλοσόφου λόγος, ὑφ᾽ οὗ μηδὲν πάθος ἀνθρώπου θεραπεύεται … "Vain is the word of a philosopher which does not heal any suffering of man" (fr. D.54, "Fragments from uncertain sources: On philosophy") [C, S15]. **Attribution, a headline-level caveat:** Bailey's commentary has "(Usener 221) is another unassigned quotation in Porphyry, Stobaeus refers it to Pythagoras, but its likeness to Sent. Vat. [54] and other Epicurean sentiments seems to justify Usener's attribution to Epicurus" [C, S15 p. 397]. So a page says: "a saying Usener gave to Epicurus, which Porphyry quotes unnamed".
- Epicurus's definition of philosophy, Sextus *Adv. Math.* 11.169 (Usener 219): "an activity that by arguments and reasonings secures the happy life" [U; Bury vol. III restricted].
- Cicero's Epicurean speaker Torquatus: *sapientia, quae ars vivendi putanda est* (*Fin.* 1.42) [C, S35]. Wisdom as "the art of living" is put in an Epicurean's mouth.

**The Stoics.**
- The *Placita* (Aetius), proem to book I: "The Stoics affirm that wisdom is the knowledge of things human and divine; that philosophy is the exercise of that art which is expedient to this knowledge; that virtue is the sole and sovereign art which is thus expedient; and this distributes itself into three general parts,—natural, moral, and logical" (Goodwin, rev., *Plutarch's Morals* III) [C, S27]. The Greek wording (ἄσκησιν ἐπιτηδείου τέχνης, SVF 2.35) is [U]. **Word test:** "exercise" renders *askēsis*, "practice, training" [U Greek].
- Seneca Ep. 89.5: other definitions of philosophy, "the study of virtue", "a study of the way to amend the mind", "the search for right reason". 89.8: "philosophy cannot exist without virtue, nor virtue without philosophy". 89.9: three divisions, "moral, natural, and rational" (Gummere) [C, S40].
- DL 7.39–41 (§1). Cynicism "a short cut to virtue" for the Stoics, Apollodorus (DL 7.121) [C, S10].
- Epictetus belongs on §7; the Stoic "art of living" line is *Disc.* 1.15.2 (§7).

**The Cynics.**
- Diogenes, DL 6.63: "On being asked what he had gained from philosophy, he replied, 'This at least, if nothing else – to be prepared for every fortune.' Asked where he came from, he said, 'I am a citizen of the world'" (Hicks) [C, S10].
- Antisthenes, DL 6.6: asked what advantage he had from philosophy, "The ability to hold converse with myself" [C, S10].
- DL 6.104: "Hence it has been said that Cynicism is a short cut to virtue; and after the same pattern did Zeno of Citium live his life" [C, S10].
- Crates, DL 6.86: what he gained from philosophy is "A quart of lupins and to care for no one" [C, S10].

**The sceptics.**
- **Sextus's three kinds**, PH 1.1–4 (Bury) [C, S23]:
  - "The natural result of any investigation is that the investigators either discover the object of search or deny that it is discoverable … or persist in their search". So with philosophy: "Those who believe they have discovered it are the 'Dogmatists' … Aristotle, for example, and Epicurus and the Stoics … Cleitomachus and Carneades and other Academics treat it as inapprehensible: the Sceptics keep on searching. Hence it seems reasonable to hold that the main types of philosophy are three—the Dogmatic, the Academic, and the Sceptic".
  - Bury's note: "Doctrine" renders ἀγωγή, a "way", "line of argument". PH 1.4: "we simply record each fact, like a chronicler (ἱστορικῶς), as it appears to us at the moment".
- **The names**, PH 1.7: "Zetetic" (from inquiry), "Ephectic" (suspensive), "Aporetic", "Pyrrhonean" [C, S23].
- **Why, and to what end**, PH 1.12: "The originating cause of Scepticism is, we say, the hope of attaining quietude. Men of talent, who were perturbed by the contradictions in things … were led on to inquire what is true in things and what false". 1.25–30: "the Sceptic's End is quietude in respect of matters of opinion and moderate feeling in respect of things unavoidable". There is the painter Apelles and the sponge: "quietude, as if by chance, followed upon their suspense, even as a shadow follows its substance" (Bury) [C, S23]. **Word test:** "quietude" is Bury's for *ataraxia*.
- **Pyrrho via Timon** (Aristocles in Eusebius *PE* 14.18): "the man who means to be happy must look to these three things: first, what are the natural qualities of things; secondly, in what way we should be disposed towards them; and lastly, what advantage there will be to those who are so disposed … the result, Timon says, will be first speechlessness, and then imperturbability" (Gifford) [C, S48]. A hostile witness; Pyrrho "has not himself left anything in writing" [C, S48]. DL 9.61: Pyrrho met the Gymnosophists and Magi, and adopted "agnosticism and suspension of judgement" [C, S10].
- **The Academic sceptics:** Arcesilaus (DL 4.28, §4); Cicero's own Academic method (§7).

## 7. Rome

**Lucretius** (Latin [C, S16]; my renderings unless marked).
- 5.8–12, on Epicurus: *deus ille fuit, deus, inclyte Memmi, / qui princeps vitae rationem invenit eam quae / nunc appellatur sapientia*, "a god he was, a god, noble Memmius, who first found that plan of life which is now called wisdom", and by his art set life "in so calm and so clear a light". **Word test:** Lucretius says *sapientia*; Leonard's verse has "Which now is called philosophy" [C, S17].
- 1.62–79: *primum Graius homo* … a Greek first dared to raise mortal eyes against religion (Leonard: "A Greek it was who first opposing dared") [C, S16, S17].
- 3.1–3: *E tenebris tantis tam clarum extollere lumen / qui primus potuisti inlustrans commoda vitae, / te sequor*, "you who first could raise so clear a light out of such darkness, lighting up the goods of life, you I follow" [C, S16].
- 1.136–139, on writing Greek philosophy in Latin: *Nec me animi fallit Graiorum obscura reperta / difficile inlustrare Latinis versibus esse, / multa novis verbis praesertim cum sit agendum / propter egestatem linguae et rerum novitatem*. My rendering: "nor does it escape me that the dark discoveries of the Greeks are hard to light up in Latin verse, especially since much must be done with new words, because of the poverty of the language and the newness of the matters". Also 1.832 *patrii sermonis egestas* [C, S16].

**Cicero, making a Latin vocabulary and a Latin philosophy.**
- *Tusc.* 1.5: *Philosophia iacuit usque ad hanc aetatem nec ullum habuit lumen litterarum Latinarum; quae inlustranda et excitanda nobis est*, "philosophy has lain neglected till our time and had no light from Latin letters; it is for us to light it up and rouse it" (my rendering) [C, S30].
- *Acad.* 1.24–26 (ch. 7), speaker Varro: he will keep Greek words already in use, "*philosophiam aut rhetoricam aut physicam aut dialecticam*". "*qualitates igitur appellavi quas ποιότητας Graeci vocant*", a word that even among the Greeks "*non est vulgi verbum sed philosophorum*". "*aut enim nova sunt rerum novarum facienda nomina aut ex aliis transferenda*"; if the Greeks do it, "how much more must it be allowed to us, who are now attempting to handle these things for the first time". Cicero answers that Varro will deserve well of his citizens by enriching them "*non modo copia rerum … sed etiam verborum*" [C, S34]. **Attribution caveat:** in the dialogue it is Varro who says "I have called them *qualitates*". That Cicero coined *qualitas* is the usual reading of this passage [U].
- *Fin.* 3.3–4: for the Stoics' subtle style "*nobis, quibus etiam verba parienda sunt inponendaque nova rebus novis nomina*", "we, who must even give birth to words and put new names on new things". Every art "*cuius usus vulgaris communisque non sit*" has its own vocabulary. 3.15: if Zeno could put an unheard-of name to a new thing, why not Cato? [C, S35].
- *Tusc.* 2.11: *efficit hoc philosophia: medetur animis, inanes sollicitudines detrahit, cupiditatibus liberat, pellit timores*, "philosophy does this: it heals minds, takes away empty cares, frees from desires, drives out fears" (my rendering). *Tusc.* 3.6: *Est profecto animi medicina, philosophia*. Yonge: "Philosophy is certainly the medicine of the soul, whose assistance we do not seek from abroad … but we ourselves are bound to exert our utmost energy and power in order to effect our cure" [C, S30, S31]. **Correction to the brief:** the phrase *animi medicina* is at 3.6. At 2.11 the verb is *medetur animis*.
- *Tusc.* 5.5: *O vitae philosophia dux, o virtutis indagatrix expultrixque vitiorum!* Yonge: "O Philosophy, thou guide of life! thou discoverer of virtue and expeller of vices! what had not only I myself, but the whole life of man, been without you? To you it is that we owe the origin of cities …" [C, S30, S31].
- *Tusc.* 5.8–11: the coinage story (§1); Socrates (§3). At 5.11 Cicero names his own method, Socrates' as he takes it: "*ut nostram ipsi sententiam tegeremus, errore alios levaremus et in omni disputatione, quid esset simillimum veri, quaereremus*", "to hide our own opinion, free others from error, and in every discussion seek what is most like the truth" (my rendering). He adds that Carneades held this practice [C, S30].
- *ND* 1.10: "*non enim tam auctoritatis in disputando quam rationis momenta quaerenda sunt*", in debate it is the weight of reason, not of authority, that is to be sought [C, S37].
- *De officiis* 2.5: philosophy "*si interpretari velis, praeter studium sapientiae*" is nothing else but the pursuit of wisdom; wisdom is the knowledge "*rerum divinarum et humanarum causarumque*" [C, S36].
- *Tusc.* 1.74: *Tota enim philosophorum vita … commentatio mortis est* (from the *Phaedo*) [C, S30].
- **Cicero's *Academica* as a Roman sorting of the schools.** Book 1 has Varro's history: Socrates (1.15–16); the Academics and Peripatetics "*rebus congruentes nominibus differebant*" (1.17); "*Fuit ergo iam accepta a Platone philosophandi ratio triplex*", "one on life and morals, another on nature and hidden things, a third on disputing and judging what is true and false" (1.19) [C, S34]. Section numbers within chapters are [U]; the Latin Library text gives chapter numbers.

**Varro, as Augustine reports him** (*City of God* 19.1): Varro counted 288 possible sects from the views on the supreme good. He held that a sect is made only by a different view of the ends, "*quando quidem nulla est homini causa philosophandi, nisi ut beatus sit*". Dods: "For man has no other reason for philosophizing than that he may be happy" [C, S38 Latin; S39 Dods]. It is a report by a Christian author of a lost work, so attribute it to "Varro, as Augustine reports".

**Seneca** (Gummere; Latin from Wikisource and the Latin Library).
- Ep. 16.3: "*Non est philosophia populare artificium nec ostentationi paratum; non in verbis sed in rebus est*". Gummere: "Philosophy is no trick to catch the public; it is not devised for show. It is a matter, not of words, but of facts … It moulds and constructs the soul; it orders our life, guides our conduct, shows us what we should do and what we should leave undone" [C, S40, S42].
- Ep. 20.2: "*facere docet philosophia, non dicere*". Gummere: "philosophy teaches us to act, not to speak; it exacts of every man that he should live according to his own standards, that his life should not be out of harmony with his words" [C, S40, S42].
- Ep. 89.4–9 (§1, §6) [C, S40, S41].
- Ep. 108.23: "*Itaque quae philosophia fuit facta philologia est*". Gummere: "those who teach us how to debate and not how to live … Thus the study of wisdom has become the study of words" [C, S40, S41]. **Word test:** Gummere's "study of wisdom/study of words" hides the Latin pair *philosophia/philologia*. 108.35: "all study of philosophy and all reading should be applied to the idea of living the happy life … We should so learn them that words may become deeds" [C, S40].

**Epictetus** (Higginson 1890 via Perseus, Greek Schenkl via Perseus; Enchiridion Higginson via Gutenberg).
- *Disc.* 2.11.1: ἀρχὴ φιλοσοφίας παρά γε τοῖς ὡς δεῖ καὶ κατὰ θύραν ἁπτομένοις αὐτῆς συναίσθησις τῆς αὑτοῦ ἀσθενείας καὶ ἀδυναμίας περὶ τὰ ἀναγκαῖα. Higginson: "The beginning of philosophy, at least to such as enter upon it in a proper way, and by the door, is a consciousness of our own weakness and inability in necessary things". The chapter ends with the search for "some rule which shall serve like a balance … This is the beginning of philosophy" [C, S18, S19].
- *Disc.* 1.15.2: philosophy "does not promise to procure any outward good for man … For as the material of a carpenter is wood; of a statuary, brass; so of the art of living, the material is each man's own life" (Higginson). The Greek is οὕτως τῆς περὶ βίον τέχνης ὕλη ὁ βίος αὐτοῦ ἑκάστου [C, S18, S19]. **Word test:** "the art of living" renders ἡ περὶ βίον τέχνη, "the art concerned with life".
- *Disc.* 3.23.30: ἰατρεῖόν ἐστιν, ἄνδρες, τὸ τοῦ φιλοσόφου σχολεῖον· οὐ δεῖ ἡσθέντας ἐξελθεῖν, ἀλλ᾽ ἀλγήσαντας. Higginson: "The school of a philosopher is a surgery. You are not to go out of it with pleasure, but with pain" [C, S18, S19]. The word is *iatreion*, a doctor's consulting room.
- *Ench.* 5: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things" [C, S21]. *Ench.* 46: "Never proclaim yourself a philosopher, nor make much talk among the ignorant about your principles, but show them by actions" [C, S21]. *Ench.* 49: if I admire only the interpretation of Chrysippus, "what do I become more than a grammarian, instead of a philosopher?" [C, S21].
- *Ench.* 52: "The first and most necessary topic in philosophy is the practical application of principles, as, *We ought not to lie*; the second is that of demonstrations … the third, that which gives strength and logical connection to the other two … But we do just the contrary. For we spend all our time on the third point … and entirely neglect the first. Therefore, at the same time that we lie, we are very ready to show how it is demonstrated that lying is wrong" [C, S21]. **Numbering:** Higginson numbers this LI; it is chapter 52 in the standard numbering [U for the concordance, C that Higginson prints it as LI].

**Marcus Aurelius** (Haines 1916, Wikisource; Greek from Greek Wikisource).
- 2.17: τί οὖν τὸ παραπέμψαι δυνάμενον; ἓν καὶ μόνον φιλοσοφία [C, S22 Greek]. Haines: "What then is it that can help us on our way? One thing and one alone—Philosophy; and this consists in keeping the divine 'genius' within pure and unwronged, lord of all pleasures and pains" [C, S22]. **Word test:** *parapempsai* is "to escort, see on one's way"; the brief's "escort" is closer than Haines.
- 5.9: "Come not back to Philosophy as to a schoolmaster, but as the sore-eyed to their sponges and their white of egg … forget not that Philosophy wishes but what thy nature wishes" [C, S22].
- 6.12: "So hast thou now the court and philosophy for stepmother and mother". 6.30: "Strive to be always such as Philosophy minded to make thee" [C, S22].

## 8. Late antiquity

**Plotinus and Porphyry** (MacKenna, Wikisource).
- Porphyry, *Life of Plotinus* 1: "Plotinus, the philosopher our contemporary, seemed ashamed of being in the body" [C, S45].
- *Life* 3: at twenty "he was caught by the passion for philosophy"; on hearing Ammonius he said "This was the man I was looking for"; he joined Gordian's army, eager "to investigate the Persian methods and the system adopted among the Indians" [C, S45].
- *Life* 23: "the Term, the one end, of his life was to become Uniate, to approach to the God over all: and four times, during the period I passed with him, he achieved this Term" [C, S45].
- *Enn.* I.3.5: "What, then, is Philosophy? Philosophy is the supremely precious. Is Dialectic, then, the same as Philosophy? It is the precious part of Philosophy … by Dialectic it comes to contemplation" [C, S46]. The Greek (τὸ τιμιώτατον) is [U].
- *Enn.* I.6.9: "Withdraw into yourself and look … act as does the creator of a statue … never cease chiselling your statue, until there shall shine out on you from it the godlike splendour of virtue" [C, S46].
- Porphyry, *Ad Marcellam*: philosophy as healing (§6). It also marks his own life in philosophy: "I recalled you also to your proper mode of life, and gave you a share in philosophy, pointing out to you a doctrine that should guide your life" (§3, Zimmern) [C, S47]. Porphyry's *Isagoge* became the standard entry text of the curriculum (SEP Elias: Porphyry's *Isagoge* and Aristotle's *Categories* "were part of the standard curriculum for beginners") [C, S56].

**Proclus, as Marinus tells it** (Guthrie 1925, tertullian.org).
- *Life of Proclus* 13: in less than two years Proclus read "all of Aristotle's treatises on logic, ethics, politics, physics, and on the science which rises above all these, theology". These were "a kind of preparatory initiation or lesser mysteries", before Syrianus led him "to the Greater Mysteries of Plato" [C, S49]. That is the school's order of reading as its own account gives it.
- §19: "a philosopher should watch over the salvation of not only a city, nor over the national customs of a few people, but that he should be the hierophant of the whole world in common" [C, S49]. The section number is [U]; the file marks 19 and lacks a 20.
- §38: "If I had the power, of all ancient books I would leave in circulation only the Oracles and the Timaeus" [C, S49].

**The Alexandrian school and the six definitions.**
- **Elias's list**, *Prolegomena philosophiae* ch. 4, p. 8.7–19 Busse (*CAG* 18.1, 1900), read from the Toronto scan's OCR [C, S54; OCR errors, Greek normalised by me]. The six are:
  1. γνῶσις τῶν ὄντων ᾗ ὄντα ἐστί, knowledge of beings as beings;
  2. γνῶσις θείων τε καὶ ἀνθρωπίνων πραγμάτων, knowledge of things divine and human;
  3. μελέτη θανάτου, practice of death;
  4. ὁμοίωσις θεῷ κατὰ τὸ δυνατὸν ἀνθρώπῳ, likeness to god so far as possible for a human;
  5. from pre-eminence, τέχνη τεχνῶν καὶ ἐπιστήμη ἐπιστημῶν, art of arts and science of sciences;
  6. from etymology, φιλία σοφίας, love of wisdom.

  The grouping: 1–2 "from the subject" (ἐκ τοῦ ὑποκειμένου), 3–4 "from the end" (ἐκ τοῦ τέλους), 5 "from pre-eminence" (ἐκ τῆς ὑπεροχῆς), 6 "from etymology" (ἐκ τῆς ἐτυμολογίας). **Elias's own attribution:** the etymological one and the two from the subject to Pythagoras; the two from the end to Plato, "the one in the *Theaetetus* … the one in the *Phaedo*"; the one from pre-eminence to Aristotle "in the *Metaphysics*". The next line asks "why six definitions of philosophy?" [C, S54].
- **Ammonius.** SEP (Blank): Ammonius "introduced as a new set a sequence of lessons introductory to the study of Aristotle", beginning with "an introduction to philosophy". This "was particularly congenial and influential, giving a number of definitions of philosophy and, particularly, two definitions 'from the goal': Plato's 'assimilation to God as far as possible' (Theaetetus 176b) and 'practice of death' (Phaedo 64a; Ammonius, in Isag. 3,7 ff. and 4,15 ff.)". The study of Aristotle in his school "began with logic, then moved on to ethics, physics, mathematics, and theology (Ammonius, in Cat. 5,31–6,8)". The present prooemium of his *in Isag.* "is … considered inauthentic" [C, S55]. That Ammonius's list is the first full six-fold list, which Elias and David repeat, is [C as reported] in a search summary of Chroust 1972 (bot-blocked; not opened). Treat it as [U].
- **David** (*Prolegomena*, *CAG* 18.2): repeats the six and adds the sceptical question whether philosophy exists [U; Gertz 2018 blurb via search only].
- **Elias on the commentator** (SEP Wildberg, quoting Wilson's translation): "When expounding Aristotle he must not become an Aristotelian … he must repeat to himself at all times 'the author is a dear friend, but so also is truth, and when both stand before me truth is the better friend'" (*in Cat.* 122f.) [C as quoted, S56]. SEP: Elias "repeatedly emphasizes the … conviction that the purpose of philosophy is the transformation or assimilation of a human being to the godhead" [C, S56]. The Elias authorship is itself uncertain ("good evidence that a … philosopher bearing the … name 'Elias' ever existed is in fact scant") [C, S56]. One clause at most.
- **The parallel with al-Kindī, as a fact.** The site's Islamic-world page already gives al-Kindī's six (love of wisdom; imitating God's acts as far as possible for man; concern with death; craft of crafts and wisdom of wisdoms; knowledge of man himself; knowledge of eternal things). Stefaniuk (2017, p. 320): "al-Kindī did not develop the definitions of the term 'philosophy' collected in the treatise, but rather drew them from Greek sources". His footnote 17 cites "David's and Elias' commentaries on Porphyry's Isagoge" [C, S58]. SEP (Adamson): *On Definitions* is "almost certainly at least a production of al-Kindi's circle, probably composed in several strata" [C, S57]. **Headline only:** four of al-Kindī's six match the Alexandrian list (love of wisdom, likeness to god, death, art of arts); "knowledge of man himself" and "knowledge of eternal things" are worded differently (my comparison of the two lists as given on the site page and in Elias).
- **Justinian, 529: fact and contest.**
  - SEP (Helmig, *Simplicius*): "In 529 … emperor Justinian ordered that 'no one was any longer allowed to teach philosophy and astronomy' (this is often referred to as the closure of the Platonic Academy …). It is assumed that this edict mainly targeted non-Christian philosophers". Simplicius and six others went "under the lead of Damascius to the court of the Persian King Chosroes, who had only come to power … in 531". A 532 peace treaty let them return "without being forced to adopt opinions which they did not hold". "Where the seven went after that is still a matter of debate" (Athens, Syria or Harran) [C, S59].
  - SEP (Wildberg, *Elias*): teaching went on in Alexandria, "pagan philosophy was indeed still being taught in Alexandria, only by Christians … in marked contrast to Athens, where pagan philosophy died out rapidly after the Athenian academy was closed by Emperor Justinian in 529" [C, S56].
  - **Fact:** a decree of 529; the Athenian philosophers' journey to Persia (Agathias 2.30–31) and return. **Contested:** that "the Academy" was an institution "closed" (Platonic teaching at Athens was a private school; the edict's wording survives through the chronicler Malalas, 18.47); whether teaching at Athens stopped at once; and where Simplicius wrote. Malalas's wording and Agathias's account are not opened [U]; see "Sources to fetch".

## 9. Byzantium (short closing paragraph)

- **Psellos** (*Chronographia* VI.36–42, Sewter 1953, Fordham) [C, S60]:
  - VI.37: "Philosophy, when I first studied it, was moribund as far as its professors were concerned, and I alone revived it, untutored by any masters worthy of mention". He passed from teacher to teacher "and he to Aristotle and Plato".
  - VI.38: "coming down to Plotinus, Porphyry, and Iamblichus", then "the mighty harbour of the admirable Proclus".
  - VI.41: "Philosophy is less concerned with the embellishments of words. Its aim is rather to explore the nature of the universe".
  - VI.42: "But there is a new philosophy, based on the mystery of our Christian religion, which transcends the ancient systems … It was this philosophy, rather than the profane, which became the object of my special study".
  - Sewter's 1953 copyright was not renewed (Fordham's note) [C, S60].
- **Facts** (SEP Ierodiakonou, *Byzantine Philosophy*) [C, S61]:
  - the standard curriculum was "elementary Aristotelian logic and natural philosophy combined with elementary mathematics";
  - Psellos and John Italos were "Consuls of the Philosophers" from the late 1040s to the 1070s;
  - "There was never in Byzantium anything resembling the autonomous universities of the Latin West";
  - Italos was "condemned by the Church in 1082", and is still anathematised in the Synodikon read on the first Sunday of Lent.
- **Plethon:** "the great controversy which started in 1439, when George Gemistos Plethon (c. 1360–1452) published his pamphlet De differentiis, and ground to a halt after the Latin version of Cardinal Bessarion's (1403–1472) In calumniatorem Platonis was printed thirty years later". The pamphlet "purported to show how inferior Aristotle was to Plato, in spite of being more admired especially in the Latin West"; Gennadios Scholarios answered it [C, S61]. Plethon went "completely to discard Christianity in favor of Neoplatonic polytheism" [C, S61]. Bessarion was in the Greek delegation to the Council of Ferrara-Florence (1437–39) [C, S62]. That Plethon wrote *De differentiis* at Florence during the Council is [U]. The SEP Bessarion entry gives Plethon "d. 1454" against "c. 1360–1452" in SEP Byzantine [C, S61, S62]; a dating detail, so give no death year. Plethon's own words (the opening of *De differentiis*) are not opened [U].
