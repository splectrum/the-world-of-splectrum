# Metaphilosophy, the Greek and Latin page's gaps: research note

Purpose: to source the stretches the Greek and Latin page skips: late antiquity and the early Middle Ages, scholastic method and the arts-faculty conflict, the Renaissance, and the eighteenth century before Kant. Each find is set against the page's three questions: (1) What is philosophy? (2) Its point? (3) How is it done? Headlines only. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by key (Sources at the end). **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a secondary I could not open). Quotations are given as found in the named edition or translation; "my rendering" marks my own English.

---

## 1. Late antiquity and the early Middle Ages

### Augustine

- *De vera religione* 5.8, Latin: "Sic enim creditur et docetur, quod est humanae salutis caput, non aliam esse philosophiam, id est sapientiae studium, et aliam religionem" [C, as quoted by D'Elia S3 n. 6 (OCR "creditor", "stadium" corrected); critical edition CCSL 32 not opened]. My rendering: "philosophy, that is the pursuit of wisdom, is not one thing and religion another".
- Burleigh's translation: "So it is taught and believed as a chief point in man's salvation that philosophy, i.e., the pursuit of wisdom, cannot be quite divorced from religion, for those whose doctrine we do not approve do not share in our sacramental rites" (v, 8) [C, S1]. **Word test:** "cannot be quite divorced" softens *non aliam … et aliam*, "not one … and another". The gloss *id est sapientiae studium* is Augustine's own. The context is polemical: pagan philosophers worshipped with the people and taught otherwise in their schools (v, 8; i, 1) [C, S1].
- *City of God* VIII.1: "si sapientia Deus est, per quem facta sunt omnia … verus philosophus est amator Dei" [C, S4]; Dods: "Now, if wisdom is God, who made all things … then the philosopher is a lover of God" [C, S5]. Dods leaves out *verus*, "the true philosopher". Augustine adds at once that not all who are called philosophers "are lovers of true wisdom" [C, S5].
- Mink's introduction to Burleigh credits Augustine with the dictum "Credo, ut intelligam" [C, S1]. The formula is usually traced to Anselm, with Augustine's "crede ut intelligas" behind it [U]. Not for the page.
- Q1: philosophy is the same pursuit as true religion. Q2: wisdom, which is God. Q3: none from these passages.

### Boethius, *Consolation of Philosophy* (c. 524 [U])

- I prose 1: the robe. "Harum in extremo margine Π graecum, in supremo uero Θ legebatur intextum atque inter utrasque litteras in scalarum modum gradus quidam insigniti uidebantur, quibus ab inferiore ad superius elementum esset ascensus" [C, S6, where the font renders the Greek letters as P/Q]. James: "On the lower-most edge was inwoven the Greek letter Π, on the topmost the letter Θ, and between the two were to be seen steps, like a staircase, from the lower to the upper letter" [C, S7]. The robe "had been torn by the hands of violent persons, who had each snatched away what he could clutch" [C, S7]. In I prose 3 these are the Epicureans, Stoics "and the rest" [C, S7].
- **Word test:** Boethius does not say what Π and Θ stand for. James's footnote glosses them as "the Political life, the life of action" and "the Theoretical life, the life of thought" [C, S7]. The usual reading, practical and theoretical philosophy, is the commentators' [U]. On the page this should read "the letters are usually read as …".
- The physician. I prose 1: she drives off the Muses of poetry, "who, so far from giving medicine to heal his malady, even feed it with sweet poison" [C, S7]. I prose 2: "Sed medicinae, inquit, tempus est quam querelae" [C, S6]; James: "'But the time,' said she, 'calls rather for healing than for lamentation'" [C, S7]. I prose 3: "ad cognoscendam medicantis faciem … respicio nutricem meam … Philosophiam" [C, S6]; James: "regained the power to recognise the face of my physician … I beheld my nurse, Philosophy, whose halls I had frequented from my youth up" [C, S7].
- Q1: a single figure, torn into pieces by the sects. Q2: healing the mind. Q3: a staircase from Π to Θ (as image only).
- *De Trinitate* 2's division of speculative philosophy (natural, mathematical, theological) [U, not opened]. It sorts the parts of philosophy and does not answer Q1. **Discard.**

### Eriugena, *De divina praedestinatione* (851 [U])

- "Quid est aliud de philosophia tractare, nisi verae religionis, qua summa et principalis rerum causa, Deus, et humiliter colitur et rationabiliter investigatur, regulas exponere? Conficitur inde veram esse philosophiam veram religionem conversimque veram religionem esse veram philosophiam" [C, as quoted by D'Elia S3, citing Madec ed., CCCM 50, p. 5 = PL 122, 358A]. That it is chapter 1.1 [U]. D'Elia's footnote sets it beside Augustine *De vera religione* V, 8 [C, S3].
- My rendering: "What else is it to treat of philosophy than to set out the rules of the true religion, by which God, the highest and principal cause of all things, is both humbly worshipped and rationally searched out? It follows that true philosophy is true religion and, conversely, true religion is true philosophy." The English of Brennan (1998) was not opened [U].
- Q1: philosophy is identical with true religion. Q3: God "rationally searched out" (*rationabiliter investigatur*).
- This is a third answer for the page, beside "servant": **identity**, not subordination. Augustine and Eriugena say the same, not the less.

## 2. Scholastic method and the arts-faculty conflict

### Abelard, *Sic et Non*, prologue (c. 1120s [U])

- Lewis's translation, from the Boyer–McKeon text (lines 330–350): "Indeed this first key of wisdom is defined, of course, as assiduous or frequent questioning … And indeed, through doubting we come to questioning and through questions we perceive the truth" [C, S8]. Abelard cites Aristotle, via Boethius on the *Categories*: "it would not be useless to have some doubts concerning individual points" [C, S8].
- Latin as commonly quoted: "Haec quippe prima sapientiae clavis definitur, assidua scilicet seu frequens interrogatio … Dubitando enim ad inquisitionem venimus; inquirendo veritatem percipimus" [U, search summary only; Boyer–McKeon not reachable (archive.org copy restricted)]. **Word test:** *inquisitio* comes out as "questioning" in Lewis and "inquiry" elsewhere [U].
- The work collects the Fathers' apparently conflicting sayings "to the greatest exercise of seeking the truth" [C, S8]. Its matter is theology, not philosophy by name. Q3 only: doubting leads to inquiry, and inquiry to the truth.

### The *quaestio* and *disputatio* (SEP, "Literary Forms of Medieval Philosophy")

- Disputation "arises out of and is an academic formalization of the philosophical dialogue (Novikoff 2012) and comes into the classroom as an outgrowth of the *lectio*, the careful reading and commentary on authoritative texts (Kenny and Pinborg 1982)". Differences among sources were "eventually formalized into the posing of a question" [C, S9 §2.6].
- Bazán's definition: "a disputed question is a regular form of teaching, apprenticeship and research, presided over by a master, characterized by a dialectical method which consists of bringing forward and examining arguments based on reason and authority which oppose one another … and where the master must come to a doctrinal solution by an act of determination" [C, S9, quoting Bazán 1985, 40].
- There were two sessions: first the arguments, provisionally determined by a student *respondens*; then the master's determination and replies [C, S9]. Quodlibets were open questions from any member of the audience, in Advent and Lent [C, S9]. The forms "represent what has been called 'the institutionalization of conflict'" [C, S9; who coined the phrase is not given there].
- Q3: the sequence runs *lectio* → *quaestio* → *disputatio* → *determinatio*. The page already has the article form (objections, "On the contrary", "I answer that", replies). This adds where that form came from.

### Boethius of Dacia, *De summo bono* (c. 1270 [U])

- "Et isti sunt philosophi, qui ponunt vitam suam in studio sapientiae" [C, S10]. "Haec est vita philosophi, quam quicumque non habuerit non habet rectam vitam" [C, S10]. "Philosophum autem voco omnem hominem viventem secundum rectum ordinem naturae, et qui acquisivit optimum et ultimum finem vitae humanae" [C, S10]. "Ideo philosophus ducit vitam valde voluptuosam" [C, S10]. My renderings: "This is the life of the philosopher, and whoever does not have it does not have a right life"; "I call a philosopher every man who lives according to the right order of nature and has gained the best and final end of human life."
- Hadot reads the arts masters as rediscovering, through Aristotle, "that philosophy is not only discourse but a way of life". He cites Boethius of Dacia and quotes Domański that this current "accorded complete autonomy to philosophy, and did not consider it a mere propaedeutic to Christian doctrine" [C, S11 pp. 262–263].
- Q1: the life spent in the pursuit of wisdom. Q2: the highest human good, according to nature. **This qualifies the page.** It currently gives Hadot's arts faculty as Aristotle commentary only, and "the servant" as the whole medieval answer. Hadot himself records the counter-current (S11).

### The Paris condemnation of 7 March 1277 (Bishop Stephen Tempier)

- In the Chartularium's numbering (CUP I, no. 473, p. 543 ff.): "40. Quod non est excellentior status, quam vacare philosophie" and "154. Quod sapientes mundi sunt philosophi tantum" [C, S12 (OCR "esl exceilenlior" corrected)]. Nearby: "152. Quod sermones theologi fundati sunt in fabulis"; "153. Quod nichil plus scitur propter scire theologiam" [C, S12].
- Numbering: 40 and 154 are the original list's numbers as the Chartularium prints them. Mandonnet later renumbered the list, separating 179 philosophical theses from 40 theological ones. In Wippel's summary, "The first seven of the philosophical propositions bear on the nature and excellence of philosophy" [C, S13]. That 40 and 154 are Mandonnet 1 and 2, and Hissette 1 and 2 [U]. On the page: cite "articles 40 and 154" and leave the other numberings out.
- **Word test:** *vacare philosophie* means to be free for philosophy, to give one's time to it. "Study" is a translator's word. The search summary's "There is no better way to live than philosophically" is a paraphrase [U].
- The prologue names the problem as Tempier saw it. The arts masters "Dicunt enim ea esse vera secundum philosophiam, sed non secundum fidem catholicam, quasi sint due contrarie veritates" [C, S12]. My rendering: "they say these things are true according to philosophy but not according to the Catholic faith, as if there were two contrary truths". The condemned are "nonnulli Parisius studentes in artibus proprie facultatis limites excedentes" [C, S12].
- Q1/Q2: the arts masters' claim, on this list, is that philosophy is the best state of life and that philosophers alone are the wise. The bishop condemns both. This is the headline conflict of the stage.
- Scotus and Ockham: neither bears on the three questions in a way the stage needs. **Discard.**

## 3. Renaissance

### Petrarch, *De sui ipsius et multorum ignorantia* (1367–71 [U])

- Nachod's translation: "It is safer to strive for a good and pious will than for a capable and clear intellect. The object of the will, as it pleases the wise, is to be good; that of the intellect is truth. It is better to will the good than to know the truth" [C, S14]. Latin: "Satius est autem bonum velle quam verum nosse" [C, as given in Hadot S11's note to p. 263; Petrarch's text not opened].
- On Aristotle's *Ethics*: "I have perhaps become more learned through them when I went home, but not better … It is one thing to know …" [C, S14]. "The true moral philosophers and useful teachers of the virtues are those whose first and last intention is to make hearer and reader good" [C, S14].
- Hadot: Petrarch "refused to apply the term 'philosophers' to 'professors sitting in a chair,' and reserved the word for those who confirmed their teachings by their acts" [C, S11 pp. 262–263].
- Q1: the name belongs to those who live what they teach. Q2: to make people good, not learned.

### Erasmus, *Paraclesis* (1516)

- Prefaced to the 1516 Greek and Latin New Testament [C, S15 intro]. An anonymous English translation (Antwerp 1529, ed. Luttmer): "this kind of philosophy doth rather consist in the affects of the mind, than in subtle reasons. It is a life rather than a disputation. It is an inspiration rather than a science. And rather a new transformation, than a reasoning" [C, S15]. "What other thing is this doctrine of Christ which he calleth the new regeneration, but a restoring or repairing of our nature which in His first creation was good" [C, S15].
- Latin *philosophia Christi* and the "vita magis quam disputatio" line [U, Latin not opened; Holborn's edition is the standard].
- Hadot: Erasmus "repeatedly affirms that the only philosopher is the person who lives philosophically, as did Socrates, Diogenes the Cynic, and Epictetus, but also like John the Baptist, Christ, and the Apostles". Petrarch and Erasmus "have in mind a Christian philosophical life" [C, S11 p. 263].
- Q1: Christ's teaching as a philosophy, lived rather than disputed. Q3: by the affects, not syllogisms; against the disputation.

### Pico, *Oration*

- Forbes's translation: the soul should cast out "her uncleanness through moral philosophy and dialectic, adorned herself with manifold philosophy … and crowned the pediments of her doors with the garlands of theology"; Raphael frees us "by moral philosophy and by dialectic as though by wholesome drugs", then Gabriel leads "through the miracles of nature" [C, S14]. Q3: a ladder of moral philosophy, dialectic, natural philosophy and theology. **Optional, probably discard.** It repeats the ancient and Christian ladder and adds nothing on Q1.

### Montaigne

- Numbering: the 1595 text (Michaud 1907) and Cotton number the chapters **I.19** and **I.25** [C, S16, S17]. Villey's edition, the modern standard, numbers them I.20 and I.26 [U]. The page must name its edition.
- I.19/20 opening: "Cicero dit que Philosopher ce n'est autre chose que s'aprester à la mort … toute la sagesse et discours du monde se resoult en fin à ce point, de nous apprendre à ne craindre point à mourir" [C, S16]. Then: reason "ne doit viser qu'à nostre contentement, et tout son trauail tendre en somme à nous faire bien viure" [C, S16]. Cotton: "to study philosophy is nothing but to prepare one's self to die" [C, S17].
- Sources. Cicero, *Tusculans* I.74 (ch. XXX): "Tota enim philosophorum vita, ut ait idem, commentatio mortis est" [C, S18], the *idem* being Socrates. Plato, *Phaedo*, Jowett: "the true philosophers, Simmias, are always occupied in the practice of dying, wherefore also to them least of all men is death terrible" [C, S19]. That this is 67e [U, Stephanus numbers not in the Gutenberg text]. **Word test:** Plato's "practice" (*meletan*) [U] becomes Cicero's *commentatio*, a rehearsal or meditation, and then Montaigne's *s'aprester*, to get ready. Montaigne then turns the point to living well.
- I.25/26: "Puis que la Philosophie est celle qui nous instruict à viure, et que l'enfance y a sa leçon, comme les autres aages, pourquoy ne la luy communique-on?" [C, S16]. Cotton: "Since philosophy is that which instructs us to live …" [C, S17]. On the state of the schools: "C'est grand cas que les choses en soyent là en nostre siècle, que la philosophie soit iusques aux gens d'entendement, vn nom vain et fantastique, qui se treuue de nul vsage, et de nul pris … Ie croy que ces ergotismes en sont cause" [C, S16]. Cotton: "The most manifest sign of wisdom is a continual cheerfulness" [C, S17].
- Hadot: Montaigne's *Essays* "show the philosopher trying to practice the various modes of life proposed by ancient philosophy: 'My trade and my art is living.'" In Hadot's account his itinerary ran from Seneca's Stoicism through Plutarch's probabilism and Scepticism "finally—definitively—to Epicureanism" [C, S11 p. 264]. That itinerary is Hadot's reading, an old and disputed thesis (Villey's "evolution" of the *Essais*) [U]. It is a dispute over details, so attribute it and do not stage it.
- Q1: an art of living. Q2: to learn to die, so as to live well; contentment. Q3: none beyond "teach it to children" and against the "ergotismes".

## 4. The eighteenth century before Kant

### Christian Wolff, *Discursus praeliminaris de philosophia in genere* (1728)

- §29: "Philosophia est scientia possibilium, quatenus esse possunt" [C, S20: the 1740 Latin, where the §29 heading is garbled in OCR but later sections repeat the formula "(§. 29.)" verbatim; 1728 title page seen]. Blackwell: "29. Philosophy is the science of the possibles insofar as they can be." Wolff's note says he found the definition "in 1703 while I was attending private lectures on philosophy at the Leipzig Academy" [C, S21].
- §6: "The knowledge of the reason of things which are or occur is called philosophy" (Blackwell). His example: knowing how a river's motion "depends on the slope of the bottom" [C, S21]. §31: philosophy "must give a reason why the possibles can actually occur" [C, S21]. Chapter Six is "The Freedom to Philosophize" [C, S21].
- Q1: the science of possibles, giving the reason why things are. Q3: demonstration ("a science is a habit of demonstrating propositions", §30 as cited at §31 [C, S21]) and the freedom to philosophise.
- **Collision:** the evolution page's "Wolff's epigenesis (1759)" is Caspar Friedrich Wolff, not Christian Wolff [C, site grep].

### D'Alembert, *Discours préliminaire* (1751), and the *Système figuré*

- "Ainsi la mémoire, la raison proprement dite, & l'imagination, sont les trois manieres différentes dont notre ame opere … l'Histoire, qui se rapporte à la mémoire ; la Philosophie, qui est le fruit de la raison ; & les Beaux-arts, que l'imagination fait naître" [C, S22]. Under philosophy fall the science of God (natural and revealed theology), the science of man and the science of nature ("n'est autre que celle des corps") [C, S22].
- The debt to Bacon: "nous devons principalement au Chancelier Bacon l'Arbre encyclopédique … le plan de Bacon étant différent du nôtre … nous ayons pris quelquefois une route différente" [C, S22]. "L'arbre ou système figuré en sera la mappemonde" [C, S22].
- Q1: philosophy is the whole field of reason, sciences included, set beside history and the arts. This continues the page's "whole system" stage in Bacon's frame, not Descartes's tree.
- The Encyclopédie's own article PHILOSOPHIE (vol. 12, pp. 511–515) adopts Wolff: "la définition que M. Wolf a donnée de la Philosophie, me paroit renfermer dans sa brieveté tout ce qui caractérise cette science. C'est, selon lui, la science des possibles en tant que possibles" [C, S23]. "Toutes les sciences, tous les arts ont-ils leur philosophie" [C, S23]. **Word test:** "en tant que possibles", "as possibles", shifts Wolff's "quatenus esse possunt", "insofar as they can be". Authorship: Wikisource lists "D'Alembert, Diderot" [C, S23]; the usual attribution is to others, e.g. Formey or Yvon [U].

### "Philosophe" (Encyclopédie vol. 12, pp. 509–511)

- "Le philosophe au contraire demêle les causes autant qu'il est en lui … c'est une horloge qui se monte, pour ainsi dire, quelquefois elle-même" [C, S24]. "La raison est à l'égard du philosophe, ce que la grace est à l'égard du chretien" [C, S24]. "Notre philosophe ne se croit pas en exil dans ce monde … c'est un honnête homme qui veut plaire & se rendre utile" [C, S24]. "La société civile est, pour ainsi dire, une divinité pour lui sur la terre" [C, S24]. "Le philosophe est donc un honnête homme qui agit en tout par raison, & qui joint à un esprit de réflexion & de justesse les mœurs & les qualités sociables" [C, S24].
- Attribution: Wikisource lists "Dumarsais, Venel" [C, S24]. It is a reworking of the anonymous essay *Le Philosophe* (1743), usually given to Dumarsais [U]. Vol. 12 is dated 1765 [U]; Wikisource's "1751" is the series date [C, S24]. The page could say "the article 'Philosophe', usually attributed to Dumarsais".
- Q1: a social being who acts by reason. Reason is to him what grace is to the Christian, which reverses the servant picture. Q2: to be useful to civil society. Q3: to disentangle causes and to hold the true, the doubtful and the probable apart ("pour douteux ce qui est douteux") [C, S24].

### Shaftesbury, *Characteristicks* III, *Miscellaneous Reflections*

- "To philosophize, in a just Signification, is but to carry Good-breeding a step higher. For the Accomplishment of Breeding is, To learn whatever is decent in Company, or beautiful in Arts; and the Sum of Philosophy is, To learn what is just in Society, and beautiful in Nature, and the Order of the World" [C, S25, 1773 ed., vol. III p. 161; that it is Misc. III ch. 1 [U]]. Q1: a short and quotable line. **Optional.**

### Hume

- *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* §1.1: "MORAL philosophy, or the science of human nature, may be treated after two different manners; each of which has its peculiar merit, and may contribute to the entertainment, instruction, and reformation of mankind" [C, S26]. §1.2: the other species "consider man in the light of a reasonable rather than an active being" [C, S26]. §1.3: "the easy and obvious philosophy will always, with the generality of mankind, have the preference above the accurate and abstruse" [C, S26].
- §1.6: in Hume's personification Nature says "Indulge your passion for science, says she, but let your science be human, and such as may have a direct reference to action and society … Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man" [C, S26]. Hume's conclusion: "nature has pointed out a mixed kind of life as most suitable to human race" [C, S26].
- §1.12: "We must submit to this fatigue, in order to live at ease ever after: And must cultivate true metaphysics with some care, in order to destroy the false and adulterate" [C, S26]. The method: "to enquire seriously into the nature of human understanding" [C, S26]. "This mental geography, or delineation of the distinct parts and powers of the mind" [C, S26].
- **Word test:** the two "manners" are of *moral* philosophy, "the science of human nature", not of philosophy at large. In Hume's usage that is very broad [U]. Still, the page should keep his wording.
- "Of Suicide", Su 1: "ONE considerable advantage, that arises from philosophy, consists in the sovereign antidote, which it affords to superstition and false religion. All other remedies against that pestilent distemper are vain, or, at least, uncertain" [C, S27]. It was suppressed and published posthumously [U for dates (c. 1755 written; 1777/1783 printed)].
- Q1: two species, the easy and the abstruse. Q2: a remedy for superstition, and science kept "human". Q3: accurate reasoning and mental geography. Destroy false metaphysics by cultivating the true.

### Voltaire

- Voltaire's coining of "philosophy of history" (1765) already stands on the residue page. No short line on Q1 found worth adding. **Discard.**

## Draft additions to the page

Options, not decisions. The present stages are kept. The changes are two widenings and two new stages.

**A. "The servant" widens: identity and servant, then the schools and the arts faculty.** Three variants of the stage title: "The servant" kept; "Faith's philosophy"; or "The same as religion, and the servant".
- *What it is.* Before Clement's ladder, add Augustine's identity claim (*De vera religione* 5.8: *non aliam … philosophiam … et aliam religionem*) and *City of God* VIII.1 ("verus philosophus est amator Dei"). Add Eriugena's "veram esse philosophiam veram religionem conversimque". Then the existing servant lines (Clement, Damian, Aquinas). Close on the arts masters' counter-claim: Boethius of Dacia ("Haec est vita philosophi …") and the 1277 articles 40 and 154, with Tempier's "two contrary truths". Hadot's line on Boethius of Dacia and Domański's "complete autonomy to philosophy" belong here too.
- *Its point.* Boethius's Lady Philosophy, the physician ("medicinae … tempus est quam querelae"), before Aquinas's salvation line. Or, if preferred, at the end of "A way of life" as its last ancient form (option A′).
- *How it is done.* Add before the existing article form: Abelard's "through doubting we come to questioning and through questions we perceive the truth", and the *lectio* → *quaestio* → *disputatio* sequence (SEP, Bazán).

**B. New stage: "The way of life again" (Renaissance), between the servant and the whole system.** Hadot's own framing is "recurrences of the ancient concept".
- *What it is.* Petrarch keeps the name for those who live their teaching (Hadot). Erasmus's *philosophia Christi* is "a life rather than a disputation".
- *Its point.* Petrarch: "It is better to will the good than to know the truth"; Aristotle made him "more learned … but not better". Montaigne: philosophy "nous instruict à viure"; to philosophise is to learn to die, after Cicero (*commentatio mortis*) and Plato.
- *How it is done.* Erasmus: by the affects, not by "subtle reasons". Montaigne against the "ergotismes" that made philosophy "vn nom vain et fantastique". Hadot on the *Essays* as practice ("My trade and my art is living") is optional. Pico's ladder is optional.

**C. "The whole system, and the under-labourer" runs on to the eve of Kant.** Variant: split off a new last stage, "Reason and the philosophe".
- *What it is.* Wolff: "scientia possibilium, quatenus esse possunt" and the knowledge of the reason of things. D'Alembert: philosophy is "le fruit de la raison", one of the three branches after Bacon, with the PHILOSOPHIE article taking over Wolff's definition. Hume: the two species of moral philosophy, the easy and the abstruse. Shaftesbury's "good-breeding a step higher" is optional.
- *Its point.* "Philosophe": an "honnête homme" for whom civil society is "une divinité … sur la terre", and reason is to him "ce que la grace est à l'égard du chretien". Hume: "sovereign antidote … to superstition and false religion"; "let your science be human".
- *How it is done.* Wolff: demonstration and the freedom to philosophise. Hume: "mental geography" and cultivating "true metaphysics … to destroy the false".

**Table.** Add rows to match whichever of A–C is taken:
- Identity and servant: the same as true religion; the handmaid.
- Arts faculty: "no more excellent state".
- Renaissance: a life rather than a disputation; "instructs us to live"; against the disputation.
- Eighteenth century: the science of possibles, reason's branch; the honnête homme, the antidote to superstition; demonstration, mental geography.

**Front matter.** If C is taken, change the description's "from the Greeks to Locke".
