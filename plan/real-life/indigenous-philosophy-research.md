# Indigenous philosophy: research note

Purpose: to record, for future work on the metaphilosophy subject, what Indigenous thinkers in Latin America today and in North America say the work of thought or philosophy is, in their own words; the key texts and dates; and the debates over applying the word "philosophy" and over who defines it. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open). "As quoted in" means the wording is checked in the secondary source named, not in the primary text. English glosses marked "my rendering" are mine.

Site check (2 November 2026): no page or mention exists for Reinaga, *indianismo*, *suma qamaña*, *sumak kawsay*, *buen vivir*, Mapuche *kimün*, Estermann, "Andean philosophy", Rivera Cusicanqui, Deloria, Cordova or Burkhart. Related material already on the site: the Latin America landing and *The knowers* (Nahua *tlamatini*, K'iche' *ilb'al*, Inca *amauta*, Guaman Poma's "yndios filósofos"); *A philosophy of our America?* (Kusch's *estar* over *ser*, quoted from *América profunda*); *After liberation* (Quijano, Lugones, Fornet-Betancourt); the cosmopolitics subject names Mignolo and de la Cadena; Africa's *Oral wisdom* and the ethnophilosophy debate give the closest parallel. ## 1. Latin America: the debate over "Andean philosophy"

### The proponent: Josef Estermann

- Who: born 1956 in Sursee, Switzerland; Swiss and Dutch; theology licentiate (Lucerne), philosophy licentiate (Amsterdam), PhD in philosophy (Utrecht); worked for the Bethlehem Mission in Peru (1990–98) and Bolivia (2004–12) [C, S1 author note and journal bio]. (English Wikipedia currently calls him "an Iranian philosopher" — an error; do not use.)
- Key text: *Filosofía andina: estudio intercultural de la sabiduría autóctona andina* (Quito: Abya-Yala, 1998); further editions La Paz (ISEAT, 2006) and Lima (2018) [C, S2]. Estermann himself gives the first publications under the name as Regalsky 1994, Miranda-Luizaga 1996, then his own 1998 [C, S1 n. 8].
- What it is, in his words (2018 ed., p. 77): "el conjunto de concepciones, modelos, ideas y categorías vividos por el runa/jaqui andino, es decir: la experiencia concreta y colectiva del ser humano andino en su universo físico y simbólico. Las concepciones 'filosóficas' en esta vivencia son 'praxológicas' e implícitas" — the conceptions, models, ideas and categories *lived* by the Andean person (Quechua *runa*, Aymara *jaqi*); its philosophical conceptions are "praxological" and implicit (my rendering) [C, as quoted in S2].
- Three types he distinguishes: (a) precolonial, "filosofía inka" or "del Tawantinsuyu"; (b) "indigenista", which "pretende conservar un pensamiento andino 'puro'"; (c) "intercultural", which "rechaza un esencialismo cultural andino". He has chosen (c) since 1998 [C, S1]. He places the Universidad Indígena Tawantinsuyu in El Alto, with careers in Indigenous philosophy and theology, "una suerte de escuela para amautas (sabios) e yatiris", in type (b) [C, S1 n. 10].
- Who does it and from what sources: the West requires "textos, … autoría individual, … lógica bivalente y … metodología analítica"; Andean philosophy rests on "una cultura básicamente oral o ágrafa, en la autoría colectiva o comunitaria, en una lógica incluyente y polivalente y en una metodología simbólica representativa" [C, S1]. Its sources are myths, customs, rituals, oral tradition and the Indigenous chronicles, with ethnography, linguistics, archaeology and historiography as auxiliary "ciencias"; it is "una empresa colectiva-comunitaria", and its recent named authors are "portavoces de una cosmovivencia colectiva" [C, S1].
- Content headline: "La relacionalidad es la característica principal de la Filosofía Andina, su axioma directriz", with correspondence, complementarity, reciprocity and cyclicity as its principles; his own terms include *pachasofía* and *cosmovivencia*, and he adapted Næss's "ecosofía" to the Andes (2007) [C, S1].
- On universality: no philosophy is universal a priori; the universal is built "mediante diálogos o polílogos continuos … de modo a posteriori", and Andean philosophy is "una de las partes en este polílogo filosófico, ni más ni menos" [C, S1]. Also (2018, p. 41): "Lo que es 'filosofía', no se puede definir monoculturalmente (desde una sola cultura), sino solo en el diálogo (o 'polílogo') intercultural" [C, as quoted in S2].
- He notes that liberation philosophy "tampoco … tomaba nota de una tradición filosófica milenaria de Abya Yala", naming Dussel, who changed only in the last twenty years [C, S1 and n. 6]. (Relevant to the existing *Liberation* page.)
- He names the objection itself: the academy holds it is not "filosofía" but "pensamiento", "cosmovisión", "espiritualidad" or "mitología"; "Tal como en la época de Hegel, Occidente siempre pretende definir monoculturalmente lo que es 'filosofía'…" [C, S1]. Critics he lists: Mansilla 2023, Hernández 2022, Sobrevilla 2018 ("Sobrevilla me considera erróneamente cura") [C, S1 n. 11].

### The critics: who defines it

- **David Sobrevilla** (Peruvian historian of philosophy): the project lacks basis "porque no distingue entre filosofía y no-filosofía"; he defends philosophy's Greek and Western origin, and writes that "la lectura del P. Estermann obliga a la cosmovisión andina a acomodarse a las categorías filosóficas occidentales, con el resultado de que él obtiene una filosofía andina muy semejante en sus partes a la filosofía occidental" (2007, p. 245) [C, as quoted and summarised in S2]. Hernández cites Sobrevilla 2007; Estermann cites Sobrevilla 2018 (*Solar* 4) — whether the same text, unchecked [open].
- **Mario Mejía Huamán** (Peruvian philosopher; his own project in *Hacia una filosofía andina*, 2005) objects from the Andean side: "Admitir que el runa andino … es ser filósofo, es un favor barato a los pueblos andinos. Hacernos creer que nosotros tenemos una filosofía propia y que podemos entrar en diálogo o discusión con cualquier filosofía de occidente es una ficción occidentalista … hoy, se nos quiere hacer concebir que somos filósofos y que por nuestra mente y venas fluye una filosofía de la que somos inconscientes y que precisamente es la filosofía que occidente necesita para humanizarse" (2005, p. 91); "haremos filosofía para resolver nuestros problemas teóricos … No nos interesa que nos levanten artificialmente, nos pongan a su nivel y nos den la palabra" (p. 92); "Más que hacer diálogo con los otros, necesitamos hacer diálogo entre nosotros" (p. 104) [C, as quoted in S2]. That Mejía is a Quechua speaker from the southern Andes [U]. This is the clearest "who defines it" voice found: philosophy as something Andeans will *do*, not something an outsider finds in them.
- **Javier Eduardo Hernández Soto** (UNMSM / Cayetano Heredia): sorts the critics as "negacionismo" (Sobrevilla), "etnocentrismo" (Mejía) and his own earlier "ontologismo"; his earlier view was that philosophy "no era una posibilidad originaria del horizonte de sentido del hombre andino", that one should speak of "pensar andino", and that "El filósofo suizo, al querer ser portavoz del runa, olvidaba que este tenía voz propia" [C, S2]. His present view: philosophy is "griega y europea, sí, pero esto no es ningún privilegio"; "Es preciso divorciar filosofía y dignidad humana"; Andean philosophy in a second sense is "la mirada filosófica que se lanza sobre" Andean culture, an interpretation, not an incarnation; yet "una vez aparecida la filosofía andina, no se puede volver a la postura ingenua que negaba su posibilidad" [C, S2]. Note the structural parallel to the African debate already on the site (Hountondji's ethnophilosophy critique against a philosophy found in a people's collective thought); none of these authors draws it in the passages read [C for absence in S1–S3 as read].
- **H. C. F. Mansilla** (Bolivian Academy of Sciences), "Una aproximación crítica a la filosofía andina" (2023): calls Estermann's the most important reconstruction and Andean philosophy a somewhat artificial reconstruction [U — search summary only; source blocked].
- **Zenón Depaz**, *La cosmovisión andina en el Manuscrito de Huarochirí* (2015), reads a seventeenth-century Quechua text through five Quechua categories, *pacha, yana, waka, kama, yachay*, chosen "por la densidad de significados de orden ontológico que conlleva" (Depaz 2015, p. 35) [C, as quoted in S3]. In Hernández's summary, *yachay* is knowing "en tanto experiencia integral. No se trata de un conocimiento puramente teórico, sino de un modo de estar en el mundo que articula verdad, bien y belleza. Yachay implica aprender, enseñar, cuidar y actuar adecuadamente" (summarising Depaz 2015, pp. 269–273) [C, S3]. Hernández's critique (2026): a Kantian "conditions of possibility" frame, a non-dialectical reading of *yana*, a metaphysical optimism in *kama* [C, S3]. This is the strongest lead for a Quechua word for knowing with a text behind it.

### Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui: the critique of the decolonial academy

- Bolivian sociologist (b. 1949), of Aymara ancestry, co-founder (1983) of the Taller de Historia Oral Andina [C, S19 agenda21 profile; birth year C only via Wikipedia].
- *Ch'ixinakax utxiwa: una reflexión sobre prácticas y discursos descolonizadores* (Buenos Aires: Tinta Limón, 2010) [C, S6]:
  - "No puede haber un discurso de la descolonización, una teoría de la descolonización, sin una práctica descolonizadora" (p. 62).
  - Official multiculturalism produced "un multiculturalismo ornamental y simbólico" (p. 58); speaking of peoples placed at the "origen" "se niega la coetaneidad de estas poblaciones y se las excluye de las lides de la modernidad" (p. 59).
  - "Mignolo y compañía han construído un pequeño imperio dentro del imperio" (p. 58); ideas travel south to north "convertidas en materia prima" and come back as finished product (p. 68); Patzi relied on Quijano and Mignolo "ignorando las ideas kataristas sobre el colonialismo interno … e incluso en los sesenta, en la pionera obra de Fausto Reinaga" (pp. 67–68).
  - Her own word: *ch'ixi*, a grey made of juxtaposed points of two colours "que se confunden para la percepción sin nunca mezclarse del todo", obeying "la lógica del tercero incluido"; against García Canclini's hybridity it "plantea la coexistencia en paralelo de múltiples diferencias culturales que no se funden, sino que antagonizan o se complementan" (pp. 69–70).
- On *vivir bien* in the plurinational state: "Por el momento el 'vivir bien' es una palabra hueca que no se basa en un conocimiento de las dimensiones concretas, locales, con las cuales la gente enfrenta la relación comunitaria y la relación con la naturaleza, con los muertos y el espacio sagrado" [C, S8, interview at the third Foro Andino Amazónico de Desarrollo Rural; dated 2015 by inference from the Fondo Indígena affair it mentions — U].

## 2. Latin America: Fausto Reinaga and *indianismo*

- Life: born Chayanta, northern Potosí, 27 March 1906; died La Paz, 19 August 1994 [C, S4 n. 2] (a search summary gives Macha, a town in the same area [U]).
- Periods, in Gustavo Cruz's division: Marxist-nationalist *indigenismo* c. 1934–60; *indianista* 1960–74; *amáutico* 1974–94, which Cruz reads as "una 'ruptura epistemológica'" with his own *indianismo*, "moral-mesiánico-científico", with humanity as its subject-object and "un cosmopolitismo escéptico de las reivindicaciones nacionales y raciales" [C, S5 and n. 6].
- The distinction, as the scholarship frames it from Reinaga 1970: *indigenismo* = political and cultural projects "pensados y realizados por no-indígenas con el propósito de incorporar, asimilar, incluir al indígena"; *indianismo* = a project "pensado por sujetos que se afirman como indios, que proponen la liberación del indio" [C, S4]. Reinaga rejects "indígena" and reclaims "indio", "invirtiendo el uso racista" [C, S4, the authors' paraphrase]. Indio is defined by culture and cause, not skin: "El color de la piel no es el patrón absoluto para medir el valor de la raza; puesto que también está ahí la cultura" (*La revolución india*, 1970, p. 40) [C, as quoted in S5].
- Key text: *La revolución india* (La Paz: PIB, 1970; cover dated December 1969 [U]), which includes the *Manifiesto del Partido Indio de Bolivia* [C, S4, S5]. Quotations (2001 ed.): "el indio para ser sujeto de la historia tiene que saber, por sobre todo, su historia" (p. 383); "en Bolivia hay dos Bolivias: la Bolivia del cholaje y la Bolivia del indio … El indio es una Nación" (p. 433); the Inca moral "ama súa, ama llulla, ama khella, es mandato cósmico al mismo tiempo que imperativo de la naturaleza" (p. 440) [C, as quoted in S4].
- On thought: the late books carry the metaphilosophical claim in their titles — *La razón y el indio* (1978), *El pensamiento amáutico* (1978), *Sócrates y yo* (1983) [C for titles and dates, S4, S5]. In *La revolución amáutica*: "el campo de la batalla del pensamiento mundial queda definido en dos frentes, el pensamiento socrático y el pensamiento amáutico" (p. 16) [C only as quoted in a hostile newspaper column, S7; U against the book]. Cruz names the late utopia "el Reino de la Verdad y la Vida" [U, abstract only]. *Amauta* ties back to Garcilaso's gloss already on the Latin America landing.
- Who defines it: Cruz and Cruz set Kusch's "interpretación 'externa'" of the Quechua-Aymara world against Reinaga, who built *indianismo* "desde las entrañas del mundo quechua-aymara, para liberarse de todo Occidente" [C, S4]. Bonfil Batalla read the indianist golden age as a "nuevo proyecto de sociedad futura" rather than a reconstruction of the past [C, as reported in S4].
- Reception headline: mixed. Rivera Cusicanqui credits his "pionera obra" [C, S6]; a Bolivian columnist calls his late work megalomania [C, S7]. That the Bolivian state under Morales took him up (a vice-presidential selection of his writings was announced) [C, S7, as the columnist reports; U otherwise].

## 3. Latin America: *suma qamaña* and *sumak kawsay*

- Constitutional texts. Bolivia 2009, Art. 8.I: "El Estado asume y promueve como principios ético-morales de la sociedad plural: ama qhilla, ama llulla, ama suwa (no seas flojo, no seas mentiroso ni seas ladrón), suma qamaña (vivir bien), ñandereko (vida armoniosa), teko kavi (vida buena), ivi maraei (tierra sin mal) y qhapaj ñan (camino o vida noble)" [C, S9]. Ecuador 2008, preamble: "una nueva forma de convivencia ciudadana, en diversidad y armonía con la naturaleza, para alcanzar el buen vivir, el sumak kawsay"; also Arts. 14 and 275 [C, S10].
- A sorting of knowledge in law: Ecuador's constitution sets "saberes ancestrales" beside science — a section "Ciencia, tecnología, innovación y saberes ancestrales"; Art. 385 gives the national system the aims to "Generar, adaptar y difundir conocimientos científicos y tecnológicos" and to "Recuperar, fortalecer y potenciar los saberes ancestrales"; Art. 57.12 guarantees peoples' "conocimientos colectivos; sus ciencias, tecnologías y saberes ancestrales" [C, S10]. This is the clearest state-level sorting of Indigenous knowledge found, and it fits the subject's "how it relates to other knowledge" question.
- The debates, as mapped by Hidalgo-Capitán and Cubillo-Guevara (2014), six of them: meaning, translation, origin, cultural paradigm, relation to development, direction [C, S11]:
  - Translation: Indigenist authors hold "buen vivir"/"vivir bien" wrong, since *sumak*/*suma* also mean "lo pleno" and "lo completo"; better "vida plena" or "vida en plenitud"; "buen vivir" would render *alli kawsay* [C, S11].
  - Origin: one reading calls it a "tradición inventada" (Viola 2011: 272); the authors describe Viteri (2000, 2003) for the Amazonian Kichwa of Sarayaku and Rengifo (2002) for Peruvian Quechua and Aymara communities as making it "emerge" ("enactúan") rather than inventing or discovering it [C, S11]. A search summary dates the term's first organisational use to the OPIP Amazanga plan of 1992 [U].
  - Currents: socialist-statist, ecologist-postdevelopmentalist, and "indigenista y 'pachamamista'", the last including Choquehuanca, Huanacuni, Macas, Pacari, Yampara and Medina; criticised for "pachamamismo" (Stefanoni 2010) [C, as reported in S11].
  - Equivalents the authors name, spellings as found: "ñandareko guaraní, … shin pujut awajún, … kyme mogen mapuche, … utz kaslemal quiché … lekil kuxlejal tseltal" [C, S11 n. 1]. Standard spellings not checked.
- Estermann reads *allin kawsay / suma qamaña* as the ethical horizon of Andean philosophy, the human as "ecósofo" and "cuidante" (*arariwa*) [C, S1]. Rivera Cusicanqui's "palabra hueca" is the main Indigenous critique of the state's use [C, S8].
- Headline judgement: the strand is as much a political-constitutional discourse as a philosophy; whether it belongs on a metaphilosophy page turns on the saberes-ancestrales sorting and the translation/origin dispute, not on its policy career.

## 4. Latin America: Mapuche *kimün*

- Juan Ñanculef Huaiquinao, *Tayiñ mapuche kimün: epistemología mapuche, sabiduría y conocimientos* (Santiago: Universidad de Chile, 2016) [C, S12]. Mapuche author; tells of being announced in his mother's dream (*pewma*) as a *wülzugufe*, "dador de conocimiento"; trained with *kimche* (sages) such as Antonio Levicura [C, S12]. The preface presents him as a recognised *kimche* [C, S12].
- His words (page numbers of the PDF edition, approximate):
  - "mapuche kimün es el compendio del conocimiento mapuche, y abarca todo el bagaje del ser humano, la naturaleza plena y todas las dimensiones…" (n. 2, c. p. 16) [C].
  - "Tanto la lengua mapuche, como la filosofía, el pensamiento, la religiosidad y la cultura mapuche, constituyen un gran todo inseparable, que no se puede dividir, ni separar sus contenidos por materia, y menos en ramos a estudiar o aprender como lo hacen desde occidente" (p. 20) [C] — a refusal of the Western sorting into subjects.
  - "La filosofía mapuche es pragmática" (p. 21); "Nosotros no aprendemos 'cómo hacer las cosas', sino que simplemente aprendemos a 'hacer las cosas'" (p. 20) [C].
  - Method: *inarrumen*, "una metodología del aprendizaje intuitiva", observing slowly; "La metodología del kimün es el inarrumen" (pp. 22, 45) [C].
  - *Rakizuam*: from *raki*, "to count"; "la acción de contar en forma lógica lo que se viene a la cabeza, la acción del pensar. Rakizuam es la forma ordenada y lógica de la acción del pensamiento propio y único del chegen, esto es del ser humano" (p. 43) [C].
  - "El kimün es la ciencia del conocimiento mapuche, la base de todo" (p. 45) [C].
  - Word test: he uses *kimün* also for others' knowledge — Descartes's cogito is "aquel principio del wigka kimün" (p. 43) [C]. *Kimün* is knowledge in general, owned by whoever holds it, not a word for philosophy as such; he uses "filosofía mapuche" himself, in Spanish.
  - On translation: missionary renderings "nos tergiversaron el valor original de la palabra"; *epew* rendered "mito" reads as "un mero cuento" (c. p. 16); writing in Castilian is "una paradoja necesaria" [C].
- Albertsen and Zuchel (2019), non-Mapuche philosophers, ask whether *rakiduam* (another spelling) can simply be translated "pensamiento", noting the Universidad de Chile titled a 2014 showcase book *Rakiduam*; they describe *kimün* "como un conocimiento oral, o que se intercambia de modo oral y directo a través del dialogo (nütram)" [C, S13]. The spelling variants (*rakizuam / rakiduam*, *kimün / kimvn*) follow different Mapuzugun alphabets [U].
- Other leads not opened: Marimán, Caniuqueo, Millalén and Levil, *¡…Escucha, winka…!* (2006), a Mapuche historians' collective; Elisa Loncon's work on Mapuche philosophy; Ricardo Salas, "Filosofía occidental y filosofía mapuche" (2011) [U].
- Detail not to stage: Ñanculef credits the *kimche* with knowing heliocentrism and twelve planets (p. 22) [C]. Not a headline; leave out.

## 5. North America: Indigenous philosophy as a tradition of its own

### Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005)

- Dakota family (Yankton), enrolled Standing Rock Sioux; *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969); professor at Arizona and Colorado [C, S19 Wikipedia only — U against a primary biography].
- Who defines Indigenous knowledge — the academy's sorting: "Minorities were believed to have little to contribute to human knowledge"; "all courses and publications on Indians were classified as anthropology"; "No one ever believed that racial minorities might have their own point of view" ("Indian Studies—The Orphan of Academia", *Wicazo Sa Review* 2:2, 1986, pp. 1–2) [C, S14]. The 1969 chapter "Anthropologists and Other Friends" [C, S19; book not opened].
- Two worlds: "The fundamental factor that keeps Indians and non-Indians from communicating is that they are speaking about two entirely different perceptions of the world" (*The Metaphysics of Modern Existence*, 1979; 2012 ed., p. 1) [C, as quoted in S15].
- Knowledge: "Non-Western knowledge is believed to originate from primitive efforts to explain a mysterious universe…" (*Spirit & Reason*, 1999, p. 41); "In the Indian conception, it was impossible that there could be abstract propositions that could be used to explore the structure of the physical world. Knowledge was derived from individual and communal experiences in daily life, in keen observation of the environment, and in interpretive messages that they received from spirits in ceremonies, visions, and dreams" (p. 44); "Western science today is akin to a world history that discusses only the Mediterranean peoples" (p. 101) [C, as quoted in S15].
- With Daniel Wildcat, *Power and Place: Indian Education in America* (2001): "power and place produce personality"; the universe is "personal and, therefore, must be approached in a personal manner" (p. 23) [C, as quoted in S15].
- *God Is Red* (1973): space against time as the line between Native and Western religion; the closing "for this land, God is red" (p. ix in a later edition) [C for the closing line via Wikiquote; U for the space/time passage, from memory: "American Indians hold their lands—places—as having the highest possible meaning…"; check in the book].
- The science dispute (a headline of his reception): *Red Earth, White Lies* (1995) rejects the Bering land-bridge account; he gives its political motive as the wish to believe the hemisphere "was a vacant, unexploited, fertile land" (1997 ed., pp. 67–68) [C, as quoted in S15]. Critics: H. David Brumble, "Vine Deloria, Jr., Creationism, and Ethnic Pseudoscience" (*American Literary History* 10:2, 1998), charging "affirmative action science" [C, as reported in S15]; Wikipedia adds that he "embraced human–dinosaur coexistence" [C, S19; U against the book]. Defenders: Southall argues Brumble misreads him; Wildcat (2005) that Deloria treated Indian knowledges "as knowledges that should be taken seriously for contemporary and practical purposes" [C, S15].
- Word test: his book titles speak of "religion", "metaphysics", "reason" and "education"; whether he ever called his own work "philosophy" was not checked [U]. Burkhart reports that Deloria insisted Burkhart complete a PhD in philosophy so that Native philosophy would not be seen as "the stage in human development in which superstition and ignorance reigned supreme" [C, as quoted in S18].

### Viola F. Cordova (1937–2002)

- Born 20 October 1937 to a Hispanic mother and a Jicarilla Apache father; raised in Taos; MA thesis "Conceptual frameworks as a source of cultural distinctions"; PhD in philosophy, University of New Mexico, 1992, dissertation "The concept of monism in Navajo thought"; taught at Alaska-Fairbanks, UNM, Idaho State, Oregon State, Colorado State; died 2 November 2002 [C, S16]. "The first Native American woman to receive a PhD in philosophy" is the publisher's claim [C, S17]; Wikipedia says "one of the first" [C, S19]. Report as the publisher's claim.
- *How It Is: The Native American Philosophy of V. F. Cordova*, ed. Kathleen Dean Moore, Kurt Peters, Ted Jojola and Amber Lacy (Tucson: University of Arizona Press, 2007), posthumous, in three parts: the nature of reality; becoming human through relation to people and places; a reverence with no line between sacred and mundane [C, S17]. "How It Is: A Native American Creation Story" was already her own title in 1994 [C, S16]. The publisher says she contrasts Native thought with "Euroman" traditions [U — page summary only].
- Her own words: "If you speak for me, you rob me of my voice" (*How It Is*, p. 42); on her path: "The view that ideas had origins led me to philosophy. Philosophy, I thought, was uniquely prepared to deal with questions about origins of ideas and concepts" (source not given on the page); "The minority student has not only the 'right' to engage in philosophical discourse, but the duty to do so … Philosophy Departments must open their doors to the new questions posed by persons formerly absent from their ranks" ("Western Thought: An Outsider's Perspective", Colorado State, 1994); on Western history of the world as a "climb": "There is, in this perspective, no truly 'other'" ("A Sense of Place", 1994); "I am not an adherent of the school that demands that works of 'dead white males' be overthrown" [all C, S16].
- Reception: one review (Project MUSE, not opened) found the essays closer to "philosophical editorials" shaped by academia than by Apache experience [U, search summary]. Southall reports she compared the Native conception of the whole to Spinoza [C, S15].

### Brian Burkhart

- Cherokee philosopher [C, S18]; citizen of the Cherokee Nation, University of Oklahoma [U, event listing via search]; earlier published as Brian Yazzie Burkhart [U]. Deloria was his mentor and external examiner of his PhD [C, S18].
- "What Coyote and Thales Can Teach Us: An Outline of American Indian Epistemology", in Anne Waters (ed.), *American Indian Thought: Philosophical Essays* (Blackwell, 2004): "meaning and value arise in the intersection between us and all that is around us" (p. 16) [C, as quoted in S18].
- *Indigenizing Philosophy through the Land: A Trickster Methodology for Decolonizing Environmental Ethics and Indigenous Futures* (East Lansing: Michigan State University Press, 2019) [C, S18, S17]:
  - "Locality is being-from-the-land and knowing-from-the-land. I use 'locality' as a term of art in this book as a way to reference the manner in which being, meaning, and knowing are rooted in the land" (p. xiv) [C, as quoted in S18]. Delocality: meaning, being and knowledge "floating free from the land", which Miller glosses as Western philosophy's way [C, S18].
  - "Indigenizing philosophy is then, in part, making it more active and dynamic … a movement back to the land, regrounding our language, being, knowing, meaning, and so on back in the land" (p. xxv) [C, as quoted in S18].
  - Against "ethnographic containment", in which "Indigenous voices only have meaning as a form of ethnography, which would mean that the truth or value of their words is determined by the ethnographic authenticity of their words rather than truth or value in a broader sense" (p. xxi) [C, as quoted in S18].
  - Publisher: Indigenous ways of knowing "not as artifact that Western philosophy can incorporate into its canon, but rather as a force of anticolonial Indigenous liberation" [C, S17].
  - Method: Iktomi, the Lakota spider trickster, and Black Elk; a chapter "Refragmenting Philosophy through the Land" [C for Iktomi and Black Elk, S18; U for the chapter title].
- Debate inside the field: McPherson and Rabb (Lakehead), in Nanabush's voice, ask "Why Indigenize philosophy? … Does this not go counter to Indigenous pluralism and noninterference?" and suggest Burkhart "is not really Indigenizing philosophy, he is rather engaged in Indigenous philosophizing" [C, S18].

### The field and its names

- McKenna and Pratt, *American Philosophy: From Wounded Knee to the Present* (2015): "It would not be misleading to say that American Indian philosophy is a 'new' field. Although Native American thinkers have been discussing and publishing on questions easily recognized as philosophical for centuries, few of these thinkers have carried out their discussion and writing in the context of the North American academy" (p. 295) [C, as quoted in S18].
- The APA's newsletter, first "on American Indians in Philosophy", is now the *APA Newsletter on Native American and Indigenous Philosophy* (title on the 2020 issue) [C, S18]; renaming approved 2017 [U]. Anne Waters's 2004 volume is titled *American Indian Thought* [C].
- Other names to weigh for a folder (not researched): Anne Waters (Seminole), Thomas Norton-Smith (Shawnee), *The Dance of Person and Place* (2010); Dale Turner (Anishinaabe), *This Is Not a Peace Pipe* (2006), on "word warriors"; Gregory Cajete, *Native Science* (2000); Kyle Powys Whyte; Shay Welch (2019). Earlier written voices — Charles Eastman, *The Soul of the Indian* (1911); *Black Elk Speaks* (1932) and the dispute over Neihardt's hand — would be this tradition's "knowers" page [all U].

## Placement proposal (options, not decisions)

**Latin America.** The folder already ends its colonial-to-present line with *After liberation*; nothing yet covers Indigenous voices after the conquest-era knowers. Options:

- **A1, one page** "Indigenous voices" (or similar) after *After liberation*: Reinaga's *indianismo* against *indigenismo* and his *pensamiento amáutico*; Rivera Cusicanqui's practice-before-theory and *ch'ixi*; *suma qamaña / sumak kawsay* with the saberes-ancestrales sorting and the translation/origin dispute; Ñanculef's *kimün*, *rakizuam*, *inarrumen*. Answers the three questions directly (activity: *kimün*, *yachay*; point: liberation, *vivir bien*; method: *inarrumen*, oral *nütram*, practice).
- **A2, two pages**: (i) "Andean philosophy?" — the debate over the name and who defines it (Estermann; Sobrevilla; Mejía Huamán; Hernández; Depaz), parallel in shape to Africa's ethnophilosophy page; (ii) "Indigenous voices" as in A1 without the debate. A2 keeps the dispute from crowding the voices, and keeps Estermann (a Swiss scholar whom Hernández faults for wanting to be "portavoz del runa") from heading the Indigenous page.
- Landing: either option means a new line under *The lines*, and possibly new own words in the opening paragraph (*yachay*, *kimün*, *rakizuam*) — only once the pages back them. The *Liberation* page could take Estermann's remark that liberation philosophy passed over Indigenous thought, as a reception note.

**North America.** Options:

- **B1, a ninth tradition folder**, "Indigenous North America" (or "Native North America"; the field's own names are "American Indian thought/philosophy" and "Native American and Indigenous philosophy"). A landing with the field's names and McKenna and Pratt's "new field" remark, then pages such as: (1) the earlier written voices (Eastman, Black Elk and Neihardt, Ella Deloria — needs a research round); (2) Deloria's critique of anthropology, science and Western metaphysics, with Brumble and the defenders; (3) philosophers in the academy — Cordova, Waters's volume, the APA newsletter, Norton-Smith, Turner; (4) land and locality — Deloria and Wildcat, Burkhart, the Indigenizing debate with McPherson and Rabb. The hub list and its "eight traditions" count would change.
- **B2, a smaller start**: one or two pages in a folder whose landing says plainly that the pre-contact record is oral and nation-specific; grow later. Avoids filling a template the sources cannot yet back.
- **B3, not a folder**: hold North America for now; only Deloria's critique of science enters elsewhere (e.g. as reception on a science-related subject). Weakest on the equal-footing aim of the subject.

**Person pages — questions, not decisions.**

- Deloria: case on own standing (widely read critique of Western science and metaphysics; a principal of the field). Is his weight within SPLectrum high enough? His science dispute would sit on his page.
- Rivera Cusicanqui: case on own standing (*ch'ixi*; the practice-before-theory critique of the decolonial academy). A person page or external marker?
- Reinaga: principal of *indianismo*; earns a page only if *indianismo* is treated as an important subject here.
- Cordova: a principal of the North American field and its first PhDs; likely external unless B1 is chosen and she carries a page's weight.
- Burkhart, Estermann, Ñanculef, Mejía Huamán, Hernández, Depaz: external, as the material stands.
