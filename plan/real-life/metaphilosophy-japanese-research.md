# Metaphilosophy in Japan: research note

Purpose: to gather material for a page on Japan beside the China, India and other place pages of the Metaphilosophy subject. For each stage it asks the subject's three questions (1. What is philosophy? 2. What is its point? 3. How is it done?), with the tradition's own words and the Japanese original for key terms. Headlines only. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C as quoted]** = the wording is checked as it appears in a secondary source I opened; the primary was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search summary, or a source I could not open). "My rendering" = mine, from the Japanese text I fetched.

**Access.** The archive.org copies of the key English books are lending-only, and search-inside was refused: Heisig, Kasulis and Maraldo, *Japanese Philosophy: A Sourcebook* (2011); Tanahashi, *Moon in a Dewdrop* (1985); Tanabe, *Philosophy as Metanoetics*, tr. Takeuchi (1986); Nishida, *An Inquiry into the Good*, tr. Abe and Ives (1990). I found no online text of Sorai's *Bendō* or Chōmin's *Ichinen yūhan*. In their place I used Aozora Bunko, Japanese Wikisource, the Sōtōshū translation, the 1972 Waddell–Abe article, the SEP, and Japanese and Chinese journal papers.

## 1. The words and the name

**Before *tetsugaku*.**
- ***Gaku* 学, learning.** Kasulis: it "signified learning, especially in the sense of modeling oneself after a textual or human model (that is, a master text or a personal master)". In Meiji the same word was the suffix for the new disciplines, "functioning as an equivalent for the German *Wissenschaft*" [C, J1 §1].
- ***Michi* / *dō* 道, the Way.** Kasulis: the premodern philosopher was "a sage who has mastery in one of the traditional *Ways* of wisdom (*dō* or *michi*)": the Way of the buddhas, of the Confucian sages, of the kami, or of the arts (calligraphy, tea, pottery, the martial arts) [C, J1 §1]. **Word test:** *tetsujin* ("wise people"), Kasulis's name for this premodern sage, is his own label: "for convenience in this article *tetsujin* will appear throughout" [C, J1]. It is not the tradition's word.
- **Names for Confucianism.** *Jugaku* (the learning of the scholars), *Jukyō*, *seigaku* (the learning of the sages), *seirigaku*, *rigaku* (the learning of principle), *shingaku* (the learning of the mind). Matsunaga Sekigo (1640): "Between heaven and earth, there are three major ways": the Confucian, the Buddhist and the Daoist [C, J2 §1].
- ***Kokugaku* 国学.** It has been translated as "Japan Studies", "National Learning", "nativism" and "essentialism" [C, J3 §1]. The SEP gives "Native Studies" (Kasulis) and "Native Japan Studies" (Fujiwara).

**Nishi's path to the word** [C as quoted, J10, citing *Nishi Amane zenshū* vol. 1]:
- **1861.** In a postface to Tsuda Mamichi's *Seiriron*, Nishi writes 「特至吾希哲学一科、則未見其人矣」 (p. 13). *Kitetsugaku* 希哲学, roughly "the learning that aspires to wisdom" (my gloss), names philosophy.
- **1862.** His lecture notes on Western philosophy explain that Socrates' *philosophos* means "a person who loves wise virtue", 「所謂希賢の意と均しかるべし」, equal to the sense of "aspiring to worthiness" (p. 16).
- **The source of 希.** Zhou Dunyi's *Tongshu*, "Aspiring to learning": 「聖希天、賢希聖、士希賢」, the sage aspires to Heaven, the worthy to the sage, the scholar to the worthy [C as quoted, J10; J11].
- ***Seisei hatsuun* (1871–73).** Philosophy is 「周茂叔ノ所謂ル士希賢ノ意ナリ、後世ノ習用ニテ専ラ理ヲ講スル学ヲ指ス、理学理論ナト訳スルヲ直訳トスレドモ、他ニ紛ルコト多キ為メニ今哲学ト訳シ東洲ノ儒学ニ分ツ」 (p. 31). My rendering: "it is what Zhou Maoshu called 'the scholar aspires to the worthy'; in later usage it means the learning that discourses solely on principle. *Rigaku* or *riron* would be the literal rendering, but since these are easily confused with other things, I now translate it *tetsugaku* and distinguish it from the Confucianism of the Eastern continent." This is the primary behind the hinge's "not be confused with Confucianism".
- ***Hyakuichi shinron*** (written 1866–67, published 1874), Nishi's definition: 「天道人道を論明して、兼て教の方法を立つるをヒロソヒー、訳して哲学と名け」 (p. 289). My rendering: "to make clear the Way of Heaven and the Way of man, and also to establish the method of teaching, is called *philosophy*, which I translate and name *tetsugaku*."
- **The order of the words.** Lin Mei-Mao gives the sequence as 希哲学 → 希賢学 → 哲学 [C, J11]. He quotes Nishi's *Kaidaimon* as well: 「東土謂之儒、西洲謂之斐鹵蘇比、皆明天道而立人極、其実一也」, "the Eastern land calls it *ju* [Confucian learning], the Western continent *philosophy*; both make clear the Way of Heaven and establish the human pole; in substance they are one" [C as quoted, J11, citing *Zenshū* 1 p. 19].
- **Tucker:** *tetsu* alludes to the *Book of History*, where it names the wisdom of the ancient sages. He adds that Nishi "read Ogyū Sorai's philosophical works before turning to the study of the ideas of Auguste Comte and John Stuart Mill" [C, J2 §5].
- **Rival word.** In the 1880s "some professors favored an older Confucian term, *rigaku* 理学" [C, J12 p. 5]. That Chōmin kept *rigaku* in *Ichinen yūhan* is [U, search summary].

**The naming dispute (headline).**
- **Inoue Tetsujirō** (1855–1944) lectured on the "History of Eastern Philosophy" (*Tōyō tetsugaku shi*) at Tokyo from late 1882 [C as quoted, J11, after Satō Masayuki]. His recollection (1932): 「西洋の哲学者は希臘以来の哲学のみを哲学として考へたが間違ひである。印度だの支那の哲学も考慮なければならぬ」, Western philosophers thought only the philosophy since Greece was philosophy, and that was a mistake; the philosophy of India and China must also be considered [C as quoted, J11].
- **His trilogy** on the Wang Yangming school (1900), the school of Ancient Learning (1902) and the Zhu Xi school (1905). In Tucker's account Inoue stood "as Hegel's antithesis" and suggested Japan had "a substantial philosophical tradition of its own"; "It is no exaggeration to say that the study of Japanese Confucianism as philosophy began with Inoue's trilogy" [C, J2 §1]. Tucker adds that the trilogy's three-school dialectic "simply does not accurately represent developments", and that the reading was "overtly nationalistic" [C, J2 §§1, 4].
- **Chōmin (1901)** said "no such thing as philosophy in Japan", and "He directed this remark at both Japan's past thinkers and the 'philosophy professors' then at the University of Tokyo" (Maraldo) [C, J12 p. 5]. The surrounding passage, in which Norinaga and Atsutane are dismissed as antiquarians and Jinsai and Sorai as mere classicists, is [U].

## 2. Kūkai (774–835), headline only

- **Q3.** *Benkenmitsu nikyōron*: "exoteric Buddhism emphasizes the intellectual study and comprehension of the doctrines set forth in the sūtras", while "esoteric Buddhism provides a bodily hermeneutic whereby esoteric truths … can be experienced" (Krummel) [C, J4 §3.2]. In the same summary the Dharma is "perpetually expressed in and as its embodiment in the phenomenal world"; it is not beyond words.
- **Q1.** *Himitsu mandara jūjūshinron* (c. 830) classifies all teachings as ten states of mind, with Shingon at the top. "It is Kūkai's own invention however to associate the different doctrines evaluated and classified with specific states of mind" (Krummel) [C, J4 §3.12].
- **A reading (Kasulis).** Kūkai "demonstrated that a strong philosophical position should not only be able to show weaknesses in other positions, but also should be able to explain how such an error or misunderstanding could occur": "assimilation through relegation is a stronger position than simple refutation" [C, J1 §4.1.1]. This is Kasulis's account, not Kūkai's wording.
- I opened no primary line. If the page wants one, the Sourcebook's "The Ten Mindsets" (pp. 51ff.) is the place [open check].

## 3. Dōgen (1200–1253): "Genjōkōan"

- **The text.** Completed in 1233 for a lay disciple. Dōgen placed it at the head of the 75-fascicle *Shōbōgenzō* (colophon of 1252) [C, J6 p. 129].
- **The Japanese** (Kawamura ed., [01:8], DZZ 1:3) [C, J5]: 「佛道をならふといふは、自己をならふなり。自己をならふといふは、自己をわするるなり。自己をわするるといふは、萬法に證せらるるなり。萬法に證せらるるといふは、自己の身心および他己の身心をして脱落せしむるなり。」
- **Three translations, all opened:**
  - Waddell and Abe (1972, p. 133): "To learn the Buddha Way is to learn one's own self. To learn one's self is to forget one's self. To forget one's self is to be confirmed by all dharmas. To be confirmed by all dharmas is to effect the casting off one's own body and [mind]…" [C, J6; the page break is uncertain in the OCR].
  - Tanahashi (in *Enlightenment Unfolds*, 1999, ed. Tanahashi, as reprinted by Tricycle): "To study the Buddha Way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be actualized by myriad things. When actualized by myriad things, your body and mind as well as the bodies and minds of others drop away" [C, J21].
  - Sōtō Zen Text Project (ed. Bielefeldt, 2023): "To study the way of the buddhas is to study oneself. To study oneself is to forget oneself. To forget oneself is to be verified by the myriad dharmas. To be verified by the myriad dharmas is to slough off one's own body and mind and the body and mind of others" [C, J5].
- **Word test.** Dōgen's object is 佛道 *butsudō*, "the Buddha Way", not philosophy: the 道 again. *Narau* is "learn" (Waddell–Abe) or "study" (Tanahashi, Sōtōshū). That the word also carries "learn by imitating, get used to" is my gloss [U, check a dictionary]. 證 *shō* is "confirmed", "actualized" or "verified" depending on the translator; the choice is the translator's.
- **The direction of knowing** ([01:4]): 「自己をはこびて萬法を修證するを迷とす、萬法すすみて自己を修證するはさとりなり」, "Bringing the self to practice and verify the myriad dharmas represents delusion; the myriad dharmas proceeding to practice and verify the self [is awakening]" (Sōtōshū) [C, J5].
- **Method.** The Sōtō school "follows a method called 'just sitting' (*shikan taza*)", "practice-realization" (*shushō*), in which "meditation is not a means to an end" (Nagatomo) [C, J7 §2]. Kasulis: Dōgen "selected … the nondichotomous state of bodymind achieved in seated meditation (*zazen*) under the guidance of a master" [C, J1 §4.2.1].
- **Reception (headline).** Dōgen is read as a philosopher only from the twentieth century on. Maraldo gives "works that deal with Dōgen's philosophy of being and time" as an example of retrospective reading [C, J12 p. 7]. Tanabe's "The Philosophy of Dōgen" is in the Sourcebook [C, J13, title only]. The dates of Watsuji's and Tanabe's Dōgen essays are [U].
- **On the questions.** Q1: study of the Buddha Way, which is study of the self. Q2: to forget the self and be verified by the myriad things. Q3: *zazen* as practice-realization.

## 4. Edo Confucianism (1600–1868)

- **Setting.** Philosophy's "center of activity shifted from the Buddhist temples to secular urban academies supported by student tuition", and "This commodification of knowledge led to sharp competition among schools of thought" (Kasulis) [C, J1 §4.3.1].
- **Q1 (Tucker's headline).** "Perhaps the unifying feature of Confucian philosophizing in the early modern period was the view that words were crucially important as vehicles for substantial meaning … Japanese Confucian philosophy can be viewed as an East Asian philosophy of language engaged in a search for right meaning", against a Buddhist view of words as empty [C, J2 §4].
- **Q3: doubt.** Kaibara Ekken's *Taigiroku* opens by quoting Lu Xiangshan, "In learning, a person should be worried when he has no doubts", and cites Zhu Xi, "Those without doubts make no progress" [C, J2 §4.3].
- **Q3: reading.** Hayashi Razan: "Read them horizontally! Read them vertically! … When you realize a mystical unity with the sages' writings, one in which the self and those texts are non-dual, you will have read them well!" [C, J2 §4.1].
- **Q3: emulation.** "According to Zhu Xi, learning involved most basically the process of emulation", and Jinsai's model for it was calligraphy [C, J2 §4.8].
- **Itō Jinsai (1627–1705)**, preface to *Gomō jigi* (as translated in Tucker's entry; no translator is named there): "I teach students to scrutinize the *Analects* and *Mencius* thoroughly … Detailed philological studies of the *Analects* and *Mencius* facilitate understanding of those sagely texts and keep students from erratically manipulating them by imposing their subjective thoughts on them" [C, J2 §4.1]. He held those two books sufficient and argued that the *Great Learning* "was not a Confucian text" [C, J2 §4.8].
- **Ogyū Sorai (1666–1728), the book.** *Bendō* (辨道, "Distinguishing the Way") was written in 1717 and printed in 1737, in 25 articles. It argues that knowledge of 古文辞, the ancient phrasing, is indispensable for understanding the Way; it attacks the Song learning of principle; and it "greatly influenced" Norinaga's *kokugaku* [C, J8].
- **Sorai's lines:**
  - Opening (as quoted by Heibonsha): 「道は知り難く、また言い難し。その大なるがためなり」, the Way is hard to know and hard to speak of, because it is great [C as quoted, J8].
  - 「先王之道，先王所造也，非天地自然之道也」, the Way of the former kings was made by the former kings; it is not the natural Way of heaven and earth (*Bendō*, p. 15 of the 1931 *Dai Nihon shisō zenshū* vol. 7) [C as quoted, J14; my rendering].
  - 「先王之教，以物不以理…物者众理所聚也，而必从事焉者久之，乃心实知之，何假言也」, the kings taught by things, not by principle; things gather many principles, and only after long practice does the mind truly know them, with no need of words (*Bendō*, pp. 35–36) [C as quoted, J14].
  - 「道者統名也…辟諸人由道路以行，故謂之道」, "the Way is a comprehensive name … like people walking along a road, hence it is called the Way". **Locator: this is *Benmei* (辨名, "Distinguishing Names"), not *Bendō*** [C as quoted, J14 note 26].
- **Sorai through Tucker.** Sorai "valued learning gained through practice in following the way … knowing how to do something rather than knowing that something is the case"; for him Confucius "had not been fully a sage" [C, J2 §4.8]. Tucker's translation of both books (*Ogyū Sorai's Philosophical Masterworks: The Bendō and Benmei*, 2006) is [C, J2 bibliography, title only].
- **On the questions (Sorai).** Q1: the Way of the former kings, a made thing. Q2: 「安天下」, to bring peace to the realm [C as quoted, J14]. Q3: philology of the ancient words, plus practice of the rites and music.

## 5. Kokugaku (Native Studies)

- **Kamo no Mabuchi (1697–1769).** "Ancient Japan was governed well in accordance with the spirit of heaven and earth, and there was none of this petty sophistry; but then suddenly when these convincing theories were imported from China …" (*Kokui kō*). His method was reciting the oldest *Man'yōshū* verses aloud, so that one's "language and heart (*kokoro*) are transported back to the ancient past" [C, J3 §3.1].
- **Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801), *Naobi no mitama* (1771).** Japanese Wikisource, base text Mitsuya Shigematsu ed. 1916, pp. 55–80 [C, J15]. All renderings are mine:
  - 「古の大御世には、道といふ言擧もさらになかりき」: in the great reign of antiquity there was no lifting-up of words (*kotoage*) about a "Way" at all.
  - 「美知とは…たゞ物にゆく路ぞ、これをおきては、上つ代に、道といふものはなかりしぞかし」: *michi* is only the road one takes to go somewhere; apart from that, there was no such thing as a "Way" in the earliest age.
  - 「物のことわりあるべきすべ、萬の敎へごとをしも、何の道くれの道といふことは、異國のさだなり」: to call the principles of things and every teaching "this Way" or "that Way" is the custom of foreign countries.
  - 「己々がさかしらごゝろを心とするは、漢意の移れるなり」: to take one's own clever heart for one's heart is the Chinese heart (*karagokoro*) passed over to us.
  - **Method:** 「きたなきからぶみごゝろを祓ひきよめて淸々しき御國ごゝろもて、古典どもをよく學びてよ、然せば、受行ふべき道なきことは、おのづから知りてむ、其をしるぞ、すなはち神の道をうけおこなふにはありける」: purify away the soiled Chinese-book heart, and with the clear heart of the august land study the ancient texts well; then you will know of yourself that there is no Way to be received and practised, and to know that is to receive and practise the Way of the kami.
  - **On arguing:** 「かゝれば如此まで論ふも、道の意にはあらねども」: even to argue this far is not in the spirit of the Way.
  - **Context the page must carry.** The same text calls the Chinese sages "only thieves who succeeded" (「聖人もたゞ賊の爲とげたる者」) and makes following the emperor's heart the Way [C, J15]. Kokugaku later "went beyond its original philological and textual interests to construct a Shintō theory of racial purity", a resource for State Shintō (Kasulis) [C, J1 §4.4.1; also J3 §1].
- **The heart (Fujiwara's SEP translations).** "The true heart (*magokoro*) is that heart with which one is born by virtue of the Musubi (creating) *kami*, and among true hearts some are wise and some clumsy, some are good and some bad" (*Kuzubana*). And "human intelligence is puny while the acts of the *kami* are illimitable and wondrous" [C, J3 §3.2]. The *karagokoro* verse: "Though he may think himself / Rid of the Chinese heart / The heart of a man / Who reads Chinese / Is still Chinese" (adapted from Muraoka 1964) [C, J3].
- ***Tamakatsuma*.** Its definition of *karagokoro* (not liking China as such, but the habit of arguing good and bad and fixing the principles of things) I recall but did not open [U].
- **Word test.** 漢意 *karagokoro* is "Chinese heart" (SEP) or "Chinese mind"; 真心 *magokoro* is "true heart"; *kokoro* is heart and mind at once. *Kotoage* 言挙げ, "lifting up words", is my rendering.
- **On the questions.** Q1: a Way that is not a doctrine; learning is philology. Q2: to recover the heart of antiquity and follow the kami. Q3: reading the ancient texts with a purified heart, and argument itself held suspect.

## 6. Meiji (1868–1912)

- **Nishi, *Shōhaku sakki*** (manuscript dated March 1872) [C, J9]: 「故に統一の觀を立つるは哲學家の論究す可き所と爲し、學術の精微を究むるは各科の學術を專攻する者に存する也」. My rendering: "Establishing a unified view is what the philosopher must investigate; working out the fine detail of learning belongs to those who specialise in each science." He cites Comte's ordering of the sciences in the next paragraph. Q1 and Q2 together: philosophy as the unity of the hundred sciences. His notes name "Positive Philosophy" with "Comte, Whewell, Mill" as its roots [C as quoted, J10, *Zenshū* 4 p. 181].
- **Inoue Enryō (1858–1919)** founded the Tetsugakukan ("Academy of Philosophy") in 1887, the forerunner of Tōyō University. Its founding motto: "The Basis of All Learning Lies in Philosophy". The Tetsugakudō (Temple of Philosophy) honours Four Sages: "Confucius, Buddha, Socrates, and Kant" [C, J16]. The dates of the 1885 ceremony and the 1904 temple are [U].
- **Inoue Tetsujirō** (§1). He also promoted "*kokumin dōtoku*, or 'national ethics'" [C, J2 §5] and used his prestige "to lend philosophical support to the *kokutai* theory" [C, J1 §4.4.2].
- **The frame (Kasulis).** Academic *tetsugaku* "better fit the 'Western ingenuity' side of the *wakon yōsai* allocation, especially as it restricted itself to the subfields of epistemology, logic, philosophy of science, and metaphysics" [C, J1 §4.4.2].
- **Chōmin on positivism** (*Zoku ichinen yūhan*, as quoted by Fujita Masakatsu): the school "seems extremely certain and reliable", but in its attachment to the actual it erases whatever cannot be verified by experiment, "自ら狭隘にし、自ら固陋に陥いりて", making itself narrow and closed [C as quoted, J17].

## 7. The Kyoto School

**Nishida, *Zen no kenkyū*, preface dated January 1911** [C, J18; Aozora Bunko, base text Iwanami Bunko]. My renderings:
- 「第四編は余が、かねて哲学の終結と考えている宗教について余の考を述べたものである」: Part IV sets out my thoughts on religion, which I have long held to be the conclusion of philosophy.
- 「この書を特に「善の研究」と名づけた訳は、哲学的研究がその前半を占め居るにも拘らず、人生の問題が中心であり、終結であると考えた故である」: I named the book *An Inquiry into the Good* because, although philosophical inquiry fills its first half, I hold the problem of life to be the centre and the conclusion. **A direct Q2 line.**
- 「個人あって経験あるにあらず、経験あって個人あるのである」: it is not that there is experience because there is an individual; there is an individual because there is experience.
- The preface closes with Mephistopheles' mockery and Hegel's "我は哲理を考えるように罰せられている", I am condemned to think philosophical thoughts.
- The Abe–Ives wordings are [U] (lending-only).

**The "sorrow" line, not upgraded.** I have not opened the primary. The U-Tokyo paper gives it as the close of 「場所の自己限定としての意識作用」 in 『無の自覚的限定』 (1932), with the locator old *Nishida Kitarō zenshū* (Iwanami 1965–66), vol. 6, apparently p. 116. The OCR is garbled, so the page is [U]; the wording stays [C as quoted, J22].

**Tanabe Hajime (1885–1962), *Zangedō to shite no tetsugaku*.**
- **Dates.** Published by Iwanami Shoten in April 1946. The framework was laid out in lectures at Kyoto in November–December 1944 [C, J19, Takeuchi's introduction].
- **Heisig's foreword:** the book "argues for the ultimate futility of all philosophy, and proposes instead a 'philosophy that is not a philosophy'" [C, J19 pp. 5–6]. The Japanese 哲学ならぬ哲学 is [U, search summary]. Tanabe's own preface wording is an open check.
- **Davis (SEP):** a call for "an 'absolute critique' of human rationality as such": reason "is inevitably driven to antinomies through which it must repeatedly die to its own self-power in order to be reborn again through the workings of an Other-power" (*tariki*, after Shinran) [C, J20 §4.5]. The book was also "a call to self-critique on the part of the entire nation", though "one looks through that work in vain for any admission of guilt for particular actions or statements that he had made" (Heisig, quoted) [C, J20 §4.5]. Tanabe "crisscrosses the line between philosophy and religion as much as any Kyoto School thinker" [C, J20 §3.4].
- **Word test.** The title is 懺悔道 *zangedō*, "the Way of repentance", with *zange* the Buddhist word for repentance and confession; the SEP renders the title "Philosophy as the Way of Metanoetics". Tanabe himself "conceived of *zange* in terms of the Greek terms μετάνοια or μετανόησις" [C, J19], so "metanoetics" is his own gloss, which the translator kept. The 道 is in the title again.
- **On the questions.** Q1: "a philosophy that is not a philosophy". Q3: absolute critique by way of *zange*.
- **Error in a source.** The Kasulis–Bouso SEP entry gives Tanabe's dates as "1865–1962"; the Sourcebook contents page and Davis's Kyoto School entry (§1) have 1885 [C, J1 §4.4.2; J13; J20]. Do not copy the 1865.

**The school as a school.** Tosaka's point, "often repeated today": "without Tanabe's critical appropriation of Nishida's thought there would be no tradition of the Kyoto School" [C, J20 §2.2].

## 8. After the war, and the naming question

- **Kasulis.** After the war "liberal and leftist thinkers scorned the very idea of 'Japanese' philosophy as smacking of nativism, ethnocentrism, jingoism, and State Shintō ideology". Departments "escaped into Western philosophy", and most academic philosophers since "have pursued philosophy according to methods and themes hardly distinguishable from those of the West". The Kyoto School has since revived abroad [C, J1 §4.4.3]. His closing section is titled "Metaphilosophy: Philosophy as *Wissenschaft* or Way?": "When Japanese philosophy considers itself a Way rather than a *Wissenschaft*, it stresses engagement over detachment" [C, J1 §5.1].
- **Maraldo** (Prologue to *Japanese Philosophy in the Making 1*, 2017, which "elaborate[s] on my summary in jpsb, 17–21") [C, J12]:
  - "Even more controversial is the question whether pre-modern Japanese thought counts as philosophy" (p. 4).
  - **Four senses of *nihon tetsugaku*:** (1) philosophy "of a Greco-European vintage distilled by people who happen to be Japanese"; (2) classical Japanese thought before the European term, which is how Inoue used it; (3) Western methods applied to premodern thought, as in "Dōgen's philosophy of being and time"; (4) a distinctively Japanese "originality", criticised as "inverted orientalism" (pp. 6–9).
  - "Japanese philosophy need not be the same as philosophy in Japan. Philosophy in Japan not only avoids ethnocentric connotations; it is also more balanced and inclusive. It designates what philosophers in Japan do and have done regardless of the provenance of their interests and methods" (p. 9).
  - "If there is a defining characteristic to philosophy through the ages and across cultures, surely it is the habit of interrogating given definitions" (p. 9).
  - His own working definition: "the critical investigation of deeply perplexing questions: what is the best way to live, what is true and how can we best know it, and what are our obligations to one another?" (p. 3).
- **The Sourcebook** has a "Defining Philosophy" section at pp. 17ff. and closes its twentieth-century part with Fujita Masakatsu, "The Question of Japanese Philosophy" (p. 993) [C, J13, contents only]. The section's own wording is [U]. Maraldo says the omission of Western-style fields was "simply to manage the length of the book, and not to impose a culturalist criterion" [C, J12 pp. 9–10].

## 13. Discard list (do not place)

- Kasulis's *tetsujin* used as if it were the tradition's own word.
- The potter and geologist analogy, and Kasulis's "holographic" vocabulary: his interpretive apparatus, not the field's terms.
- Nishitani on nihilism and Watsuji's *ningen* and *aidagara*: these bear on ethics and religion, not on what philosophy is (Watsuji's *Ethics as the Study of Man* is a *-gaku* title, nothing more).
- Kokugaku's later lineage (Atsutane, provincial schools), Nichiren and Pure Land "selection", Muromachi aesthetics.
- Tucker's three-school critique of Inoue's schema beyond one clause; the dating of Inoue's first lecture (1882 or 1883); the Lin Mei-Mao argument that Nishi misread *philosophia*.
- Any "Sorai or Norinaga anticipated" line (e.g. Sorai's made Way "anticipating" modern political thought, Maruyama's reading). That is reception and would need its critics.
- The SEP's "1865" for Tanabe.
