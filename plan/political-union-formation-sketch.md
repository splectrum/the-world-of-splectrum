# Working Sketch: When Does an Alliance Become a Society?

*Two angles on one definition: (1) the TRANSITION Moffett's category scheme doesn't theorise — alliances that became societies; (2) the TERRITORIAL CRITERION itself, pressed by de-localised interaction and by the non-territorial societies that already existed. The second is the more fundamental.*

Working document, opened 2026-09-12. Provisional throughout. Scope, territory test and open questions all subject to revision. This is a *scoping* document: it states what the territory is, why it is worth taking up, and what would have to be researched — it does not do the research, and it does not settle which surfaces carry which part.

## 1. Where this came from

Moffett's framework (see the retained record; the person page carries the outside-voice account) defines a society by three coinciding conditions: members recognise one another through shared markers; the identity is *primary* and abiding rather than one affiliation among many; and the group claims exclusive say over a physical space. Markers do the scaling work — with badges rather than faces certifying membership, size is limited "virtually ad infinitum", with no cognitive ceiling.

From this he draws a consequence: a wide grouping that lacks primary abiding identification and exclusive territorial control is an **alliance**, a different category rather than a bigger society. Cosmopolitanism is therefore a "pipe dream" — a definitional consequence, not a mood. The European Union is his worked example: the member states hold the territory and the loyalty.

**The objection that opened this thread (Jules, 2026-09-12).** The EU is not a counterexample to the category scheme so much as a case caught mid-transition, and the historical record is full of groupings that *began* as alliances of territorial units and *became* societies:

- **The United States.** Thirteen sovereign states; an explicitly confederal first attempt (the Articles) that failed; deep and persisting inter-state differences — and "American" is now unambiguously a primary identity. Federation, not fusion, and it worked.
- **Germany (1871).** A customs union (Zollverein) and then a confederation before it was a nation-state.
- **Italy (1861).** The state preceded the identity, and contemporaries said so: "we have made Italy, now we must make Italians."

So the question the framework does not answer: **when, and how, does an alliance become a society?** If the two are different categories, what happens during the transition — and what is the thing that changes?

**The internal pressure.** Moffett already has fission-fusion: societies split and merge, markers drift. The *fusion* half is precisely alliance-becoming-society, and it is the half he does not develop — his expectation is fragmentation, "turning the familiar into the foreign." The question is therefore not foreign to his apparatus; it is the underexplored direction of it.

**Already on the record against him** (BBS 2025 commentaries, verified): Brewer & Caporael argue people recognise membership in many identity groups "on an equal par" and that his definition is shaped by the nation-state as prototype; Smaldino presses the nested/fuzzy cosmopolitan case (France inside the EU — which is the society?); Wiessner & Roscoe would drop territoriality and add that expansion requires *cultural institutions* as the mechanism. Moffett's answer to the complaint that his concept excludes other tiers: "That is my intent." **Note for any build: siding with the commentators is siding with the dissent, not reporting Moffett.**

## 2. The territory test (does the subject hold?)

Does this hold territory of its own, or is it our construction?

**It holds.** The formation and dissolution of political unions is a real field with its own principals, canonical texts and live disputes — not a frame we impose. Candidate cast, unverified and unordered:

- **State formation:** Charles Tilly (war and the state; coercion and capital), Michael Mann, Hendrik Spruyt (the sovereign state and its competitors).
- **Nation-building / nationalism:** Ernest Renan (*Qu'est-ce qu'une nation?*, 1882 — the daily plebiscite), Ernest Gellner, Eric Hobsbawm (invented tradition), Benedict Anderson (*Imagined Communities* — already external on the Moffett page, and the humanities-side twin of the markers thesis), Anthony D. Smith (the ethnosymbolist counter-position), Rogers Brubaker.
- **Federalism and union:** William Riker, Alfred Stepan, the federal-bargain literature; Alexander Hamilton/Madison as primary sources on the American case (the Articles' failure is argued in the *Federalist*).
- **Solidarity and secession:** Michael Hechter (group solidarity, internal colonialism), Donald Horowitz, the secession literature.
- **The EU as its own case:** the neofunctionalism-vs-intergovernmentalism dispute is *exactly* the alliance-or-more question in political-science vocabulary. Haas, Moravcsik. This is the field already arguing our question under its own terms — worth checking before building anything, since it may supply the vocabulary.

**Naming, undecided.** "Alliances" is too narrow and reads as international relations. Candidates: *political union formation*; *state and nation formation*; *the formation of political unions*. The territory-naming test from the phase-transitions call applies — name the territory, not the star concept.

## 2a. The second angle: the territorial criterion itself (Jules, 2026-09-12)

The historical cases press the *transition* Moffett doesn't theorise. This angle presses the *criterion* — exclusive control of a physical space — and it is the more fundamental of the two.

**The virtualisation argument.** Interaction is progressively less bound to physical proximity. Tight, high-frequency, identity-forming interaction once required living near one another; it no longer does. If territorial control is a defining condition of a society, and the interaction that constitutes membership increasingly does not run through territory, then the criterion is tracking something that was a *contingency of the interaction medium* rather than a necessary feature of societies. Moffett keeps territoriality explicitly to stop ethnicities becoming "societies within societies" — i.e. it is doing boundary-work for the definition. Ask what happens to that work when the medium changes.

**The historical counterweight — and this is the load-bearing part.** Non-territorial membership is not new, so the argument cannot be dismissed as internet-age novelty. Candidate cases to research:

- **Secret societies.** Membership certified by markers (signs, passwords, degrees, regalia), recognisable between strangers who have never met, deliberately *not* territorial, in some cases with membership obligations outranking local ones. Freemasonry is the obvious large case; the guild-and-confraternity literature, the Carbonari, the Chinese hui and triads, and the anthropological literature on initiation societies (e.g. the West African Poro and Sande) are all in scope. The recognition-between-strangers mechanism is *exactly* Moffett's anonymous-membership mechanism running with no territory at all.
- **Diasporas and stateless nations.** Identity primary and abiding, membership marked, territory absent or contested — the Jewish diaspora pre-1948, the Roma, overseas Chinese networks.
- **Religious orders and transnational churches.** Primary identification, markers, own law, and a jurisdiction that is explicitly not territorial (medieval canon law as the worked historical case).
- **The merchant and maritime networks** — Hanseatic, Armenian, Sephardic trade diasporas: recognition among strangers, enforceable obligation, dispersed space.
- **Modern non-territorial cases:** online communities with real membership costs, open-source projects, professional bodies, transnational movements.

**Why this is not merely a counterexample list.** Moffett grants that markers make membership scale without a cognitive ceiling. Territory is then the only thing in his definition that *limits* what can count. So if non-territorial groups can satisfy the other two conditions — marked mutual recognition, and primary abiding identity — the definition's exclusions rest on territory alone, and territory is the criterion most exposed to the medium of interaction changing. **Note the honest counter:** Moffett anticipates part of this. He widens territoriality beyond compulsory expulsion to admit "shades of grey", and his reply to those who want the concept to cover other tiers is "that is my intent" — the strictness is chosen, not overlooked. The question is whether the choice survives its own rationale once interaction de-localises.

**Already in the dissent:** Brooks & Samuni argued in the BBS commentaries that societal spaces need not be physical (bonobos, gorillas, academic societies, online communities), giving "a more plural and intersectional notion of societies"; Wiessner & Roscoe would drop territoriality outright and count religious, class and ethnic groups as societies. Again — that is the dissent's position, not Moffett's.

**Research needed on this angle:**
- The anthropology of non-territorial societies and initiation/secret societies: who are its principals, and does the field itself treat these as societies or as something else?
- Whether the *legal* literature on non-territorial jurisdiction (personal law, millet systems, canon law, consociational arrangements) already formalises the distinction.
- Whether anyone has posed the virtualisation question against a territorial definition of society directly — in political theory, in internet studies, or in the "network society" literature (Castells is the obvious name to check; possibly Benedict Anderson's print-capitalism argument read forward).
- Whether the interaction-medium framing has a name in the field already.


## 3. What the angle adds

The field studies the transition extensively but mostly does not pose it as a question about *when a grouping stops being an arrangement between units and becomes a unit*. Put that way it is a question about markers and their scope: when does identification at the wider scope become primary, and what makes it so? That has a clean empirical handle, because the transition has happened repeatedly, unevenly, and with well-documented failures (the Articles of Confederation; the United Arab Republic; Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia running the other way).

The second angle adds a different contribution: not when the boundary moves, but what the boundary *needs*. If territory is a contingency of the interaction medium rather than a condition of society, the same question — when does a grouping become a unit? — has to be asked without it, and the non-territorial cases (old and new) are where the answer would have to come from.

Held as questions, not theses. The temptation to answer either with "markers" before the research is done is the thing to resist.

## 4. Open questions

- Is the alliance/society distinction a genuine category difference with a transition between, or a continuum that Moffett's definition cuts at a point? His own answer is available (the criteria either coincide or they don't) — but the historical cases are the test.
- What actually changes in a successful transition: markers, institutions, the locus of force, generational turnover? The Italian case suggests institutions first and identity after; the American case suggests something else again.
- Does the *failed* transition teach more than the successful one? The Articles, the UAR, the EU's stalled constitutional moment.
- Is there an asymmetry between fusion and fission? Moffett expects fragmentation; the record has both, and secession literature is separate from federalism literature.
- Where does this land on the site — a subject page with the field's own material, a close-affinity or fence reading, a blog post, or several? **Not to be decided by drift.** The subject comes first if the territory test holds; any reading rests on it.
- Is territorial control a condition of society or a contingency of how interaction happened to work? If the latter, what does the definition rest on once it is removed — and is "primary abiding identity" enough on its own to do the excluding Moffett wants?
- Do the old non-territorial cases (secret societies, diasporas, orders) and the new ones (online, transnational) differ in kind, or only in scale and reach?
- Relation to the politics material already built (coexistence read at every scale; the one-and-the-many as the field's standing concern) — the connection is obvious enough to be worth checking rather than assuming.
