What this means for the submission:

- **The emphasis in 5.6 is not on "my".** The 1922 German letter-spaces the whole subject phrase *Die Grenzen meiner Sprache*, and both translations italicise "The limits of my language" [C, S1 scans pp. 148–149; S2]. The emphasised *meine/meiner* ("my world") belong to 5.62, as does *der* ("**the** language") [C, S1 scan p. 150; S2].
- **"mean", not "means".** Both translations have "mean", from the plural German *bedeuten* [C, S1, S2]. The submission's point about the quote card stands.
- **5.64 in Pears–McGuinness says "The self of solipsism shrinks"**, not "the subject". Ogden has "The I in solipsism"; the German is *Das Ich* [C].
- **5.641 belongs with the cluster** and is not in the submission. Ogden: "the metaphysical subject, the limit—not a part of the world". Pears–McGuinness: "the metaphysical subject, the limit of the world—not a part of it". German: "die Grenze—nicht ein Teil—der Welt" [C, S1, S2].
- **6.54 does not say that logic shows itself.** That is 4.121: "That which expresses *itself* in language, *we* cannot express by language. The propositions *show* the logical form of reality" (Ogden). German: "Was *sich* in der Sprache ausdrückt, können *wir* nicht durch sie ausdrücken. Der Satz *zeigt* die logische Form der Wirklichkeit" [C, S1].
- **Word test, 6.54.** Wittgenstein's word is *unsinnig*: Ogden has "senseless", Pears–McGuinness "nonsensical". The book keeps *unsinnig* apart from *sinnlos*: tautologies are *sinnlos*, "without sense", but "not *unsinnig*" (4.461, 4.4611) [C, S1].
- **The preface line the submission quotes is Pears–McGuinness's wording** ("find both sides of the limit thinkable") [C, S2]. Ogden has "be able to think both sides of this limit" [C, S1].

### The 5.62 parenthesis (headline)

- The rendering "the language which only I understand" appears **only in Ogden's first impression of 1922** [C, S1].
  - Russell's Introduction, printed in the same 1922 volume, gives the parenthesis as "(the only language I understand)" [C, S1, l. 997 of the LaTeX].
  - Klement's Ogden column reads "(*the* language which I understand)" [C, S2]. Klement points to Lewy's note for the changes between the 1922 and 1933 versions of the Ogden text [C, S2 landing page]. So his Ogden text is apparently the corrected 1933 one, though that is an inference.
  - Hintikka says that his copy (the third impression, 1947) renders the passage "essentially" as "the only language that I understand" [C, S6].
- **Hintikka (1958).** "The clause in the brackets is, beyond reasonable doubt, a mistranslation … The joker here is the word 'allein'." In the dictionary examples and elsewhere in the *Tractatus* (2.224, 5.631) *allein* qualifies the word it follows. Hence "a correct translation of the words in the brackets is 'the only language that I understand'". He adds that "the incorrect translation of 5.62 was the only passage of the Tractatus which would have forced one to conclude that Wittgenstein held something like solipsism in the usual sense of the word" [C, S6]. The rendering he was correcting was Urmson's 1956 quotation, "(the language, which I alone understand)" [C, S6].
- **Anscombe** (*An Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus*, 2nd ed., 1963, p. 167) [C, S7]:
  - Main text: "In the Tractatus Wittgenstein speaks of 'my language' (5.6) and explains this as meaning 'the only language that I understand'! (5.62)."
  - Footnote: "The emphasized definite article shews that 'der Sprache' in 5.62 means 'that language' making a back reference which must be to 'my language' in 5.6.—In the first edition of this book I translated the parenthesis in 5.62 'the language that only I understand'. But Dr. C. Lewy has found a copy of the first edition of the Tractatus with a correction by Wittgenstein giving 'the only language that I understand'."
- **Lewy (*Mind* 76, 1967)** is still unopened. That Wittgenstein made the correction is now [C] at second hand through Anscombe (S7); its wording in Lewy, and the claim that the copy was Ramsey's, stay [U].
- Pears–McGuinness's "that language which alone I understand" reads most naturally as "the only language that I understand", since "alone" qualifies "which". That is my reading of the English, not a sourced claim.
- **Headline for the piece.** The reading "which only I understand" rests on Ogden's first impression. Wittgenstein's own correction, Russell's introduction, the later Ogden text, Anscombe and Hintikka all read "the only language that I understand". The submission's hinge ("joined at 'the language which only I understand'") can still be used as the author's phrase, but if it is presented as Wittgenstein's sense it misreports him. Say in a clause that it is Ogden's 1922 rendering, later corrected; do not stage the scholarship.

**Schopenhauer behind the remarks.** The SEP describes the *Tractatus* as "showing Schopenhauerian and other cultural influences" and as growing out of the *Notebooks* 1914–16 [C, S9b]. How far the influence goes is disputed: Magee calls it primary, Glock limits it. Pears notes that Wittgenstein re-read Schopenhauer while writing the last notebooks [U, search summaries]. Report it as influence, with the extent disputed.

## 2. Private language argument

- **The *Investigations* text** (Anscombe, the English text of the third edition) [C, S8]:
  - **§243** opens with ordinary solitary speech: "A human being can encourage himself, give himself orders, obey, blame and punish himself; he can ask himself a question and answer it. We could even imagine human beings who spoke only in monologue … —An explorer who watched them and listened to their talk might succeed in translating their language into ours." It then asks: "But could we also imagine a language in which a person could write down or give vocal expression to his inner experiences … for his private use?—Well, can't we do so in our ordinary language?—But that is not what I mean. The individual words of this language are to refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language."
  - **§258**: "I want to keep a diary about the recurrence of a certain sensation. To this end I associate it with the sign 'S' … in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'."
  - **§293**: "Suppose everyone had a box with something in it: we call it a 'beetle'. No one can look into anyone else's box … The thing in the box has no place in the language-game at all; not even as a something: for the box might even be empty.—No, one can 'divide through' by the thing in the box".
  - **§119**: "The results of philosophy are the uncovering of one or another piece of plain nonsense and of bumps that the understanding has got by running its head up against the limits of language." The German and the Hacker–Schulte wording are [U].
- **The definition is in-principle.** The words refer to what "can only be known" to the speaker, "So another person cannot understand" [C, S8]. §243 itself first sets aside the monologue speakers, whose language an explorer could translate [C, S8].
- **The SEP's reading** (Candlish and Wrisley, revision of 2019) [C, S9]:
  - It quotes §243 in Hacker–Schulte: "The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know — to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language."
  - It continues: "This is not intended to cover (easily imaginable) cases of recording one's experiences in a personal code, for such a code, however obscure in fact, could in principle be deciphered. What Wittgenstein had in mind is a language conceived as necessarily comprehensible only to its single originator because the things which define its vocabulary are necessarily inaccessible to others."
  - It adds that the older Anscombe translation of §243 "loses the crucial contrast, so evident in the original German, between the ordinary human beings described as solitary speakers in the first paragraph and the second paragraph's mysterious 'one' who is the 'speaker' of a private language".
  - On §258 it notes that "formulated" renders *aussprechen*, "better translated as 'expressed'".
- **Crusoe** [C, S9]. The argument "is not committed to ruling out in advance all hypothetical cases of 'Robinson Crusoes'", because "there is no a priori barrier to imagining a form of life complex enough for us to be assured that a determinate ostensive definition had been accomplished by such a being".
- **Kripke and the community view** [C, S9]:
  - Kripke reads the argument as following from rule-following: "The impossibility of private language emerges as a corollary of [Wittgenstein's] sceptical solution of his own paradox" (Kripke p. 68, as the SEP quotes it).
  - The SEP judges his account "vitiated by his unargued reliance on ideas which Wittgenstein argued against", but says it "gave new life to the debate over the community view".
  - The texts "seem to point two ways": some support the reading "that language must be potentially social", others "the community view that language is essentially social". Malcolm holds the community view; Baker and Hacker oppose it.
  - Kripke's own distinction between a Crusoe "physically isolated" and one "considered in isolation" is still [U], from a search summary of Humphrey 1996.
- **Headline answer.** The mainstream reading takes the argument to rule out a language that is unshareable in principle. A personal code that is private in fact but could be deciphered falls outside the target [C, S9]. The broader community reading (Malcolm; Kripke's sceptical solution) holds that language is essentially social, and it is contested [C, S9]. The submission's reading matches the mainstream reading. The SEP's own formula, that language "must be potentially social", fits the submission's route closely.
- **Site backing.** The Wittgenstein page keys the argument to §202. The SEP agrees that the rule-following sections are logically prior, but places the private language sections at §243 ff. [C, S9].

The language in the world:

- **Merleau-Ponty, speaking and spoken speech.** *Phenomenology of Perception*, "The Body as Expression, and Speech" (tr. Smith, p. 196) [C, S10]:
  - "one might draw a distinction between the word in the speaking and the spoken word. The former is the one in which the significant intention is at the stage of coming into being."
  - "Hence the spoken word, which enjoys available significances as one might enjoy an acquired fortune. From these gains other acts of authentic expression — the writer's, artist's or philosopher's — are made possible. This ever-recreated opening in the plenitude of being is what conditions the child's first use of speech and the language of the writer … like a wave, gathers and poises itself to hurtle beyond its own limits."
  - The submission's gloss (made new by one speaker, inherited by the next) is a fair headline.
  - Word test: Smith's "word in the speaking / spoken word" renders *parole parlante / parole parlée*, which Landes translates as "speaking speech / spoken speech". The French wording and the Gallimard page (around p. 229) are [U]. The *Signs* essays are [U].
- **Saussure.** CLG, text as established by Bally and Sechehaye with Riedlinger; from fr.wikisource, Payot edition [C, S14]:
  - Introduction, ch. IV, "Linguistique de la langue et linguistique de la parole": "historiquement, le fait de parole précède toujours … Enfin, c'est la parole qui fait évoluer la langue : ce sont les impressions reçues en entendant les autres qui modifient nos habitudes linguistiques. Il y a donc interdépendance de la langue et de la parole ; celle-là est à la fois l'instrument et le produit de celle-ci. Mais tout cela ne les empêche pas d'être deux choses absolument distinctes."
  - Part III, ch. V, "Analogie et évolution": "Rien n'entre dans la langue sans avoir été essayé dans la parole, et tous les phénomènes évolutifs ont leur racine dans la sphère de l'individu." - **Humboldt.** Introduction to the Kawi work, *Über die Verschiedenheit des menschlichen Sprachbaues* (Berlin: Akademie, 1836), §8, "Form der Sprachen" [C, S15, OCR of the scan]:
