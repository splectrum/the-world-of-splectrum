# The subject and its language in the world: research note

Purpose: the research round for `submissions/the-subject-and-language-in-the-world.md`. It checks the *Tractatus* remarks at source, the private language argument's scope, and the field cast, and lists what the person pages already back. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked against a source I opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a secondary paraphrase I could not open).

Network: only github.com was reachable. gutenberg.org, people.umass.edu, plato.stanford.edu and gwern.net were refused by WebFetch; Wikipedia, Wikisource, archive.org and the Wittgenstein text sites were refused by curl. Findings for the submission:

- **The emphasis in 5.6 is not on "my".** The 1922 German letter-spaces the whole subject phrase *Die Grenzen meiner Sprache*. Both English translations italicise "The limits of my language" [C, S1 scan p. 148/149; S2]. The emphasised *meine/meiner* ("my world") is in **5.62**, and so is *der* ("**the** language") [C, S1 scan p. 150; S2]. The submission's line "with 'my' emphasised" belongs to 5.62, not 5.6.
- **"mean", not "means":** both translations have "mean" (German *bedeuten*, plural) [C, S1, S2]. The submission's point about the quote card stands.
- **"the language which only I understand" is Ogden's wording**, not Pears–McGuinness's. Pears–McGuinness have "of that language which alone I understand" [C, S1, S2]. The submission gives P–M's wording at 5.62 and then uses Ogden's for the second strand, without saying so.
- 5.64 in Pears–McGuinness: "The self of solipsism shrinks to a point", not "the subject". Ogden has "The I in solipsism". The German is *Das Ich* [C].
- 5.641 belongs with the cluster and is not in the submission: "the metaphysical subject, the limit—not a part of the world" (Ogden); "the metaphysical subject, the limit of the world—not a part of it" (P–M); German "die Grenze—nicht ein Teil—der Welt" [C, S1, S2].
- **6.54 does not say that logic shows itself.** That is 4.121: "That which expresses *itself* in language, *we* cannot express by language. The propositions *show* the logical form of reality" (Ogden; German "Was *sich* in der Sprache ausdrückt, können *wir* nicht durch sie ausdrücken. Der Satz *zeigt* die logische Form der Wirklichkeit") [C, S1]. The submission folds the two together.
- Word test, 6.54: Wittgenstein's word is *unsinnig*. Ogden renders it "senseless", P–M "nonsensical". The book keeps *unsinnig* apart from *sinnlos* (tautologies are *sinnlos*, "without sense", but "not *unsinnig*", 4.461/4.4611) [C, S1]. "Nonsense" (P–M) is the closer fit.
- Small textual note, headline-neutral: the 1922 printing reads "methaphysisches" (a misprint) and letter-spaces "Wo in" at 5.633. S2's German has "metaphysisches" and spaces only "in" [C, S1 scan p. 150; S2].

**5.62 ambiguity (headline only).** The German parenthesis *der Sprache, die allein ich verstehe* can be read as "the language that only I understand" (Ogden) or "the only language that I understand". It is recognised as a crux and it changes the headline: Hintikka argues that the mistranslation was the only passage that would force a solipsist reading of the book [U, search summary of Hintikka, "On Wittgenstein's 'Solipsism'", *Mind* 67 (1958), pp. 88 ff.]. C. Lewy reported a copy of the first edition (F. P. Ramsey's) with corrections in Wittgenstein's hand, presumably from 1923, and called the 5.62 correction "probably the most important". Anscombe reports that the correction gives "the only language that I understand" [U, search summaries of Lewy, "A Note on the Text of the Tractatus", *Mind* 76 (1967), pp. 416–420, and of Anscombe's footnote. The summary placed the footnote in the *Investigations*; it is almost certainly in her *An Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus* (1959). To check]. For the piece this matters more than a detail, because the submission's hinge ("joined at 'the language which only I understand'") rests on the rendering that Wittgenstein himself reportedly corrected. It can still be used, named as Ogden's wording with the correction noted in a clause.

**Schopenhauer behind the remarks** [U, search summaries]. The influence on the young Wittgenstein is standard: he re-read *The World as Will and Representation* while writing the 1916 notebooks, where the solipsism and will remarks first appear (Pears). How far it goes is disputed: Magee treats Schopenhauer as Wittgenstein's first and greatest influence, Glock limits it. Report it as influence, with the extent disputed. Do not present Schopenhauer as "the source".

## 2. Private language argument

- **§243 definition** [U, SEP via search summary]. Hacker–Schulte (4th ed.): "The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know — to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language." Anscombe's older rendering, from memory: "The individual words of this language are to refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language" [U]. In both, the target is a language another person *cannot* understand, because its words refer to what only the speaker *can* know. That makes it unshareable in principle. §243 also sets aside, before the definition, people who speak only in monologue (an explorer could still translate them) and the use of ordinary language for one's own inner experiences [U, memory].
- **Locations** [U]: §258 is the diary of the sensation "S" (the usual anchor of the argument); §270 is the manometer variant; §293 is the beetle in the box. Wittgenstein never uses the phrase "private language argument" [U, SEP via search summary]. The site's Wittgenstein page attributes the argument to §202, which is the rule-following route (see below).
- **Mainstream headline** [U, SEP and Byrne via search summaries]. The SEP entry states the conclusion as the impossibility of "a language in principle unintelligible to anyone but its originating user". It adds that the §243 scenario does not cover a personal code for recording one's experiences, since any such code "could in principle be deciphered". Byrne (*Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2nd ed., 2005) agrees that the target is a language "in principle" understood by one person only. He notes that §243 seems to leave room for a Crusoe, while §198 points the other way. **So the submission's reading (the argument rules out the in-principle case and leaves a private-in-fact but shareable language open) matches the mainstream reading of §243.**
- **The broader reading** [U, search summaries]. Kripke (*Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, 1982) puts the real argument in the rule-following sections before §243. On that view an individual "considered in isolation" cannot mean anything. The "community view" draws the strong conclusion that meaning needs a community. Kripke separates a Crusoe "physically isolated", who can be taken into our community, from one "considered in isolation" (Humphrey 1996). Main critics: Baker and Hacker (1984). Ayer (1954) and Putnam defended the possibility of a Crusoe language. The submission's [C] that "some readings take §243 more broadly" holds. Strictly, Kripke's reading is about rules, not §243, and it does not make a contingently isolated speaker meaningless.
- **PI §119** [U]. Anscombe: "The results of philosophy are the uncovering of one or another piece of plain nonsense and of bumps that the understanding has got by running its head up against the limits of language." Hacker–Schulte reportedly has "running up against", without "its head". The German, from memory, is "Beulen, die sich der Verstand beim Anrennen an die Grenze der Sprache geholt hat". "Running its head up against" is therefore Anscombe's wording, and German *Grenze* is singular.

## 3. Field cast

Existing person pages (grep of `docs/positioning/persons/`): wittgenstein, schopenhauer, heidegger, merleau-ponty, von-uexkull, von-foerster, maturana, rovelli, quine, saussure, polanyi (Michael, not Karl; the page is Michael's), peirce, vygotsky. **No pages:** Neurath, Humboldt, Davidson, Kripke, Anscombe, Hintikka. Same-surname watch: Humboldt (Wilhelm, linguist, against Alexander, naturalist; both are named on the site). If a page is made, the site's convention suggests `h/humboldt-w`.

The subject in the world:
- **Schopenhauer.** *WWR* §1 opens: "'The world is my idea:'—this is a truth which holds good for everything that lives and knows, though man alone can bring it into reflective and abstract consciousness" (Haldane–Kemp 1883) [C, S3]. German "Die Welt ist meine Vorstellung" and Payne's "The world is my representation" [U]. Word test: *Vorstellung* is rendered "idea" (Haldane–Kemp) or "representation" (Payne, and the site's page). Influence on Wittgenstein: see §1.
- **Heidegger.** Being-in-the-world (*In-der-Welt-sein*), *Being and Time* (1927). The page already carries it.
- **Merleau-Ponty.** The body as the subject's way of being in the world. The page has "The body is our general medium for having a world". "Of the world" as a quotation is unlocated [U]; *The Visible and the Invisible* (the seer is part of the visible; flesh) is the likeliest home. The phrase *être au monde* is rendered "being-in-the-world" (Smith) and reportedly "being in and toward the world" (Landes 2012) [U]. A strong candidate line from the *Phenomenology* preface, from memory: "there is no inner man, man is in the world, and only in the world does he know himself" (Smith tr.) [U].
- **von Uexküll.** *Umwelt*. The page already carries it.
- **von Foerster.** Second-order cybernetics, the observer in the system. The page already carries it. It dates "Cybernetics of Cybernetics" to 1974; one summary gives 1979 for the paper quoting Maturana [U, minor].
- **Maturana.** The wording in "Biology of Cognition" (BCL Report 9.0, Urbana 1970; reprinted in *Autopoiesis and Cognition*, 1980) is reportedly "(1) Anything said is said by an observer." [U, search summary]. Von Foerster called it "Maturana's Theorem Number One" [U]. "Everything said…" is a common variant (e.g. cited to Maturana & Varela 1980, p. xix). The site page already uses "Anything said …". **The submission's "Everything said" should follow the page.**
- **Neurath's boat.** "Protokollsätze", *Erkenntnis* 3 (1932/33), pp. 204–214. German as commonly quoted: "Wie Schiffer sind wir, die ihr Schiff auf offener See umbauen müssen, ohne es jemals in einem Dock zerlegen und aus besten Bestandteilen neu errichten zu können." English (Ayer, *Logical Positivism*, 1959): "We are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea, never able to dismantle it in dry-dock and to reconstruct it there out of the best materials" [U for wording and page; the volume and pages are via search summary]. Earlier uses: 1913 and *Anti-Spengler* (1921) [U]. The image became famous through Quine's epigraph to *Word and Object* (1960) [U].
- **Quine.** Naturalised epistemology (1969) is on the page. Neurath's boat and "no first philosophy" are not on it. The boat is the epigraph of *Word and Object* and recurs in its §1 [U]. The phrase "no first philosophy" needs locating; candidates are "Five Milestones of Empiricism" (1975) or "Things and Their Place in Theories" (1981) [U].
- **Rovelli.** Already backed: "limited parts of nature, and we are so even as understanders of this same nature" is on the page [C, page grep].

The language in the world:
- **Merleau-Ponty, *parole parlante / parole parlée*.** *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), Part I, ch. 6, "Le corps comme expression et la parole", around p. 229 of the Gallimard edition. *Parole parlante* is speech in which the significative intention is "à l'état naissant"; *parole parlée* takes up meanings already acquired; the two are likened to a wave gathering and throwing itself beyond itself [U, secondary summaries]. Also in *Signs* (1960), "On the Phenomenology of Language" and "Indirect Language and the Voices of Silence" [U]. Translation: Smith has "speaking word / spoken word", Landes "speaking speech / spoken speech" [U]. The submission's gloss (made new by one speaker, inherited by the next) is a fair headline, if attributed.
- **Saussure.** CLG (1916), compiled by Bally and Sechehaye from students' notes, so the wording is theirs [U]. Introduction, ch. 4: "c'est la parole qui fait évoluer la langue : ce sont les impressions reçues en entendant les autres qui modifient nos habitudes linguistiques", hence the interdependence of *langue* and *parole* [U, memory; only a course handout found]. Also "rien n'entre dans la langue sans avoir été essayé dans la parole" [U, memory].
- **Humboldt.** Introduction to the Kawi work, *Über die Verschiedenheit des menschlichen Sprachbaues* (1836), §8: "Sie selbst ist kein Werk (Ergon), sondern eine Thätigkeit (Energeia)", followed by the definition "die sich ewig wiederholende Arbeit des Geistes, den articulirten Laut zum Ausdruck des Gedanken fähig zu machen" [U, memory]. Brinton (1885) gives the definition as "Language is the ever-recurring effort of the mind to make the articulate sound capable of expressing thought" and paraphrases: "language is not an εργον, but an ενεργεια" [C, S5]. Word test: the Greek pair is Humboldt's own, in parentheses after the German words [U for the German; Brinton's "As he expresses it" supports it, C, S5].
- **Polanyi.** *The Tacit Dimension* (1966), p. 4: "we can know more than we can tell" [U for page; the page already carries the line].
- **Peirce.** "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (*Popular Science Monthly*, January 1878): "The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real" [C, S4]. The same paragraph: "a fore-ordained goal … like the operation of destiny" [C, S4]. The submission's "community of inquiry's convergence" is a fair headline. The word "community" is from the 1868 papers ("Some Consequences of Four Incapacities") [U].
- **Davidson.** Triangulation, introduced in the last paragraph of "Rational Animals" (1982), developed in "Three Varieties of Knowledge" (1991) and later. Objectivity depends on two or more creatures responding together to a shared world and to each other; Davidson uses it to argue that thought requires language. Standard study: Verheggen, *Donald Davidson's Triangulation Argument* (2016) [U, search summaries].
- **Vygotsky.** *Thinking and Speech* (1934), ch. 7. Speech is social first; egocentric speech "goes underground" as inner speech, abbreviated and predicative [U, summaries]. The page already carries this.
