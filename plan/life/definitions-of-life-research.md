# Definitions of life: research note

Purpose: a gathered, sourced collection of definitions of life, as material for a blog post and as backing for a planned neutral "life" keyword doorway. This is a working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.

Marking: **[C]** = checked in a source actually opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C, as quoted by Sn]** = the wording is second-hand: the source opened quotes or reports the author; the author's own text was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a source that could not be opened). Glosses of non-English passages are mine. Quotes are as found; old-print OCR has been normalised only where the reading is unambiguous.

Each entry gives: definition and source; who and which discipline; what it takes life to be; best-known objection or counter-case; word check (does the author use "life" or "living" for it?).

Tally: **44 entries**. Author's own words opened: **24**. Second-hand but checked as quoted: **15**. Unverified: **5** (Bernal, Pirie, Dawkins, Lovelock, Machery). Sources to fetch are under *Blocked sources*.

Access note: Science, Springer, Britannica, Taylor & Francis and Royal Society full texts were not reachable. Book chapters still in copyright (Monod, Rosen, Mayr, Dawkins, Lovelock, Margulis) were not opened. Hosted copies of walled papers (course sites, proxies, mirrors) were deliberately not used, and an attempt to fetch several of them was refused by the permission system; they are listed under *Blocked sources* instead. One exception to flag: the Schrödinger wording was read in a user-uploaded Internet Archive copy (S18), so it should be checked against a printed edition before a page quotes it. A similar upload of Oparin's 1952 edition was downloaded but not used; Oparin here rests on the library scan S20.

## 1. Older

### 1. Aristotle, *De Anima* II.1, 412a13-15 and II.2, 413a22-25 (4th c. BCE)

- **Definition.** "ζωὴν δὲ λέγομεν τὴν δι' αὑτοῦ τροφήν τε καὶ αὔξησιν καὶ φθίσιν" [C, S8a; the scan prints αὗτοῦ]. Smith's translation: "by life we mean self-nutrition and growth (with its correlative decay)" [C, S8b]. And at 413a: "πλεοναχῶς δὲ τοῦ ζῆν λεγομένου" [C, S8a]; Smith: "this word has more than one sense, and provided any one alone of these is found in a thing we say that thing is living. Living, that is, may mean thinking or perception or local movement and rest, or movement in the sense of nutrition, decay and growth" [C, S8b].
- **Who.** Philosopher and naturalist.
- **Takes life to be.** Nutrition, growth and decay "through itself"; a word with several senses, any one of which suffices. Plants count on the last sense. Mariscal: living things have form, material and goal-directedness, and life is self-motion, perpetuation or self-alteration [C, S1, citing Byers 2006].
- **Objection.** Bernard paraphrases Aristotle's definition and answers that the principle named is the thing to be grasped: "Or, c'est ce principe qu'il faudrait saisir et connaître" (gloss: yet that principle is what would need grasping and knowing) [C, S12, p. 25].
- **Word check.** Passes. ζωή and ζῆν are his words; he says he is saying what "we" mean by the word, and that the word is said in many ways.

### 2. Kant, *Metaphysische Anfangsgründe* (1786) and *Kritik der praktischen Vernunft* (1788); organised beings in the *Kritik der Urteilskraft* (1790) §§64-65

- **Definition of life.** "Leben heißt das Vermögen einer Substanz, sich aus einem inneren Princip zum Handeln, einer endlichen Substanz, sich zur Veränderung, und einer materiellen Substanz, sich zur Bewegung oder Ruhe als Veränderung ihres Zustandes zu bestimmen" (AA 4:544; gloss: life is a substance's capacity to determine itself to act from an inner principle; a finite substance, to change; a material substance, to motion or rest as a change of its state) [C, S9a]. "Leben ist das Vermögen eines Wesens, nach Gesetzen des Begehrungsvermögens zu handeln" (AA 5:9n; gloss: life is a being's capacity to act according to laws of the faculty of desire) [C, S9b].
- **Organised beings, a separate term.** A product of nature counts "als organisirtes und sich selbst organisirendes Wesen" as a "Naturzweck" (AA 5:374); "Ein organisirtes Wesen ist also nicht bloß Maschine... sondern es besitzt in sich bildende Kraft" (5:374, gloss: not merely a machine; it possesses formative power in itself) [C, S9c].
- **Who.** Philosopher.
- **Takes life to be.** Self-determination from an inner principle, tied to desire. Therefore "alle Materie als solche leblos" (4:544; gloss: all matter as such is lifeless) [C, S9a].
- **Objection.** Bernard: "Kant a défini la vie 'un principe intérieur d'action'", and "Le principe d'action des corps vivants n'est pas intérieur" (gloss: the principle of action of living bodies is not internal), since it cannot be separated from external conditions [C, S12, pp. 25-26].
- **Word check.** Partly fails as the entry was framed. Kant's definition of *Leben* and his account of *organisirtes Wesen* are different things. He tests "Analogon des Lebens" for organised nature and then raises a difficulty: it either gives matter a property contrary to its essence (Hylozoism) or adds a soul (5:374-75) [C, S9c]. His self-organising being should not be reported as a definition of life.

### 3. Bichat, *Recherches physiologiques sur la vie et la mort* (1800)

- **Definition.** "On cherche dans des considérations abstraites la définition de la vie; on la trouvera, je crois, dans cet aperçu général: la vie est l'ensemble des fonctions qui résistent à la mort" (Part I, Art. 1; gloss: life is the sum of the functions that resist death) [C, S10, 1805 printing]. Next sentence: all that surrounds living bodies "tend à les détruire" (tends to destroy them) [C, S10].
- **Who.** Anatomist and physiologist.
- **Takes life to be.** A set of functions in opposition to surrounding destruction.
- **Objection.** Bernard: two terms that cannot be separated, and an error in setting vital against physical properties: "La science, il faut le dire, a condamné cette définition" (gloss: science, it must be said, has condemned this definition), because physico-chemical intensity and vital activity rise together; and the first idea is already in the *Encyclopédie*'s "La vie est le contraire de la mort" [C, S12, pp. 28-30].
- **Word check.** Passes. "la vie".

### 4. Lamarck, *Philosophie zoologique* (1809)

- **Definition.** "La vie, dans les parties d'un corps qui la possède, est un ordre et un état de choses qui y permettent les mouvemens organiques; et ces mouvemens, qui constituent la vie active, résultent de l'action d'une cause stimulante qui les excite" (chapter "De la Vie", p. 403; gloss: life in the parts of a body that possesses it is an order and state of things that permit organic movements there; these movements, which constitute active life, result from a stimulating cause) [C, S11]. He adds that it seems to him impossible "d'y ajouter ou retrancher un seul mot" (to add or cut a single word) [C, S11].
- **Who.** Naturalist.
- **Takes life to be.** An order and state of things in a body, plus an exciting cause; it covers suspended as well as active life. He corrects Richerand to "la vie est un phénomène" that gives rise to other phenomena [C, S11].
- **Objection.** Bernard reports it as "la vie est un état de choses qui permet le mouvement organique sous l'influence des excitants" and comments: "Cet état de choses, c'est évidemment l'organisation, avec la condition de la sensibilité" (gloss: this state of things is evidently organisation, with the condition of sensibility) [C, S12, Première leçon]. His general charge against the definitions is "c'est définir la vie par la vie" (p. 30) [C, S12].
- **Word check.** Passes. "Cette définition de la vie, soit active, soit suspendue" is his phrase [C, S11].

### 5. Claude Bernard, *Leçons sur les phénomènes de la vie communs aux animaux et aux végétaux*, vol. 1 (1878), Première leçon

- **Refusal.** The lesson begins with definitions "dans le but bien différent de prouver que la tentative est chimérique, étrangère et inutile à la science" (to prove the attempt chimerical, foreign and useless to science) (p. 22). "C'est pourquoi il n'y a pas à définir la vie en physiologie" (there is no call to define life in physiology) (p. 24). Lesson summary: "On peut caractériser la vie, mais non la définir" [C, S12].
- **But.** "Je rappellerai à ce sujet la formule que j'ai exprimée dès longtemps: 'La vie, c'est la création'" (p. 40), and "sous une forme paradoxale, j'ai dit ailleurs: la vie c'est la mort" (p. 41) [C, S12]. He sends the first to his *Introduction à l'étude de la médecine expérimentale* p. 161 by footnote [C, S12; the *Introduction* not opened].
- **Who.** Physiologist.
- **Takes life to be.** Not definable; characterised by organic creation (synthesis, "ce qu'il y a de véritablement vital") and organic destruction, the second being what we see when we think we see life [C, S12, pp. 40-41].
- **Objection.** None opened.
- **Word check.** Holds with a correction. He does decline to define, explicitly and at length, and he then gives two one-line formulas that he calls formulas. "Declined to define" and "la vie, c'est la création" are both his.

### 6. Herbert Spencer, *The Principles of Biology*, vol. 1 (1864)

- **Definition.** "Life is—the definite combination of heterogeneous changes, both simultaneous and successive" (§25, p. 70), then "the broadest and most complete definition of Life will be—The continuous adjustment of internal relations to external relations" (§30, ch. V) [C, S13]. Bernard cites an earlier form from the *Principles of Psychology*: "la combinaison définie de changements hétérogènes" [C, S12, p. 27].
- **Who.** Philosopher, general evolutionist.
- **Takes life to be.** A correspondence between inner relations and outer relations.
- **Objection.** Bernard quoted the form without the correspondence clause, Spencer's translator Cazelles protested, and Bernard replied that a definition built up by successive degrees "est bien capable d'égarer le lecteur" (is quite capable of misleading the reader) [C, S12, pp. 27-28]. Spencer's own test case: a volcano changes heterogeneously, a watch definitely, and we "much less tend to think of [the first] as living" [C, S13, §25].
- **Word check.** Passes. "definition of Life" is his phrase.

### 7. Engels, *Anti-Dühring* (1877-78) and notes for *Dialectics of Nature*

- **Definition.** *Anti-Dühring* I.VIII in the Burns translation: "Life is the mode of existence of albuminous bodies, and this mode of existence essentially consists in the constant self-renewal of the chemical constituents of these bodies" [C, S14]. *Dialectics of Nature* notes, in another translation: "Life is the mode of existence of protein bodies, the essential element of which consists in continual metabolic interchange with the natural environment outside them, and which ceases with the cessation of this metabolism, bringing about the decomposition of the protein" [C, S15].
- **Who.** Philosopher, social theorist.
- **Takes life to be.** A chemical substance's way of existing: "every moment it is itself and at the same time something else" [C, S14].
- **Objection.** His own: "Our definition of life is naturally very inadequate... From a scientific standpoint all definitions are of little value" [C, S14]. Forterre revives it: "I like the definition of life proposed in the 19th century by Frederich Engels", ending at "life is the mode of existence of living organisms" [C, S39].
- **Word check.** Passes for "life"; caution on the substance word. "Albuminous", "protein" and Forterre's "albuminoïd" are translators' choices for one term, reported from memory as *Eiweißkörper* [U; German text not reachable]. "Protein" in Engels is not the modern concept (he says little is known of its composition) [C, S14].

### 8. Haeckel, *The Wonders of Life* (1904; Eng. trans. McCabe 1905), ch. II, and *Kristallseelen* (1917)

- **Definition.** Organisms show periodic, apparently spontaneous movements. "Hence life may be conceived as a special process of movement. Recent study has shown that this is always connected with a particular chemical substance, _plasm_, and consists essentially in a circulation of matter, or _metabolism_" [C, S16].
- **Who.** Zoologist, monist.
- **Takes life to be.** Metabolic movement of plasm, continuous with the inorganic: "the sharp distinction formerly drawn between the organic and the inorganic cannot be sustained" [C, S16]. The chapter compares life to a flame and gives the crystal section "Life of crystals" [C, S16].
- **Stretch.** *Kristallseelen: Studien über das anorganische Leben* (1917): "Alle Kristalle, ebenso die festen Sterrokristalle wie die flüssigen Rheokristalle, sind lebendig, so lange sie wachsen und sich entwickeln" (gloss: all crystals, solid and liquid, are living as long as they grow and develop) [C, S17, end of ch. I]. Meant literally; see §7 below.
- **Objection.** No named objector found in sources opened.
- **Word check.** Passes. "lebendig", "Leben der Kristalle", "anorganisches Leben" are his.

## 2. Twentieth century

### 9. Schrödinger, *What is Life?* (1944), §§56-57

- **Passage.** "What is the characteristic feature of life? When is a piece of matter said to be alive? When it goes on 'doing something', moving, exchanging material with its environment, and so forth, and that for a much longer period than we would expect an inanimate piece of matter to 'keep going' under similar circumstances." "What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy." [C, S18]
- **Who.** Physicist.
- **Takes life to be.** Characteristically, matter that keeps going and evades decay to equilibrium by drawing order from its surroundings.
- **Objection.** His own note to ch. VI: the remarks on negative entropy "have met with doubt and opposition from physicist colleagues", he would have said free energy for physicists alone, and F. Simon pointed out that the thermodynamic argument cannot explain why we need well-ordered food [C, S18].
- **Word check.** Passes for "life" and "alive", but it is a characterisation asked in the form of a question, not a definition. The subtitle is "the living cell" and the entropy sentence is about "an organism".

### 10. Oparin, *The Origin of Life on the Earth* (3rd ed., Eng. trans. 1957)

- **Passage.** "...the metabolism, that form of the motion of matter which is characteristic of life" [C, S20]. On the everyday division of the world: this assumption "itself contains the most primitive as well as the most general definition of life" [C, S20, ch. VIII].
- **Who.** Biochemist.
- **Takes life to be.** A form of the motion of matter, namely metabolism; a material phenomenon against "the idealists" [C, S20].
- **Objection.** Horowitz's genetic line runs against it: self-duplication with mutation is the unique attribute (entry 13) [C, S21]. Not framed there as a reply to Oparin.
- **Word check.** Passes for "life", with no formal definition found in the pages searched. A compilation's "Any system capable of replication and mutation is alive" (Oparin 1961) [C as listed, S3] was not traced and sits oddly with this; see Discard list.

### 11. J. B. S. Haldane, "What is Life?" (1949), pp. 58-62

- **Passage.** Opens: "I am not going to answer this question." Then: "We may say that life is essentially a pattern of chemical happenings", and "This pattern has special properties. It begets a similar pattern, as a flame does, but it regulates itself as a flame does not except to a slight extent" [C, S19].
- **Who.** Geneticist, biochemist.
- **Takes life to be.** A pattern of chemical processes, with shape, motion, feeling and purpose added in some cases.
- **Objection.** His own: "to suppose that one can describe life fully on these lines is to attempt to reduce it to mechanism, which I believe to be impossible" [C, S19].
- **Word check.** Passes. He also says viruses are "far from clear" [C, S19]. Mix's label "Haldane life" (S5) was seen only by name.

### 12. Bernal, *The Origin of Life* (1967)

- **Definition.** "Life is a partial, continuous, progressive, multiform and conditionally interactive, self-realization of the potentialities of atomic electron states" [U; quotation sites give p. 251]. A compilation has "of the atomic electron state", singular [C as listed, S3]. Page and wording need the book.
- **Who.** Crystallographer.
- **Takes life to be.** Self-realisation of atomic electron states.
- **Objection.** None opened.
- **Word check.** Passes on the quoted wording, if the wording holds.

### 13. Horowitz, *The Biological Significance of the Search for Extraterrestrial Life*, JPL Technical Report 32-1000 (1966)

- **Definition.** "The most concise, unambiguous, and general definition of life that can be given at the present time is based on the genetic properties of living things... the unique attribute of living matter from which all of its other remarkable features derive is its capacity for self-duplication with mutation. That is to say, living organisms are systems that reproduce, mutate, and then reproduce their mutations." [C, S21] His 1959 form, as listed: "The three properties mutability, self-duplication and heterocatalysis comprise a necessary and sufficient definition of living matter" [C as listed, S3].
- **Who.** Geneticist, NASA and JPL life-detection work.
- **Takes life to be.** A genetic property. He compares it to a physicist's definition of an element [C, S21].
- **Objection.** He raises the elephant: it "takes considerable enterprise to observe the reproduction of elephants" yet we do not doubt it is alive; his answer is that its cells can be tested [C, S21]. He also uses crystals and flames as the cases the definition excludes, since "Reproduction by itself is not sufficient" [C, S21].
- **Word check.** Passes. "definition of life" is his phrase.

### 14. Monod, *Le hasard et la nécessité* (1970; Eng. 1971), ch. I

- **Definition.** Three properties of living beings: teleonomy, autonomous morphogenesis, reproductive invariance [C, as reported by S22; French text not opened]. Teleonomy glossed as "endowed with a purpose or project" (Eng. p. 9) [C, as quoted by S22].
- **Who.** Molecular biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** Objects with a project, self-built, and reproducing their own structure invariantly.
- **Objection.** His own exception to autonomous morphogenesis, the crystal: the forces are "of the same nature as the microscopic interactions responsible for crystalline morphologies" (p. 11) [C, as quoted by S22].
- **Word check.** Fails as a definition of "life" as such: S22 reports characteristics of "living beings". Primary wording open.

### 15. Pirie, "The meaninglessness of the terms life and living" (1937)

- **Claim.** In Needham and Green (eds), *Perspectives in Biochemistry*, pp. 11-22 [bibliographic data C via repository listing, S46; content U]. The argument, as reported in a search summary: the terms are beyond rigorous definition. A later critic is reported as saying that Pirie goes on to use living and non-living as meaningful, attributed to W. S. Beck 1957 [U].
- **Who.** Biochemist, virus researcher.
- **Word check.** He uses both words, in order to deny them meaning; it is a claim about the words. No exact quote is available. Do not state his wording until the chapter is fetched.

### 16. Carl Sagan, "Life", *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (1970)

- **Sorting.** Five definitions: physiological, metabolic, biochemical, genetic, thermodynamic, each with counter-examples [C, as summarised by S37]. Physiological, verbatim: "For many years a physiological definition of life was popular. Life was defined as any system capable of performing a number of such functions as eating, metabolizing, excreting, breathing, moving, growing, reproducing, and being responsive to external stimuli" (p. 303 of the Bedau and Cleland reprint) [C, as quoted by S36]. Genetic: "Life is a system capable of evolution by natural selection" [C, as quoted by S38; check wording].
- **Who.** Astronomer, exobiologist.
- **Takes life to be.** He sets out what each specialty takes it to be. Worried that biochemical definitions favour our own chemistry: "Earth Chauvinism" [C, as reported by S1].
- **Counter-cases.** A car under physiological and metabolic; spores and seeds under metabolic; books under thermodynamic; mules under the reproductive part [C, as summarised by S37, unnamed page author; check that each is Sagan's and not the page's]. Benner reports the NASA group's definition followed "a suggestion by Carl Sagan" [C, S34].
- **Word check.** Passes. He reports definitions of life rather than stating one. S36 gives the 1970 pagination as 985-1002.

### 17. Mayr, *The Growth of Biological Thought* (1982)

- **Passage.** Bedau reports Mayr's list of eight hallmarks, including "All levels of living systems have an enormously complex and adaptive organization" and "All organisms possess historically evolved genetic programs which enable them to engage in teleonomic processes and activities"; and quotes p. 59: their coexistence "make[s] it clear that a living system is something quite different from any inanimate object" [C, as quoted by S24].
- **Who.** Evolutionary biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** Not a thing with a definition; a set of hallmarks of living systems.
- **Objection.** Bedau: "each of the hallmarks can be possessed by non-living things" [C, S24].
- **Word check.** The check turns on the noun. Reported from memory, Mayr calls "life" a reification of the process of living [U; only a forum paraphrase found]. What is confirmed is that his list is of "living systems" and "organisms".

### 18. Rosen, *Life Itself* (1991)

- **Definition.** Rosen's summary "organisms are closed to efficient causation" [C, as quoted by S47]. Fuller form, "a material system is an organism if, and only if, it is closed to efficient causation", p. 244 [U, search summary].
- **Who.** Theoretical biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** A closed loop of efficient causes, as against machines.
- **Objection.** Siekmann: it "should be considered as a hypothesis to be tested", not "a specific property that can be deduced from the architecture of (M, R) systems" [C, S47]. Mossio, Longo and Stewart 2009 on computability [U, blocked].
- **Word check.** Fails. The defined word is **organism**. Siekmann calls this "Rosen's definition of life" and "Rosen's proposed characterisation of life" [C, S47]; those labels are the reader's. The book's title and question use "life" [U for his own text].

### 19. Gánti, *The Principles of Life* (2003)

- **Definition.** Five absolute life criteria: a living organism is (1) an inherent unity with new qualitative properties; (2) metabolism; (3) inherent stability; (4) an information-carrying subsystem; (5) regulation and control. Three potential criteria: growth and reproduction, hereditary change and evolution, mortality. "Non-living systems cannot die" (p. 79) [C, as reported and quoted by S25]. Chemoton: a model of metabolic, membrane and information subsystems, described as "the principle of life" [C, S25].
- **Who.** Theoretical biologist; earlier Hungarian publication from 1971 [U].
- **Takes life to be.** Absolute criteria apply to every organism; potential ones are needed for "survival of the living world" [C, as quoted by S25].
- **Objection.** None opened. His split is reported as a way of handling sterile organisms [C, S25].
- **Word check.** Mixed. "Life criteria" and "living organism" pass. The chemoton is a model, not a definition. Exact wording needs the book.

### 20. Maturana and Varela, *Autopoiesis and Cognition* (1972/1980); Varela, Maturana and Uribe (1974)

- **Definition.** "An autopoietic machine is a machine organized (defined as a unity) as a network of processes of production (transformation and destruction) of components which: (i) through their interactions and transformations continuously regenerate and realize the network of processes (relations) that produced them; and (ii) constitute it (the machine) as a concrete unity in space in which they (the components) exist by specifying the topological domain of its realization as such a network" (p. 78) [C, as quoted by S26]. They claim the notion is necessary and sufficient to characterise a living system [C, as reported by S25].
- **Who.** Biologists.
- **Takes life to be.** A kind of organisation producing itself, with no genome required [C, S25].
- **Objection.** Luisi's group built "autopoietic vesicles" that are not alive, so autopoiesis is not sufficient; Bitbol and Luisi locate the difference in metabolism and selective interaction [C, as reported by S25; Bitbol and Luisi 2004 blocked].
- **Word check.** Fails for "life". The defined term is **autopoietic machine / system**; the word used for the phenomenon is **living system**. A compilation's "Life is a metabolic network within a boundary" under their names [S3] should not be treated as theirs (Discard list).

### 21. Dawkins, *River Out of Eden* (1995), *The Selfish Gene* (1976)

- **Passage.** "Life is just bytes and bytes and bytes of digital information" and "all life evolves by the differential survival of replicating entities" [U; search summaries only; one publisher blurb varies the wording].
- **Who.** Evolutionary biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** Digital information; replicators.
- **Objection.** None opened.
- **Word check.** Passes on the wording as reported. Both are aphorisms, not offered as definitions. No page numbers.

### 22. Margulis and Dorion Sagan, *Microcosmos* (1986), *What is Life?* (1995); Margulis, *Symbiotic Planet* (1998)

- **Passage.** "To be alive, an entity must first be autopoietic—that is, it must actively maintain itself against the mischief of the world" (*Microcosmos*, p. 56, per Clarke's section on that book) [C, as quoted by S45]. "Life is matter that chooses" (*What is Life?*) [U, from a review reported in a search summary].
- **Who.** Biologist; science writer.
- **Takes life to be.** Autopoietic self-maintenance. Clarke notes Margulis took the concept from Maturana and Varela [C, S45].
- **Objection.** Within the partnership on Gaia: see entry 23 and §7.
- **Word check.** Passes for "alive"; the book title's question is theirs. Exact wording of the 1995 refrains needs the book.

### 23. Lovelock, *Gaia* (1979), *The Ages of Gaia* (1988)

- **Passage.** The Earth may be alive "not as the ancients saw her—a sentient Goddess with a purpose and foresight—but alive like a tree" [U, blog quotation seen in a search summary; source book not named]. Lovelock and Margulis 1974: "early after life began it acquired control of the planetary environment and that this homeostasis by and for the biosphere has persisted ever since" [C, S27].
- **Who.** Chemist, instrument scientist.
- **Takes life to be.** On the checked text: a biosphere controlling its planetary environment. Clarke: the qualifier Lovelock consistently uses for Gaia is "homeostatic" [C, S45].
- **Objection.** Doolittle 1981: Earth "could not evolve in principle" and granting it life was unmotivated [C, as reported by S1]. Gould: "a metaphor, not a mechanism" [C, as quoted by S42]. Margulis: "Gaia is not an organism" [C, as quoted by S45; *Symbiotic Planet*, p. 119].
- **Word check.** The 1974 paper says "biosphere" and "life", not that the Earth is alive. The stronger phrasing needs the 1979 and 1988 books.

### 24. Kauffman, *Investigations* (2000), Edge conversation, NASA grant report

- **Definition.** "My definition is that an autonomous agent is something that can both reproduce itself and do at least one thermodynamic work cycle" [C, S28]. Grant report: "The tentative definition for a molecular autonomous agent is that it must be self-reproducing and carry out at least one thermodynamic work cycle" [C, S29].
- **Who.** Theoretical biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** He says "all free-living organisms are autonomous agents"; the concept is "inherently a non equilibrium concept" [C, S28].
- **Objection.** Gershenson: "There are non-reproductive organisms which we consider alive. Ant workers, for example" [C, S30].
- **Word check.** Fails. The defined term is **autonomous agent**. Wikipedia and others present it as a definition of life [C, S2w]; neither Kauffman text opened does so. A summary's "proper definition of life" phrase was not found in the opened record.

### 25. Langton, "Artificial Life" (1989)

- **Passage.** "[AL] views life as a property of the organization of matter, rather than a property of the matter which is so organized. Whereas biology has largely concerned itself with the material basis of life, Artificial Life is concerned with the formal basis of life" [C, as quoted by S31, p. 2]. Artificial life "can contribute to theoretical biology by locating life-as-we-know-it within the larger picture of life-as-it-could-be" [C, as quoted by S31; the source's own book reference is Langton (ed.) 1989, pp. 1-47].
- **Who.** Computer scientist, founder of artificial life.
- **Takes life to be.** Form rather than material.
- **Objection.** Strong artificial life drew the pushback strong AI drew (Sober 1991, Boden 1999, Brooks 2001), mostly the ontological objection that representations are not what they represent or that life needs chemical embodiment [C, S1]. Mariscal files Langton under weak artificial life [C, S1].
- **Word check.** Passes. It is a statement of a field's view, not a definition; he does not say what the organisation is.

## 3. Working and recent

### 26. The "NASA definition": Exobiology Discipline Working Group; Joyce's foreword (1994)

- **Wording varies, and this matters.** NASA astrobiology page: "Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution" [C, S32]. NASA interview intro: "life is a self-sustaining system capable of Darwinian evolution" [C, S33]. Joyce in that interview: "a self-sustaining…. chemical system…. capable of undergoing…. Darwinian evolution" [C, S33]. Birch: "Life is a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution" [C, S36]. Joyce's own 2012 paper uses "undergo Darwinian evolution in a self-sustained manner", explaining that all of the bits necessary are "part of the system that is evolving" [C, S35]. Foreword text not opened [U].
- **Who.** A 12-person advisory panel. Joyce: "No, it's not mine"; he thinks he suggested "self-sustaining chemical system"; the plan document "doesn't say, 'The working definition is…'", and "there's nothing official about it. Nobody voted on it" [C, S33]. Benner: "Following a suggestion by Carl Sagan" [C, S34].
- **Takes life to be.** A chemical system that evolves. Joyce: "Darwinian evolution" subsumes reproduction, heredity, and metabolism; "It's the population, or even the ecology, that makes the living system"; "self-sustained" means informationally, "all of the information necessary" being in the system [C, S33].
- **Objection.** Mariscal: "It might include viruses while excluding mules" [C, S1]. Joyce: "a virus doesn't make the cut" [C, S33]. Benner reads the committee as allowing "a cell, virus, or a single rabbit" to be alive while not themselves exemplifying life [C, S34]. So the field reports three different outcomes for viruses from one sentence. Cleland and Chyba: not usefully operational, as reported by Benner [C, S34]. Walker: "Only populations evolve — individuals do not", and must all life be chemical? [C, S40].
- **Word check.** Passes, with caveats: "working definition", not NASA's official definition, and not Joyce's.

### 27. Koshland, "The Seven Pillars of Life", *Science* 295 (2002)

- **Definition.** PICERAS: program, improvisation, compartmentalization, energy, regeneration, adaptability, seclusion [C, as reported by S34 and S43]. Program: "organized plan that describes both the ingredients themselves and the kinetics of the interactions among ingredients as the living system persists through time" [C, as quoted by S43].
- **Who.** Biochemist.
- **Takes life to be.** A list of principles for any life, including artificial and alien [C, S43].
- **Objection.** Koshland's own rabbit story, in the essay: "Then one rabbit is dead. Two rabbits—a male and female—are alive but either one alone is dead" [C, as quoted by S34]. Benner: "a single rabbit remains dead if the listed elements must all be present", and the list "offers little by way of theory" [C, S34].
- **Word check.** Passes; a list of principles, not a one-line definition. Primary text blocked.

### 28. Ruiz-Mirazo, Peretó and Moreno, "A universal definition of life" (2004)

- **Definition.** "'A living being' is any autonomous system with open-ended evolutionary capacities" [C, as listed by S3; the abstract and text were not opened]. A search fragment has "open-ended evolution capacities" and a list of a semi-permeable boundary, energy transduction and so on [U].
- **Who.** Origin-of-life researchers and philosophers of biology [U for individual disciplines].
- **Takes life to be.** Two notions joined: basic autonomy and open-ended evolution [U, search summary of the authors' 2010 follow-up].
- **Objection.** None opened.
- **Word check.** Mixed. The title says "definition of life"; the sentence defines **a living being**.

### 29. Benner, "Defining Life", *Astrobiology* 10 (2010)

- **Position.** "Any definition is intricately connected to a theory that gives it meaning"; he examines the "definition-theories" that mission designers "constructively hold" (carbon, cell, Darwinian) [C, S34].
- **Who.** Chemist, synthetic biologist.
- **Takes life to be.** He works with the Darwinian definition-theory, and treats intelligent androids and nanites as biosignatures or artificial life [C, S34].
- **Objection.** His own: "Homo sapiens as a species is already stretching our definition-theory of life" through non-Darwinian inheritance [C, S34].
- **Word check.** Passes. He adds the distinction: "one rabbit may be alive even though he or she is not life" [C, S34].

### 30. Walker: Walker and Davies (2013); "AI Is Life" (2023); *Life as No One Knows It* (2024)

- **Passage.** "the transition to context-dependent causation – mediated by the onset of information control – that is the key defining characteristic of life" [C, S49]. "By 'life,' I mean all objects that can only be produced in our universe through a process of evolution and selection. Being 'alive,' by contrast, is the active implementation of the dynamics of evolution and selection" [C, S40]. Book-tour form, "life is information structuring matter" [U, search summary].
- **Who.** Theoretical physicist, astrobiologist.
- **Takes life to be.** Lineages of information; "We are lineages, not individuals" [C, S40].
- **Objection.** She gives her own hard edge: "Many of us would not recognize mineral diversity as 'life'", and "Some objects — like a dead cat — are representative of 'life'... but not themselves 'alive'" [C, S40]. Critics of assembly theory were not opened for this note.
- **Word check.** Passes, and sharper than most: she splits **life** from **alive** on purpose.

### 31. Cronin: assembly theory (Sharma et al. 2023)

- **Passage.** Assembly theory "redefines the concept of an 'object'" and introduces a measure "capturing the degree of causation required to produce a given ensemble of objects" [C, S41 abstract].
- **Who.** Chemist.
- **Takes life to be.** Nothing here: the abstract's subject is selection and evolution.
- **Objection.** Not pursued here, since no definition is on the table.
- **Word check.** Fails as a definition. The abstract speaks of "selection", not of defining life. Cronin should not be listed as giving a definition unless a text saying so is found.

### 32. Friston, "Life as we know it" (2013)

- **Passage.** "life—or biological self-organization—is an inevitable and emergent property of any (ergodic) random dynamical system that possesses a Markov blanket" [C, S44].
- **Who.** Neuroscientist.
- **Takes life to be.** A property expected of systems with a Markov blanket: "if systems are ergodic and possess a Markov blanket, they will—almost surely—show lifelike behaviour" [C, S44].
- **Objection.** His own distinction: "biological systems act on their environment—unlike a petrified stone with low entropy" [C, S44]. Bruineberg et al. on Markov blankets [U].
- **Word check.** Mixed. He uses "life" in the title and equates it with "biological self-organization"; the claim is about conditions, and his closing word is "lifelike". It is a claim about when life-like behaviour arises, not a definition.

## 4. Against defining

### 33. Cleland and Chyba, "Defining 'Life'" (2002)

- **Position.** "defining 'life' currently poses a dilemma analogous to that faced by those hoping to define 'water' before the existence of molecular theory. In the absence of an analogous theory of the nature of living systems, interminable controversy over the definition of life is inescapable" [C, S7 abstract]. Cleland advocates avoiding definitions for tentative criteria [C, as reported by S1].
- **Who.** Philosopher; astrobiologist.
- **Objection.** Benner accepts the link between theory and definition, and argues a definition-theory still guides design [C, S34]. Mix: "provisional definitions are necessary for clear communications" [C, S5].
- **Word check.** Uses the word in quotation marks, to discuss the word.

### 34. Machery, "Why I stopped worrying about the definition of life... and why you should as well" (*Synthese*, 2012)

- **Position.** Defining life is impossible or pointless depending on whether "life" is the folk concept or a scientific one [U, search summary of the abstract]. Mariscal: "folk concepts need not match up with scientific ones (Machery 2011)" [C, S1].
- **Who.** Philosopher of science.
- **Word check.** On the word and the concept; no definition offered.

### 35. Szostak, "Attempts to define life do not help to understand the origin of life" (2012)

- **Position.** The title itself [C, as quoted by S48]. Definitions "would not change scientific practice" [C, as reported by S1]. Opening claim and "futile" wording [U, search summary].
- **Who.** Biochemist, origin-of-life researcher.
- **Objection.** Brack: "I am tempted to follow Jack Szostak", but "attempts to understand the origin of life will likely eventually help in our ongoing endeavour to define life" [C, S48; hyphenation normalised]. Brack cites the paper as 2011.
- **Word check.** On the activity of defining.

### 36. Tirard, Morange and Lazcano, "The Definition of Life" (2010)

- **Position.** "Life is an empirical concept"; a precise definition "remains an elusive intellectual endeavor"; "as Nietszche once wrote, there are concepts that can be defined, whereas others only have a history" [C, S6 abstract]. If the emergence of life is a stepwise transition, "it may be meaningless to draw a strict line" [C, S6].
- **Who.** Historian of science; historian and molecular biologist; evolutionary biologist.
- **Word check.** Passes; a history of the word's defining.

### 37. Bedau, "The Nature of Life" (1996); "Four Puzzles about Life" (1998)

- **Position.** "supple adaptation defines life at its most general" [C, S23], but "not that supple adaptation is a necessary and sufficient condition for living organisms (it isn't)... but that it provides the best unified explanation of the phenomena of life" [C, S24]. Form: "X is living1 iff X is a system undergoing supple adaptation", and living2 systems stand in relations to those [C, S23].
- **Who.** Philosopher, artificial life.
- **Takes life to be.** A process in a system, primary; organisms derivative.
- **Objection.** He raises it: mules, the last of a species and neutered animals play no role in adaptation, and populations are the wrong logical category; he replies they connect to systems that do [C, S24]. Clay crystallites, chemical networks, economies: see §7.
- **Word check.** Passes; his target is the phenomenon, not "the current meaning of the word 'life'" [C, S24]. He belongs here less as an opponent than as one who gives up necessary and sufficient conditions and keeps a theory.

### 44. Hazen, *Genesis: The Scientific Quest for Life's Origin* (2005), ch. 2 "What Is Life?"; mineral evolution (2008 onward)

- **Position.** Against a sharp line: "Attempts to formulate an absolute definition that distinguishes between life and nonlife represents a similar false dichotomy" (p. 28), because life "must have arisen through a sequence of emergent events" (pp. 28-29). "To define the exact point at which such a system of gradually increasing complexity becomes 'alive' is intrinsically arbitrary. Where you, or I, or anyone else chooses to draw such a line is more a question of perceived value than of science" (p. 29). "'What is life?' is fundamentally a semantic question, a subjective matter of taxonomy" (p. 30) [C, S51].
- **But.** He keeps a working definition for practice: the NASA panel's sentence "is probably as general, useful, and concise as any we are likely to come up with—at least until we discover more about what is actually out there" (p. 27) [C, S51]. He reports Cleland and Chyba's water analogy, the passage ending "Better, therefore, to keep an open mind and simply describe the characteristics of whatever we find" (p. 30; whether the sentence is theirs or his is not clear from the text), and adds his own suspicion that "any universal theory of life will rest, at least in part, on the ideas of emergence" (p. 30). In place of one line, many: "If life arose as a sequence of emergent steps, then each of those steps represents a taxonomically distinct, fundamentally important stage"; "Each step deserves its own label" (pp. 30-31) [C, S51]. His own guess for the first thing to fit the NASA definition is "flat life", a molecular coating on mineral surfaces (p. 28) [C, S51].
- **Who.** Mineralogist, astrobiologist (origin-of-life research at the Carnegie Institution).
- **Takes life to be.** The far end of a graded chemical sequence; where to put the word "alive" on it is a choice made by value. The candidate lines he lists: a membrane ("the intrinsic isolation of each living thing"), self-replication, or genetic information (p. 29) [C, S51].
- **Mineral evolution: co-evolution, not a definition.** The 2008 paper: "The mineralogy of terrestrial planets evolves as a consequence of a range of physical, chemical, and biological processes", one mechanism being "the generation of far-from-equilibrium conditions by living systems"; "Biochemical processes may thus be responsible, directly or indirectly, for most of Earth's 4300 known mineral species" [C, S54]. 2009: "Evolution, the process by which systems under non-random selective pressure become more complex", with "the subsequent co-evolution of the geo- and biospheres" one of its episodes [C, S52]. 2024: "Evidence for the co-evolution of Earth and life is abundantly preserved in the minerals"; "life changed near-surface environments in ways that led to the formation of approximately half of all known mineral species, most of which are only known to form through biological mediation" [C, S53]. An interview line, "Life begets rock, rocks beget life" [U; Smithsonian, 403 here].
- **Objection.** None opened against the false-dichotomy claim. Against the mineral programme's word "evolution", see the stretches in §7.
- **Word check.** Passes for "life", "nonlife" and "alive", the last in scare quotes; he uses "Protolife" for the stages before (p. 29) [C, S51]. In the mineral-evolution texts the stretched word is "evolution" and "co-evolution"; "life" stays the biosphere's, as in the Wong et al. row of §7. Two cross-checks for other entries: he attributes the working definition to Joyce ("he suggested", p. 27), which Joyce later denied (entry 26), and he quotes Maynard Smith as "any population of entities which has the properties of multiplication, heredity and variation" (p. 25), a different wording from entry 38 [C, S51].
- **Placement.** By position he belongs with §4 (declining a sharp definition while keeping a working one); listed here to keep the numbering.

## 6. How many definitions, and how the field sorts them

**Counts.**
- Trifonov: "123 tabulated definitions of life", nine groups of defining terms [C, S2].
- Koh and Ling's Appendix A: 135 listed, of which nos. 1-95 from Popa, 96-128 from Barbieri, 129-135 after 2002 [C, S3]. So Popa's table has about 95, on this report. They date Popa 2003.
- Mariscal: "hundreds of scientists, philosophers, and others have tried", "perhaps thousands of competing definitions proposed across hundreds of articles"; Popa and Trifonov worked from "dozens of definitions" [C, S1].
- Brack: "over 100 definitions of life exist" [C, S48].
- Bender et al.: 68 newly solicited expert definitions, from biology, computer science, physics, engineering, in 14 countries [C, S4].
- Malaterre and Chartier: text-mining across 30,000 articles [C, as reported by S1].

**The field's sortings.**
- **Sagan 1970:** physiological, metabolic, biochemical, genetic, thermodynamic [C, as summarised by S37].
- **Popa 2004:** mechanistic, material-related, holistic, reductionist, cellularist, minimalist, genetic, cybernetic, generalist, vitalist, parametric [C, as tabulated by S1].
- **Trifonov 2011, nine vocabulary groups:** energy, matter, system, chemical, environment, evolution, complexity, reproduction, ability [C, as tabulated by S1].
- **Malaterre and Chartier 2019:** matter/energy (metabolism; catalysis and protein synthesis); structural (cellular); environmental interactions; evolution (evolvability); information (genetics) [C, as tabulated by S1]. Mariscal's table lines all three up under Matter & Energy, Structure, Environment, Evolution, Information, Miscellaneous, and calls this rough [C, S1].
- **Bender et al. 2026:** a manual table (thermodynamics/energetics, information/pattern, complexity, computation, dynamics, autonomy/agency, cognition/intelligence, structure, functionality, replication/evolution, material composition, other), then 8 LLM-derived clusters: perceptual categorization, self-sustaining dynamic patterns, dynamic relational process, pragmatic definitional skepticism, cognitive autonomy, dissipative self-organizing systems, informational self-replication, self-replicating thermodynamic systems [C, S4]. Cluster titles were generated by language models from definitions submitted to them, so they are a method's output, not the respondents' names. Two axes: observer-dependent to objective; entity to process [C, S4].
- **Kind of definition (Mariscal):** theoretical, operational, nominal, ostensive, stipulative; alternatives: prototype, exemplar, theory concepts [C, S1]. Also moves available to skeptics: advocacy, next-best theory, stipulate, specify, polysemy, skepticism, eliminativism [C, S1].
- **Parke, as reported by Mariscal:** applies to individuals or collectives first; material or functional; clean boundaries or a continuum of lifelikeness [C, S1].
- **Mix:** plural explicit categories, "Woese life, Darwin life, Haldane life" [C, S5].
- **Benner:** by the theory carried: list, carbon, cell, Darwinian [C, S34].

**The working sort (life as substrate / as organisation / as process or information), tested.**

- **What holds.** The first cut is one the field makes: material or functional (Parke via S1), and Langton's "organization of matter, rather than... the matter" [C, S31]. Biochemical and carbon definitions sit on one side; autopoiesis and Rosen on the other.
- **Where it fails.**
  1. *Evolution has no home.* It is the largest modern family (Horowitz, Sagan's genetic, Maynard Smith, NASA, Trifonov, Benner), and all the surveys give it a line of its own, separately from information (Trifonov: evolution, complexity, reproduction as three groups; Malaterre and Chartier: evolution and information as two; Bender: replication/evolution, information/pattern, dynamics as three) [C, S1, S4]. "Process or information" merges what the field separates.
  2. *"Process" is a different kind of claim.* In the field it is an ontological stance (life as entity or process, Nicholson and Dupré via S1; Bender's second axis, S4), which cuts across the other two. Mayr's reification point, if confirmed, belongs here too.
  3. *One axis is not enough.* The field also sorts by kind of definition, bearer (individual or collective) and boundary (sharp or graded). None of these fits a three-way list [C, S1].
  4. *Whole families fall outside.* Function-lists (Sagan's physiological, Koshland); energy and thermodynamics (Schrödinger; 26% of Bender's definitions); environment and relation (Trifonov, Malaterre and Chartier, Bernard's external conditions); cognition and agency (Bender); observer-dependent definitions (Fields); vitalism; skeptical positions [C, S1, S4, S12].
  5. *Straddling is the norm.* "Most of the definitions considered by these authors straddle some of these distinctions" [C, S1]. NASA is substrate ("chemical") and evolution at once. Engels is a substance's mode of existence.
- **Plain result.** As a three-way classification of definitions it does not match the field's own and it loses the evolutionary family. As a statement of the material/functional split with a third, unsettled area, it matches part of what Parke reports. The field's own closest neutral headings are Mariscal's six rows: matter and energy, structure, environment, evolution, information, miscellaneous [C, S1].

## 10. Striking contrasts (reported, not argued)

- **Against death, or including the dead.** Bichat: "la vie est l'ensemble des fonctions qui résistent à la mort". Walker: a dead cat is representative of "life" but not "alive". Bernard's paradox sits between: "la vie c'est la mort".
- **Matter, or not matter.** Engels: "the mode of existence of albuminous bodies". Langton: "a property of the organization of matter, rather than a property of the matter".
- **All matter dead, or crystals alive.** Kant: "alle Materie als solche leblos". Haeckel: "Alle Kristalle... sind lebendig".
- **Plants in, or desire required.** Aristotle: living is said if any one sense is present, and plants live by nutrition. Kant: "nach Gesetzen des Begehrungsvermögens zu handeln".
- **Four words, or none.** Trifonov: "Life is self-reproduction with variations". Bernard: "il n'y a pas à définir la vie en physiologie".
- **The object, or the observer.** Maynard Smith's reported suggestion: entities with multiplication, variation and heredity "are alive". Fields: "to be alive is to be considered alive by systems that consider themselves alive".
- **One sentence, two verdicts.** On the same working definition, Joyce: "a virus doesn't make the cut". Mariscal: "It might include viruses". Benner: the committee meant a "virus" could be alive.
- **The same planet.** Lovelock [U]: "alive like a tree". Margulis: "Gaia is not an organism".

## 12. Discard list (attributions that did not hold)

- **Kant "on organised beings" as a definition of life.** His definition of *Leben* is the capacity to act from an inner principle or by desire; organised beings are *Naturzwecke*, and he questions "Analogon des Lebens" for them.
- **Rosen, Maturana and Varela, Kauffman as defining "life".** Their defined terms are organism, autopoietic machine/system, autonomous agent. Readers supply "definition of life".
- **Cronin as a definer of life.** The assembly-theory text opened offers a measure of selection.
- **Joyce's or NASA's definition, as such.** Joyce: "No, it's not mine"; "there's nothing official about it". The name is an accident of a foreword. Best phrase: the working definition from NASA's Exobiology Discipline Working Group.
- **Friston as defining life.** He claims when "lifelike behaviour" arises.
- **Mix 2015 as a survey.** The abstract is a defence of provisional plural definitions; the historical survey Mariscal cites is Mix 2018 [C, S5, S1].
- **"Gaia is not an organism" in *What is Life?*.** A search summary placed it there; Clarke's note cites *Symbiotic Planet*, p. 119 [C, S45].
- **Bernard as flatly declining.** He declines; he also gives "La vie, c'est la création". A compilation's date of 1878 for that phrase is the *Leçons*; he says it is older.
- **Schrödinger: "Life is replication plus metabolism"** (compilation entry, S3). Not his wording; a search of the opened text finds no "replicat-" at all [C, S18; OCR caveat].
- **Maturana and Varela: "Life is a metabolic network within a boundary"** (S3). Not matched to anything opened; their term is autopoietic; treat as a compiler's paraphrase.
- **Oparin 1961: "Any system capable of replication and mutation is alive"** (S3). Untraced, and at odds with his metabolism-centred account in S20; do not use without the source.
- **Compilation tables in general.** Their wordings are compilers' abridgements. Use them for counts, not for quotation.
