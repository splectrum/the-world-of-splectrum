# Homes of the word "life": mapping note

Purpose: a mapping round across the homes of the word "life" (disciplines, traditions, everyday uses): what each means by the word, whether it uses the word itself, and what the site already holds. A working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision. Definitions of life in the biological sense are a separate round and are not redone here. The origin of life, in every sense, is a separate round and is not researched here.

Marking: **[C]** = checked in a source actually opened, named by its key in the Sources list. **[C, as quoted by Hn]** = second-hand: the source opened quotes or reports the author; the author's own text was not opened. **[U]** = unverified (memory, a search-engine summary, or a source that could not be opened). Glosses of non-English passages are mine. Quotes are as found; OCR has been normalised only where the reading is unambiguous.

Each home gives: what "life" means there; key figures and works, with a checked quote where one was found; the word check (does the home use "life", "Leben", "vie", ζωή, βίος and so on itself, or a neighbouring word?); site backing (grep of `docs/positioning/`, 2026-11-07); candidate pages and persons, as options only. Who earns a person page is a collaborative decision; listing a name here admits nothing.

Tally: **19 numbered homes** in four groups (A-D), plus two headline lists (E: four religious and non-Western senses; F: four carried-over uses). The author's own text was opened for **18** (Bergson and Plutarch in translation, Nietzsche, Simmel, Rickert, Aristotle, Elder, Baltes, John, the Kaushitaki in translation, the Yijing, Hazen, NASA, Jaspers, Unamuno, Ortega, Sartre and Beauvoir in translation). The rest rests on reference entries (SEP, IEP, Wikipedia, Wiktionary), marked as quoted where they quote, or is [U]. Unverified items are marked in place and gathered under *Open checks*.

## A. The biological sense, beyond definitions

### A1. Life elsewhere: astrobiology

- **Meaning.** Life as the biological phenomenon, asked about on other worlds. NASA's page: "Astrobiology is the study of the origin, evolution, and distribution of life in the universe" [C, H2]. The SEP entry gives "the search for life in the Universe" a section of its own, as one of the contexts that "may need a definition of life" [C, H1].
- **Word check.** Passes. The field names itself by the word's Greek root and uses "life" throughout; its working vocabulary adds "biosignature" and "habitability" [U for the vocabulary list].
- **Site backing.** No astrobiology subject. The word appears on the Walker and Hazen person pages, the assembly-theory bundle (development, other reception, the biosignature claim) and the mineral-evolution subject. The definitions page carries the NASA working definition.
- **Candidates (options).** An astrobiology subject, if the life doorway wants this home to point somewhere other than the definitions page. Persons named in the field without pages here: Carl Sagan, Carol Cleland, Christopher Chyba (all external links on the definitions page).

### A2. Artificial and synthetic life

- **Meaning.** Life made, simulated or modelled. SEP: "In principle, most contemporary scientists and philosophers believe life can be created. But researchers disagree as to how that would work." Strong A-Life holds that robots or programs "might qualify"; weak A-Life seeks "a deeper understanding of life as we know it" by simulation, "placing life in a broader context of possible biology (Langton 1989, 1995)" [C, H1]. Synthetic biology and "xenobiology" are treated in the same SEP section [C, H1].
- **Word check.** Passes, with a split. Strong A-Life uses "life" literally of software and hardware; weak A-Life uses it of what the models are models of. The everyday English "still life" also has a cellular-automaton sense, a pattern that does not change from one generation to the next [C, H29].
- **Site backing.** Strong. Person pages: Langton, Bedau, von Neumann. Subject: the complex-adaptive-systems bundle (formation and roots, applications, contested receptions, key persons, adjacent traditions). The Game of Life is on several pages. No artificial-life subject.
- **Candidates (options).** An artificial-life subject is possible but the bundle may already carry it; a check of the bundle against SEP §3 would show the gap.

### A3. The living world as a whole: biosphere, Gaia

- **Meaning.** Life as a planetary layer or system. The word "biosphere" was coined by Eduard Suess in 1875, "the place on Earth's surface where life dwells" [C, as reported by H27]. Vernadsky's *The Biosphere* (1926) popularised it, "hypothesizing that life is the geological force that shapes the earth" [C, as reported by H28]. Lovelock and Margulis's Gaia belongs here too; it is already on the site (below).
- **Word check.** Mixed. Vernadsky's working term is reported, from memory, as **living matter** (*живое вещество*), the sum of organisms treated as a geochemical quantity, rather than "life" [U; neither opened source states it]. Suess's definition uses "life" for what the sphere holds.
- **Site backing.** Gaia on the Lovelock, Margulis and Latour person pages and the definitions page. Vernadsky named only on the semiotics subject; the noosphere only on the evolution metaphysics page (Teilhard). No biosphere or Gaia subject; no Vernadsky page.
- **Candidates (options).** A biosphere/Gaia subject; Vernadsky as a person-page question to discuss.

### A4. Vitalism and its decline

- **Meaning.** Life as something over and above mechanism. SEP: "'Vitalism,' the collective label for these responses, was a heterogenous philosophical position. The only common feature was the adherents' doubt of a mechanistic view of life." Vitalists took the defining features of life to be "any of immaterial causes, particular arrangements of matter, a special life fluid, a particular end goal, or even mental forces". On the decline, the Wöhler story is "a cartoon version of history", and "some researchers continued to develop vitalist theories well into the 20th century (Bergson 1959, Driesch 1905/1914)"; "The 20th century saw the mechanist/vitalist divide dissipate" [C, H1].
- **Key figures.** Driesch (entelechy) [U for the term]; Bergson (A5 below). Canguilhem's essays on vitalism [U].
- **Word check.** "Vitalism" is mostly a label applied from outside, and SEP calls it a collective label for responses to Descartes [C, H1]. Whether a given author called himself a vitalist is a per-person check. Driesch's own book title uses the word ("The History & Theory of Vitalism") [C, as listed by H1].
- **Site backing.** Vitalism is mentioned on the Bergson, Bertalanffy, Broad, Polanyi, Dennett, Chalmers, Benjamin and Heraclitus person pages. No vitalism subject; Driesch and Canguilhem have no hits.
- **Candidates (options).** A vitalism subject, which would also hold the boundary with emergentism (Broad is already there). Driesch and Canguilhem as person-page questions.

### A5. Bergson's élan vital

- **Meaning.** Life as a creative impulse running through evolution. SEP: "there must be an original common impulse which explains the creation of all living species; this is his famous vital impulse (élan vital)" [C, H3]. In Mitchell's translation: "The impetus of life, of which we are speaking, consists in a need of creation. It cannot create absolutely, because it is confronted with matter" [C, H4].
- **Word check.** The French phrase is Bergson's [C, as reported by H3; French text not opened]. **The English varies by translator:** Mitchell (1911) writes "vital impetus" and "impetus of life", and the phrase "élan vital" does not occur in the Gutenberg text of his translation [C, H4]; SEP renders it "vital impulse" [C, H3]. English writing that says "élan vital" keeps Bergson's French, not the translation's English.
- **Site backing.** Strong. Bergson person page (creative evolution, élan vital); evolution metaphysics page; process-philosophy subject.
- **Candidates.** None needed.

## B. Life as lived

### B1. Lebensphilosophie (philosophy of life)

- **Meaning.** Life as the starting point of philosophy, against system and abstraction: lived, historical, creative. The label was in use by 1920, when Rickert attacked the movement as a fashion. His *Die Philosophie des Lebens: Darstellung und Kritik der philosophischen Modeströmungen unserer Zeit* (1920) opens with "Das Leben als Modebegriff" (life as a fashion concept), has a chapter "Die modernen Lebensphilosophen", and calls Nietzsche and Bergson "die eigentlichen Lebensphilosophen" (the life-philosophers proper) [C, H9]. Whether Nietzsche, Dilthey, Bergson or Simmel used the label of themselves was not checked [U].
- **Key figures and quotes.**
  - **Nietzsche:** "Vor Allem will etwas Lebendiges seine Kraft auslassen - Leben selbst ist Wille zur Macht" (*Jenseits von Gut und Böse* §13) [C, H5]. Zimmern's translation: "A living thing seeks above all to DISCHARGE its strength--life itself is WILL TO POWER" [C, H6].
  - **Dilthey:** lived experience (*Erlebnis*) and the understanding of "human and historical life"; in the late drafts he "analyzes the categories of life that are relevant to historical knowledge" [C, H7].
  - **Simmel:** "Damit hat das Leben zwei, einander ergänzende Definitionen: es ist Mehr-Leben, und es ist Mehr-als-Leben" (gloss: life thus has two complementary definitions: it is more-life, and it is more-than-life) (*Lebensanschauung*, ch. I, "Die Transzendenz des Lebens"; 2nd ed. 1922) [C, H8].
- **Word check.** Passes for Leben throughout. Two findings. Rickert says Simmel "versteht ... unter dem Wort Leben zwei grundverschiedene Begriffe" (understands two fundamentally different concepts by the word life), and still counts him "ein Lebensphilosoph im weiteren Sinne" [C, H9]. And Zimmern's translation of §13 opens "Psychologists should bethink themselves" where Nietzsche's German has "Die Physiologen" [C, H5, H6]: a translation slip on the very sentence about life.
- **Site backing.** Person pages: Nietzsche (life-affirmation, will to power), Dilthey (*Erlebnis*, the life of the mind, *Leben Schleiermachers*), Bergson, Schopenhauer (*Wille zum Leben*). No Lebensphilosophie subject; Simmel and Rickert have no person pages (Rickert is named on two pages). The phrase "philosophy of life" appears only on the Jonas page and the phenomenology subject, in Jonas's biological sense.
- **Candidates (options).** A Lebensphilosophie subject, which the existing persons would back well. Simmel as a person-page question.

### B2. Phenomenology of life: Michel Henry

- **Meaning.** Life as self-feeling, prior to any world. SEP: "Henry named his approach 'phenomenology of life'"; it "grounds intentionality in a pre-intentional form of appearing, namely immanent affectivity or 'life'"; "life, for Henry, equates with auto-affection, which in turn equates with ipseity. In the language of Christianity, absolute life is God" [C, H10]. A reading of the work as "phenomenological vitalism" exists but "has not been widely echoed by Henry scholars" [C, H10].
- **Word check.** Passes. *La vie* is his word, and he distinguishes absolute life from "the individual living being" (*le vivant*) [C, H10]. The word carries the meaning of affectivity, not of biology.
- **Site backing.** None. No Henry page and no hits for "Michel Henry". No same-surname collision (no `henry` slug).
- **Candidates (options).** A person page, or a strand on the phenomenology subject.

### B3. The lifeworld

- **Meaning.** The pre-scientific world of lived experience on which the sciences depend.
- **Word check.** *Lebenswelt* is Husserl's word; a late Husserliana volume is titled *Die Lebenswelt: Auslegungen der vorgegebenen Welt und ihrer Konstitution* (Hua 39) [C, title as listed by H11]. "Life" here qualifies the world; it is not a claim about living things. SEP's Husserl entry carries the term only in its bibliography [C, H11]; a checked statement of the concept from a reference work or Husserl's *Krisis* is still to find.
- **Site backing.** Strong. Husserl (the lifeworld, *Lebenswelt*), Habermas (lifeworld and system), Luckmann person pages; the phenomenology and social-construction-of-reality subjects; the metaphysics disclosive-branch page.
- **Candidates.** None needed. Related home already on the site: Wittgenstein's "forms of life" (*Lebensformen*), on his person page and the ethics subject's form-of-life line.

### B4. The meaning of life

- **Meaning.** Whether a human life, or human life as such, has a point. SEP: "A large majority of contemporary analytic philosophers have been interested in meaning in life, that is, in the meaningfulness that a person's life could exhibit, with comparatively few these days addressing the meaning of life in the specific sense of querying the point of humanity as such" [C, H12]. Meaning is held "logically distinct from happiness or rightness (emphasized in Wolf 2010, 2016)" [C, H12].
- **Key figures.** Susan Wolf's hybrid view: "Meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness" (Wolf 2015, 112) [C, as quoted by H12]. Thaddeus Metz, author of the SEP entry [C, H12]. Nagel on the absurd [U for wording]. Camus's question whether life is worth living, and the absurd, are in B8.
- **Word check.** Passes, with the field's own split: "meaning **in** life" (one person's life) against "meaning **of** life" (humanity's) [C, H12]. "Life" here is a single life, or the human condition; not the biological phenomenon. SEP also asks whether "a people, nation, or organization" can bear meaning [C, H12].
- **Site backing.** Thin. Thomas Nagel has a page (identity checked; *Mortal Questions* listed, essays "on death, the absurd"); Bernard Williams has a page (`williams-b`; `williams` is George C. Williams). Metz is an external link on the ubuntu subject. Camus is named on the Kierkegaard page. No Wolf page; no meaning-of-life subject.
- **Candidates (options).** A meaning-of-life subject. Wolf and Metz as person-page questions.

### B5. Philosophy as a way of life

- **Meaning.** Life as conduct: ancient philosophy as a way of living rather than a body of doctrine. IEP: Hadot "is best known for his conception of ancient philosophy as a bios or way of life (manière de vivre)" [C, H13].
- **Word check.** Two layers. Hadot's French is *manière de vivre*, and IEP glosses it with Greek *bios* [C, H13]. The English phrase as a book title is the translation's: *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (1981) became *Philosophy as a Way of Life* in English (1995) [C, H13]. A later French interview book is reported as *La philosophie comme manière de vivre* (2001) [U].
- **Site backing.** Hadot named on the Aristotle and Heraclitus pages and four metaphilosophy pages; no person page. "Way of life" occurs widely, mostly in ordinary use.
- **Candidates (options).** Hadot as a person-page question; the metaphilosophy pages already carry the strand.

### B6. The good life: eudaimonia, and bios against zōē

- **Meaning.** Life as a whole lived well. Aristotle surveys the lives (βίοι) men choose: "τρεῖς γάρ εἰσι μάλιστα οἱ προύχοντες" (there are three most prominent), the life of enjoyment, the political and the contemplative, and says the many choose "βοσκημάτων βίον", the life of grazing cattle (*NE* I.5, 1095b) [C, H14]. The city "γινομένη μὲν τοῦ ζῆν ἕνεκεν, οὖσα δὲ τοῦ εὖ ζῆν" (comes to be for the sake of living, exists for the sake of living well) (*Politics* I.2, 1252b29-30) [C, H15].
- **Word check.** The Greek has more than one word, and they do not sort cleanly. *Bios* names a way or course of life, and Aristotle applies it to cattle in the passage above [C, H14]. The *Politics* line uses the verb ζῆν, not the noun ζωή [C, H15]. *De Anima* II.1 defines life with ζωή [U here; not reopened this round]. Agamben's bios/zoē reading (C2) builds on this vocabulary; critics say his split overreads it [U; Finlayson 2010 not opened].
- **Site backing.** Strong for eudaimonia: Aristotle (eudaimonia, the human function, the good life), ethics subject (virtue ethics, form-of-life line), Nussbaum, Foot, MacIntyre, Murdoch, Williams (Bernard), Sen, Sandel pages. *Buen vivir* and Bolivia's *suma qamaña* on the Latin America indigenous-voices page.
- **Candidates.** None needed beyond what C2 raises.

### B7. Life and value: ethics, law, medicine

- **Meaning.** Life as something with standing: when a human life begins and ends, and whether life as such has value. SEP: "The term 'life' is important outside of science. Often, the focus is the beginning or end of individual lives"; "In public discourse, the existence of life is often conflated with its value" [C, H1]. Hazen: "doctors and lawyers require a definition of life in order to deal ethically with patients who are brain dead"; theologians debate "the beginning of human life" [C, H30, p. 26].
- **Word check.** Mixed. In practice the field defines **death**, not life: "death as the irreversible cessation of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole or death as the irreversible loss of personhood" [C, H20]. Schweitzer's "reverence for life" (*Ehrfurcht vor dem Leben*) and "sanctity of life" against "quality of life" are the value words [U].
- **Site backing.** Jonas page (*The Imperative of Responsibility*, future life). No bioethics subject, no Schweitzer page.
- **Candidates (options).** Probably a line on the life landing rather than a page; a bioethics subject is a larger decision outside this round.

### B8. Existentialism and its neighbours: existence, and life when the question turns practical

- **Meaning.** Life as the single human life that has to be lived, chosen and answered for, with no given nature or meaning to fall back on. The movement's working word is mostly "existence"; "life" comes in when the question becomes what to do with a life, and for some authors it carries the whole philosophy. The label is late and narrow: "The word, first introduced by Marcel in 1943, is certainly not a reference to a coherent system or philosophical school", and "only Sartre and Beauvoir explicitly self-identified as 'existentialists'" [C, H31]. "Heidegger resented being described as an existentialist" [C, H34]; Camus "denied being an existentialist" [C, H42]. SEP names Kierkegaard and Nietzsche as "pioneers", and counts "prominent Spanish intellectuals José Ortega y Gasset and Miguel de Unamuno" in the movement's reach [C, H31].
- **Key figures and quotes, with the word, author by author.**
  - **Kierkegaard (1813-1855).** Journal JJ:167 (1843): "Det er ganske sandt, hvad Philosophien siger, at Livet maa forstaaes baglænds. Men derover glemmer man den anden Sætning, at det maa leves forlænds" (gloss: it is quite true, what philosophy says, that life must be understood backwards. But one forgets the other proposition, that it must be lived forwards), going on that "Livet i Timeligheden aldrig ret bliver forstaaeligt" (life in time never becomes properly understandable) (SKS 18, 306) [C, as quoted by H32; the page prints æ as "ae", restored here]. **Word:** the remembered English ("Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards") is a later short form: "only" is not in the Danish, and the backwards half is what Kierkegaard credits to philosophy, his own point being the forwards half [C, H32]. The page credits a Danish short form, "Livet skal forstaas baglaens, men leves forlaens", to Julia Watkin [C, H32]. In the published works the schema has both names: SEP calls it "three 'existence-spheres': the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious", which Kierkegaard "also describes ... as 'stages' (Stadier)—as in the title Stages on Life's Way" (*Stadier paa Livets Vej*, 1845); the pseudonyms "represent different life-views" [C, H33]. His Danish terms (*Existents*, *existere*, *Livs-Anskuelse*) were not checked [U].
  - **Jaspers (1883-1969).** In *Psychologie der Weltanschauungen* (1919) the limit situations are introduced through life: "Diese Grenzsituationen als solche sind für das Leben unerträglich", and without a hold (*Halt*) against them "würde das Leben aufhören" (these limit situations as such are unbearable for life; without it, life would cease) (ch. III, §3, pp. 202-3) [C, H35]. The particular limit situations are "Kampf", "Tod", "Zufall", "Schuld" (struggle, death, chance, guilt), after a section on "Leiden" (suffering) [C, H35, contents]. Of Kierkegaard and Nietzsche: "es kommt ihnen auf das Leben der gegenwärtigen Individualität, auf die „Existenz“ an" (what matters to them is the life of the present individual, the "Existenz") (Einleitung, p. 12) [C, H35]. SEP: "Limit situations are moments, usually accompanied by experiences of dread, guilt or acute anxiety"; "Kierkegaard, rather than Nietzsche, was the existential prototype for his work"; he "was clearly opposed to the naturalistic vitalism evolving from Nietzsche's work" [C, H34]. **Word:** in 1919 *Leben* carries most of the load and *Existenz* is a quoted term glossed by life (rough whole-word count in the OCR: *Leben* 591, *Existenz* 179, *Dasein* 105) [C, H35, rough]. In *Philosophie* (1932) the second volume is "*Existenzerhellung* (Illumination of Existence)"; he avoided the title "Existential Philosophy" though the text uses *Existenzphilosophie* extensively; after 1945 "even the term *Existenz* became increasingly scarce", and he came to prefer "philosophy of reason" [C, H34]. His distinction of *Existenz* from *Dasein* (empirical existence) is not in the opened sources [U].
  - **Heidegger (1889-1976).** "Heidegger reserves 'existence' (Existenz) as a term for designating Dasein's kind of being" [C, H36]; IEP: "In everyday German language the word 'Dasein' means life or existence" [C, H37]. Life is set apart as another kind of being: "other kinds of being include: life, numbers, and perhaps nature"; "Merely living things come to an end"; "Heidegger calls this 'perishing'"; "But, Heidegger argues, 'Dasein never perishes'" (SZ 247), and "demise" is "an intermediate phenomenon" [C, as quoted by H36]. In *Sein und Zeit* §10, philosophy of life (*Lebensphilosophie*, B1) misses Dasein because life is not made an ontological problem, and the being of life is accessible only through Dasein, so that biology rests on the ontology of Dasein [C, as reported by H38, a wiki commentary]; the sentence "Leben ist eine eigene Seinsart, aber wesenhaft nur zugänglich im Dasein" [U; search-engine result, text not opened]. Before *Being and Time* the lecture titles carry the word: *Phänomenologie des religiösen Lebens* (GA60, lectures 1918-21) [C, H36 bibliography, H39]; the 1923 course is *Ontologie (Hermeneutik der Faktizität)* (GA63) [C, H39]; "faktisches Leben" (factical life) as the theme of the 1919/20 course (GA58) [U; search-engine summary of a catalogue record]. **Word:** *Leben* early, *Dasein* and *Existenz* from 1927, with life moved to a different kind of being and death split from perishing.
  - **Sartre (1905-1980).** *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946), Mairet's translation: "there is at least one being whose existence comes before its essence"; "What do we mean by saying that existence precedes essence?" [C, H40]. Life: "there is no sense in life a priori. Life is nothing until it is lived; but it is yours to make sense of, and the value of it is nothing else but the sense that you choose"; existentialism "is a doctrine that does render human life possible"; man "is therefore nothing else but the sum of his actions, nothing else but what his life is" [C, H40]. **Word:** existence carries the doctrine and life carries sense and value (rough count in Mairet's text: "existence" 24, "life" 13) [C, H40, rough]. The remembered "life has no meaning a priori" is Mairet's "no sense in life a priori"; the French "la vie n'a pas de sens a priori" was not opened [U].
  - **Beauvoir (1908-1986).** *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), Frechtman's translation, opens on Montaigne: "Life in itself is neither good nor evil. It is the place of good and evil, according to what you make it", and "The continuous work of our life ... is to build death" [C, H41, Part I]. "Life is occupied in both perpetuating itself and in surpassing itself; if all it does is maintain itself, then living is only not dying, and human existence is indistinguishable from an absurd vegetation" (Part III, "Freedom and Liberation") [C, H41]; "every living movement is a sliding toward death. But if they are willing to look it in the face they also discover that every movement toward death is life" (Part III, "The Present and the Future") [C, H41]. Ambiguity, not absurdity: "To declare that existence is absurd is to deny that it can ever be given a meaning; to say that it is ambiguous is to assert that its meaning is never fixed, that it must be constantly won" (Part III, "Ambiguity") [C, H41]. **Word:** existence and freedom carry the argument; life enters where bare self-maintenance ("vegetation") is set against surpassing. The French was not opened [U].
  - **Camus (1913-1960).** *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942): "There is only one really serious philosophical problem," Camus says, "and that is suicide. Deciding whether or not life is worth living is to answer the fundamental question in philosophy. All other questions follow from that" (MS, 3) [C, as quoted by H42]. The same SEP entry's introduction gives the first sentence with "question" for "problem" [C, H42]; IEP has "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide" [C, as quoted by H43]. The absurd: "The absurd is not in man nor in the world ... but in their presence together ... it is the only bond uniting them" [C, as quoted by H43]; in *The Rebel*, "the absurd is an experience that must be lived through, a point of departure, the equivalent, in existence, of Descartes's methodical doubt" [C, as quoted by H42]. **Word:** life (*la vie*) carries the founding question and "the absurd" the diagnosis; "existence" is not his key term. IEP ties his rejection of the label to "very little interest in metaphysics and ontology" [C, H43]. The French ("Juger que la vie vaut ou ne vaut pas la peine d'être vécue") and the translator of the English quoted (SEP cites the 1955 English edition) were not checked [U].
  - **Unamuno (1864-1936).** *Del sentimiento trágico de la vida* (1913): "Hay algo que, a falta de otro nombre, llamaremos el sentimiento trágico de la vida, que lleva tras sí toda una concepción de la vida misma y del universo" (ch. I) [C, H44]. Its subject is "El hombre de carne y hueso, el que nace, sufre y muere --sobre todo muere--" (ch. I, "El hombre de carne y hueso") [C, H44]. "Porque vivir es una cosa y conocer otra ... todo lo vital es antirracional, no ya sólo irracional, y todo lo racional, antivital. Y esta es la base del sentimiento trágico de la vida" (ch. II) [C, H44]; "en rigor la razón es enemiga de la vida" (ch. V) [C, H44]. The third chapter is "El hambre de inmortalidad" [C, H44]: life here is above all living on. **Word:** *vida* throughout, *existencia* rare (rough count in the Gutenberg text: 386 against 66) [C, H44, rough]. Two translator's choices in Flitch (1921): *sentimiento* (feeling) becomes "sense" in the title *Tragic Sense of Life*; and Unamuno's verbs "a vivir, y a vivir siempre ... A vivir siempre, no a conocer siempre" become a noun, "to life, to eternal life? Eternal life, not eternal knowledge" [C, H44, H45]. Flitch keeps "everything vital is anti-rational, not merely irrational, and that everything rational is anti-vital" [C, H45].
  - **Ortega y Gasset (1883-1955).** *Meditaciones del Quijote* (1914): "Yo soy yo y mi circunstancia, y si no la salvo a ella no me salvo yo" (pp. 43-44) [C, H47]. The sentence directly follows a biological one: "La ciencia biológica más reciente estudia el organismo vivo como una unidad compuesta del cuerpo y su medio particular" (the most recent biological science studies the living organism as a unit made up of the body and its particular medium) (p. 43) [C, H47]. Later, life is the starting point: "the radical reality which is for every one his [or her] life" (*Obras*, VIII: 273); "my life" in the "'biographical' not in the 'biological' sense"; "vital reason" and "historical reason" became "interchangeable terms"; "man, in word, has no nature; what he has is ... history" [C, as quoted or reported by H46]. He counted Dilthey among the "first discoverers" of "the new great Idea of life" (*Obras*, VI: 166) [C, as quoted by H46]. On Heidegger: "the person who has gone deepest into the analysis of life is the new German philosopher, Martin Heidegger" (*Obras*, VI: 40-41) [C, as quoted by H46]; Heidegger wrote to Jaspers that Ortega told him "when he speaks against existentialism, he never means Heidegger" [C, as quoted by H46]. **Word:** *vida* carries it throughout (*mi vida*, *razón vital*). The English "I am I and my circumstances" makes Ortega's singular *circunstancia* plural [C, H46, H47]. The phrase "la vida como realidad radical" in Ortega's Spanish was not checked [U].
  - **Near neighbours.** Marcel coined the label (above); SEP names him with Camus and Merleau-Ponty as "compatriots" of Sartre and Beauvoir [C, H31]. Buber: "beings who are always in living relation with others" [C, as quoted by H31]. Nietzsche is in B1. Merleau-Ponty's, Tillich's and Berdyaev's use of "life" was not checked [U].
- **Word check.** The home splits its words by question. "Existence" carries the doctrine for Kierkegaard's spheres, Jaspers in 1932, Heidegger and Sartre; "life" carries the practical question in all of them (what a life is worth, how it is lived, what sense it has), and carries the whole philosophy for Unamuno, Ortega and Camus. Heidegger is the one who separates the two outright: life is another kind of being, and Dasein dies but does not perish [C, H36]. Ortega read the same analysis as "the analysis of life" [C, as quoted by H46], and Jaspers in 1919 glossed *Existenz* as "das Leben der gegenwärtigen Individualität" [C, H35]. The pivot words are the authors' own; two of the English forms in common use are translators' ("sense" for *sentimiento*, "circumstances" for *circunstancia*). A tool search of SEP's "Existentialism" entry found no "Leben", "vie", "Lebensphilosophie" or "philosophy of life" in it [C, H31; a tool search, not a read-through].
- **Site backing.** Person pages: Kierkegaard (the three stages of existence, "the aesthetic life", "the ethical life"; names "Heidegger, Sartre, Camus" as the existentialist tradition that dropped the religious stage, though Camus denied the label), Heidegger (Dasein, thrownness, being-toward-death; *Letter on Humanism* "against Sartre's existentialism"), Sartre (existence precedes essence; *Existentialism Is a Humanism* listed), Beauvoir (the ethics of ambiguity), Merleau-Ponty, Buber, Nagel (*Mortal Questions*, "the absurd"), Murdoch (critique of existentialism). The phenomenology subject says Sartre "took phenomenology into existentialism". What the pages do not yet carry about life: the Kierkegaard page has no journal line and does not list *Stages on Life's Way*; the Heidegger page has no early *Leben*, no life as another kind of being and no perishing against dying; the Sartre page has nothing on life's sense; the Beauvoir page nothing on life perpetuating and surpassing itself. No existentialism subject. Jaspers: no page; external links on the Ricoeur (SEP) and Arendt (Wikipedia) pages, and his Axial Period on the comparative-philosophy page. Camus: plain text on the Kierkegaard page only. Unamuno: no hits. Ortega: no page; quoted from *¿Qué es filosofía?* on two metaphilosophy pages (the nineteenth century; philosophy and the special sciences), on philosophy's retreat before physics, not on life. No `jaspers`, `camus`, `unamuno`, `ortega` or `marcel` slugs, so no collisions.
- **Candidates (options).** An existentialism subject, which the existing Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Sartre, Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty and Buber pages would partly back. Camus, Jaspers, Ortega, Unamuno and Marcel as person-page questions. If the life doorway points here, the life wording above would first need to go onto the existing Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Sartre and Beauvoir pages.

## C. Life and power

### C1. Foucault: biopower and biopolitics

- **Meaning.** Life as the object of government. SEP: biopower is "a form of power that exerts a positive influence on life, 'that endeavors to administer, optimize, and multiply it, subjecting it to precise controls and comprehensive regulations' (1976 [1978: 137])"; "The modern state had to take care of the life and the wellbeing of its population, and Foucault therefore calls the politics of the modern state biopolitics" [C, as quoted by H22]. His line that modern man is "an animal whose politics places his existence as a living being in question" [U; *History of Sexuality* I, p. 143, not opened].
- **Word check.** Passes for "life" (*la vie*) as population-level biological life; the coined words are *bio-pouvoir* and *biopolitique* [U for the French forms].
- **Site backing.** Foucault named on twelve pages (Nietzsche, Deleuze, Derrida, Ricoeur; structuralism; philosophy of history; metaphilosophy) but has no person page; "biopolitic" has no hits.
- **Candidates (options).** Foucault as a person-page question, on his own standing as much as for this home.

### C2. Agamben: bare life

- **Meaning.** Life stripped for sovereign power. IEP: Agamben develops "bare life" "from the Ancient Greek distinction between natural life—zoe—and a particular form of life—bios", and "bare life is not natural life per se ... but rather, it is the politicized form of natural life. Being neither bios nor zoe ... 'life exposed to death'" [C, H23, citing *Homo Sacer* 88].
- **Word check.** The term *nuda vita* is reported as his rendering of Benjamin's *bloßes Leben* in "Critique of Violence" [U]. His opening claim that "the Greeks had no single term" for what we mean by "life" [U; *Homo Sacer* p. 1 not opened] is the contested point: see B6. IEP notes "Agamben's own inconsistency" on natural and bare life [C, H23].
- **Site backing.** Agamben named on the Benjamin page, as a reader of Benjamin and Schmitt whose reading "several scholars" find strained. No page; no bios/zoē material anywhere on the site ("zoē" has no hits).
- **Candidates (options).** A biopolitics subject holding Foucault and Agamben together would be the natural home, if the doorway wants this branch.

## D. Life in time

### D1. The life course (sociology)

- **Meaning.** A life as a sequence shaped by history and social structure. Elder: "The life course has emerged over the past 30 years as a major research paradigm. Distinctive themes include the relation between human lives and a changing society, the timing of lives, linked or interdependent lives, and human agency" [C, H16]. "Individual lives are influenced by their ever-changing historical context" [C, H17].
- **Word check.** Passes. "Life course" is the field's own term, kept apart from "life cycle" (generational, reproductive sequence) and "life span" [U for the distinction as Elder draws it]. The English translation of Dilthey's late drafts also has "life-course", for what autobiography grasps (D4) [C, as quoted by H7]; his German word was not checked [U].
- **Site backing.** None for the sociology. "Life course" appears on the site only in the carried-over sense (stars, civilisations, Spengler). No Elder page.
- **Candidates (options).** A short life-course subject, or a facet on a "life in time" page.

### D2. Lifespan and ageing (psychology, biology)

- **Meaning.** Development across the whole of a life, growth and decline together. Baltes: "Life-span developmental psychology involves the study of constancy and change in behavior throughout the life course"; "any developmental change includes the joint occurrence of gain (growth) and loss (decline) in adaptive capacity" [C, H18]. Biological ageing (senescence, lifespan as a species trait) was not researched [U].
- **Word check.** Passes ("life-span").
- **Site backing.** The developmental-psychology subject exists but has no hits for lifespan, life course or ageing. George C. Williams (`williams`) has a page; whether it carries his senescence theory was not checked.
- **Candidates (options).** A lifespan line on the developmental-psychology subject. Ageing biology is a separate check.

### D3. Death and mortality

- **Meaning.** Life's end, and death as what gives a life its shape. SEP's "loss of life account of death": "Being alive seems to involve [having the capacity for vital activities] ... To die is to lose this capacity"; and it is "thoroughly established in ordinary usage" [C, H19]. Simmel puts death inside life: "der Tod dem Leben von vornherein einwohnt" (death dwells in life from the start) [C, H8, ch. I].
- **Word check.** Death is defined through life and life through death: the loss-of-life account [C, H19]; Bichat's "l'ensemble des fonctions qui résistent à la mort" [U here; not reopened this round]. The philosophy of death asks whether frozen embryos are "alive" at all [C, H19], a live/dead question, not a "life" question.
- **Site backing.** Heidegger (being-toward-death), Arendt (natality against mortality), Nagel (*Mortal Questions*), Schopenhauer pages. Heidegger's split of dying from the perishing of living things, and death among Jaspers's limit situations, are in B8. Simmel's third chapter "Tod und Unsterblichkeit" [C, H8] has no site home. No death subject.
- **Candidates (options).** A line on the life landing pointing to these pages; a death subject is a larger decision.

### D4. Biography and life writing

- **Meaning.** A life as something told. Plutarch: "For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives" (*Alexander* 1.2, Perrin's translation) [C, H21]. Dilthey: in autobiography "a life-course stands as an external phenomenon from which understanding seeks to discover what produced it"; "the initial tasks of 'explicating a historical nexus are already half solved by life itself'" (SW III, 221) [C, as quoted by H7].
- **Word check.** Passes. "Biography" is the writing of *bios*; Plutarch's Greek word for his "Lives" is reported as βίους [U; Greek not opened]. "Life writing" as the name of a field was not checked [U].
- **Site backing.** Dilthey page (biography of Schleiermacher). The "autobiography" hits on the site are persons' own autobiographies, not the theory.
- **Candidates (options).** A line on a "life in time" page; no subject proposed.

## E. Religious and non-Western senses (headline level)

- **Eternal life.** The Gospel of John: "ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτὸν μὴ ἀπόληται ἀλλὰ ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον" (John 3:16; also 3:15) [C, H24]. **Word check:** the word is ζωή, the very word Agamben's reading assigns to "natural" life. Henry takes "absolute life" in this Christian sense [C, H10]. Site backing: none.
- **Prāṇa (Upanishads).** "Life is prâna, prâna is life. Immortality is prâna, prâna is immortality. As long as prâna dwells in this body, so long surely there is life" (Kaushitaki Upanishad III.2, Müller 1879) [C, H25]. **Word check:** *prāṇa* is breath, the vital force; the Sanskrit word Müller renders "life" here is reported as *āyus* (lifespan) [U]; *jīva* (the living being, in Jainism the soul) is a third word [U]. Site backing: none (no hits for prāṇa); the Śaṅkara and Abhinavagupta pages exist but were not checked for it.
- **Sheng (生) in the Yijing.** "生生之謂易" (Xici A, ch. 5) and "天地之大德曰生" (Xici B, ch. 1) [C, H26]. **Word check:** 生 is birth, growth, producing and living at once; "life" is one translator's choice among "generation", "production" and "giving life" [U for named translators]. *Qi* is breath or vital energy, not "life" [U]. Site backing: Zhuangzi has a page (life and death stories not checked); no hits for qi or sheng.
- **Indigenous relational senses.** On the site already: the Navajo Fundamental Law names *iiná*, life, among the four ordained principles (metaphilosophy, indigenous North America); Bolivia's constitution takes *suma qamaña*, living well, and *ñandereko*, harmonious life (Latin America, indigenous voices); ubuntu has a subject. Not researched further this round.
- **Candidates.** None at this level. Each would need its own round before any page.

## How the homes might group

How reference works and the field divide the senses, as found:

- **SEP keeps biology apart.** Mariscal's "Life" entry: "This article focuses on the subject matter of biology: life", and its sections run definitions, skepticism, artificial and synthetic life, origins, the search for life, "the macro and the micro" (viruses, larger entities), and "ethics, law, and politics" [C, H1]. The lived senses go to other SEP entries: "The Meaning of Life", "Death", "The Definition of Death", "Existentialism", and the person entries [C, H12, H19, H20, H31]. No SEP entry was found for Lebensphilosophie, vitalism, artificial life, biopolitics, Simmel, Agamben, Hadot or Unamuno, and no IEP entry for Unamuno or Jaspers (404 at the expected slugs, 2026-11-07; a slug check, not a full search).
- **Meaning in, meaning of.** The meaning-of-life literature sorts its own question by bearer: one person's life against humanity's [C, H12].
- **The Greek vocabulary.** ζωή, βίος and the verb ζῆν do different jobs in the texts opened (B6, E), and a widely used modern division (Agamben's) is built on them and contested [C for the texts, U for the critics].
- **Existence and life.** The existentialist home sorts the two words by question: "existence" for the doctrine, "life" for what a life is worth and how it is lived; the Spanish pair and Camus put life at the centre, Heidegger separates life from Dasein, and Ortega read Heidegger as "the analysis of life" (B8) [C, H36, as quoted by H46].
- **The critics' sort.** Rickert divides the life-philosophers by how they use the word, and finds two concepts under one word in Simmel; he ties the movement to "Das biologistische Prinzip" (a chapter title) [C, H9].
- **Dictionary senses.** Wiktionary's English entry runs from the state of organisms, through lifeforms collectively, a living being, existence, a period of existence, animation, a biography, to life insurance and component lifetimes [C, H29; ordering reported only roughly]. OED and Merriam-Webster were not reachable (see Blocked sources), and their sense divisions would be the stronger check.
- **A working grouping that the material supports** (to test, not a decision): the living (A); the living from inside (B1-B3); a life as lived and valued (B4-B8); life governed (C); a life in time (D); other traditions' words (E); the word carried over (F). Several homes straddle: Dilthey sits in B1 and D4, Simmel in B1 and D3, Aristotle in B6 and in the biological definitions, Henry in B2 and E; existentialism (B8) touches B1 (Heidegger's early *Leben*, Ortega's debt to Dilthey), B4 (Camus) and D3 (Heidegger and Jaspers on death).

## Discard list

- **"Élan vital" as the English of Mitchell's 1911 translation.** He writes "vital impetus" and "impetus of life".
- **"Philosophy as a way of life" as Hadot's 1981 title.** It is the English title of 1995; the French book is *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique*.
- **Aristotle's *bios* as human-only.** *NE* 1095b gives "βοσκημάτων βίον", the life of grazing cattle.
- **The *Politics* "living well" line as a use of ζωή.** It uses the verb ζῆν.
- **Zimmern's "Psychologists" in BGE §13.** Nietzsche wrote "Physiologen"; quote the German or a later translation.
- **Bioethics as defining life.** The opened literature defines death; "sanctity of life" is a value word, not a definition.
- **"Life can only be understood backwards" as Kierkegaard's words.** The journal has no "only", and credits the backwards half to philosophy; the short form is a later condensation.
- **"Life has no meaning a priori" as Sartre's English.** Mairet writes "there is no sense in life a priori".
- **Flitch's "eternal life" in Unamuno ch. II as Unamuno's noun.** The Spanish has the verb, "a vivir siempre".
- **"I am I and my circumstances" as the 1914 wording.** Ortega wrote the singular, "mi circunstancia", and the sentence goes on "y si no la salvo a ella no me salvo yo".
- **Camus as a self-described existentialist.** He denied the label; only Sartre and Beauvoir took it.
