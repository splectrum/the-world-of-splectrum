There was a running joke about Hilary Putnam. Every decade, a new realism. It made it into Daniel Dennett's *Philosophical Lexicon*, the philosophers' in-house dictionary of coined insults and affectionate jabs, where a **hilary** is defined as a very brief but significant period in a philosopher's career. Scientific realism in the sixties. Internal realism from the late seventies. Natural realism in the nineties. Three positions, one career, and the word "realism" stretched across all of them like a tent over a man who kept moving.

The three are quickly felt, if not quickly argued. Scientific realism says: our best theories are approximately true, and the things they posit — electrons, genes, fields — really exist; anything less would make the success of science a miracle. Internal realism says: true, but only within a conceptual scheme; what counts as an object depends on the framework in use, and a world of three individuals holds three objects or seven depending on how you count — with no answer beneath the counting. Natural realism says: enough apparatus — we perceive the world directly, mind and body and world one threefold cord, and the ordinary claim that the cup is on the table needs no theory standing behind it. Notice what changes each time: not just the doctrine, but the *register*. You can hear it. The first speaks laboratory. The second speaks framework. The third speaks kitchen table.

And that is the turn worth making. Read the three not as three answers to one question — *what is real?* — but as three languages in which the word "real" does its work. In the language of science, "real" earns its keep exactly as scientific realism says: electrons are as real as anything the vocabulary can name, and the no-miracles argument is that language taking its own success seriously. In the language of frameworks, "real" works scheme-internally, precisely as internal realism describes — three objects here, seven there, both in order. In the language of the everyday, "real" needs no argument at all: the cup is on the table, and only a philosopher would ask for more. Each realism is right — in its language. What none of them gets to be is the owner of the word.

Read that way, the middle of the career stops looking like retreat. Two of the moves can be heard as the same move: a vocabulary giving up its claim to be *the* vocabulary. The scientific realist stops holding that science's language is the one true description of the world. The internal realist stops holding that the scheme's-eye view is the last word about truth.

But the third move does not fit that pattern, and it is worth being accurate about why. Putnam did not leave internal realism by loosening it further. He left it because he had come to think it was a mistake. Internal realism had replaced correspondence-truth with what would be rationally acceptable under good enough conditions — but coherence, simplicity and empirical adequacy are themselves stated inside a scheme, so the standard went relative along with everything else. The position could not stop short of the relativism he had built it to avoid. What he gave up was the verificationist account of truth and the interface picture of perception standing between us and the world, and giving those up moved him *towards* realism, not further from it. He said as much: internal realism was an error, and he was a realist again, if not a metaphysical one.

So the direction did change. Something else, though, did not. Conceptual relativity — the three individuals and the seven sums, the count with no answer beneath it — is the piece Putnam carried out of the middle period and never gave back. He thought he could hold it without the verificationist truth it had arrived attached to, and the later work is largely the attempt to show how.

Which leaves an irony worth owning rather than hiding. Reading a career as three languages, each right within itself and none owning the word, is internal realism's own move applied to a life instead of a world. The tool comes from the phase Putnam later called a mistake. Whether that disqualifies the reading or recommends it depends on what you make of the part he kept — and he kept precisely that part.

One aside, because it is too good to leave out. The philosopher Putnam fought longest and hardest — thirty years of papers and rejoinders — was Richard Rorty, who held that there are only vocabularies, all the way down, none of them answerable to anything deeper. The thing Putnam carried through every change is the thing that sits nearest to Rorty's territory: schemes that carve differently, with no privileged carving. He spent those thirty years insisting that this was not what Rorty said it was — that a scheme still answers to something, that rational acceptability is not mere agreement.

That is the hard part, and it is worth stating as the question he left rather than as a verdict on him. Whatever a scheme answers to cannot be a standard standing above the schemes, because there is no above — that was his own argument against metaphysical realism, and it does not stop applying when the position is his. But it cannot be mere agreement either, or Rorty was right all along. Something that is neither, reachable from inside, is what the position needs. Putnam spent a career looking for it in the criteria of rational acceptability. Whether it might be found somewhere else entirely — in what is held in common between schemes rather than in what stands over them — is a question his work leaves open, and a good one to have been left.
