[From an Inquiry into the Whole](/blog/2026/11/from-an-inquiry-into-the-whole/) ended on a question: what was natural philosophy now lies scattered across many sciences, so does anything still hold them together? What is left as the core, the questions of what there is and how we can know it, keeps shrinking. Is it becoming an empty seat?

From SPLectrum's point of view it is held together, but not from above. What holds it lies underneath: the experience of reality expressed in language. Whatever a physicist, a cook or a bacterium knows, it knows because it met something and took it up in a language of its own.

From there knowledge multiplied. Physics speaks the languages of mathematics, itself a bundle of them. Chemistry has its formulas, biology and medicine their vocabularies, built up over centuries for what each needs to say. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life: the painter's colours, the cook's recipes. A specialism is to a large extent a set of specialised languages, ways of saying and seeing suited to the field.

None of them can be the centre, each just one way among many of taking up the experience of reality. The hope that one language could say what reality is, once and for all, is a hope that time will stand still. Languages do not. They change as knowledge grows, and a language that stops changing is the one we call dead.

This does not mean we're left with loose ends: languages are not islands. [Wittgenstein](/positioning/persons/w/wittgenstein/) pictured our language as "an ancient city: a maze of little streets and squares, of old and new houses, and of houses with additions from various periods; and this surrounded by a multitude of new boroughs with straight regular streets and uniform houses". The symbolism of chemistry and the notation of the calculus were, for him, "suburbs of our language". The new boroughs are laid out to a plan and the old town is not, but it is one city: the streets run into each other, words move from quarter to quarter, and every borough was built out from what was already there. No quarter rules the city, and none stands on its own.

For SPLectrum this is where it starts: not from what the whole is, but from how it is reached, how everything relates to everything else. Those relations can be put into language, and that is the way we come to know more. And "we" is meant broadly. Anything that is relates to what surrounds it, and that relating is a language. A stone lying in the sun has one. So does a bacterium swimming towards food, comparing the level of a few seconds ago with the level arriving now. Bacteria signal to each other across a population, with a molecule that microbiologists themselves call a common language. A village has its customs. The languages of the sciences did not grow outside nature either. They belong to the same web, made by people who are part of nature. Nothing in it stands outside, and the human case is one region of the web, not its centre.

Put all those languages together and they cover the whole. That field can be named again: a modern natural philosophy. The death of philosophy as judge did not end the project. It may be what lets natural philosophy rise from its ashes.

It is natural in the way the first inquiry was, with the whole as its object. It is modern in where it finds the whole: not in one description over everything, but in the relating of the many. The inquiry is into how the languages relate and meet, and how together they grow into a web of growing complexity.

What it gives the specialisms back is a place to meet, not a seat that judges. Every field of inquiry is a voice among interrelated voices, each with equal standing in potential. A modern natural philosophy does not take over what they say. A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth; what it cannot do on its own is take the broad view: see its neighbours and what its neighbours see. Where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science.

The first inquirers wanted the whole. It is still there: not as one philosophy on top, but as a plurality of languages that together hold it all.
