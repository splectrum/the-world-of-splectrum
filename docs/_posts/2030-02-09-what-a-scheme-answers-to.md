[The portrait](/blog/2026/10/the-man-who-kept-changing-his-mind/) ended on the part Hilary Putnam kept. Three realisms, and through all of them one piece he never gave back: conceptual relativity, the observation that the same world admits more than one true description and that none of them is the world's own.

He held two constraints at once. The first is that nothing stands above the schemes. First, there is no God's-eye view from which the one true description could be read off. And second, that truth cannot be mere agreement. This was his line against Richard Rorty for thirty years: whether a reform is a change for the better, he argued, is logically independent of whether most people come to see it as one, or else "reform" names an arbitrary preference. A whole community can converge on what is false. Anyone who accepts both constraints inherits a question. If a scheme does not answer to the world's own description, and does not answer to the community's vote, what does it answer to?

His first answer was idealised rational acceptability: a claim is true if an ideal inquirer, with all the evidence, would accept it. That was internal realism, and he gave it up for his own reasons rather than his critics'. It could not accommodate truths that lie beyond any possible verification, and he held that there are such truths, the unrecoverable past his kind of example: nobody will ever check what a particular Roman said on a particular morning, and it is still either true or false that he said it. Giving that answer up moved him towards realism, not away from it. What he did not give up was conceptual relativity. He left the two constraints standing with the question between them open.

Here is one way to read what he left open, and it is a reading of the position I hold, not a verdict on his. Convergence is an outcome, not a standard: nothing above the schemes certifies it, and it is checkable from where the subjects stand, which meets the first constraint. But what converges is the language, not the verdicts. Two communities share a voice when they hold enough in common that a claim made by one has a sense for the other: what it says, what would count as checking it, what would follow if it held. The claim about the Roman has all three from the shared voice it is made in: its meaning is complete, and what would check it is as clear as for any claim about yesterday. What it lacks is a verdict, and here the position parts from Putnam. He held that the claim is true or false regardless. The position says it has its full meaning in the common understanding and that no exchange will ever land on it, and stops there; it does not add a verdict that exists where no one holds it.

Putnam's objection lands on convergence of verdicts, not on convergence of the language they are made in. And the second constraint holds, because a shared voice gives claims their meaning and does not settle them. A community that converges on a falsehood is speaking its common language well and being wrong in it, and where it is wrong shows up in the flow: the next experience that does not fit, a wider base, another community's check that crosses over. Being wrong is not a verdict held outside the exchange; it is what lands later. That is the ordinary condition of knowing, not a breakdown of it. The [shared voice](/reality/shared-voice/) is built by the use and exchange Putnam's later realism rested on, and conceptual relativity, the piece he kept, is the ground it stands on: more than one description, none the world's own, and a common language between them strong enough for the describers to disagree.

A second voice on the same seam comes from the other end of the century. [Walter Benjamin](/positioning/persons/b/benjamin/), in the essay on translation, observed that "Brot" and "pain" mean the same thing in different ways of meaning, which is the sharpest statement I know of what a shared voice has to do at the moment of crossing. His account of why they can is a pure language that every tongue intends and none contains, and that is where he places the kinship of languages: messianic, at the end of their history, complete. The position reads the same kinship as dated and partial, found in descent, contact and a shared world: the common concept of bread is as wide as the cultures that share it, for as long as they share it, and no wider. The observation is his and stays. The whole he set behind it is what the shared voice does without.

What the reading keeps is what Putnam kept: nothing above the schemes, truth that is not the vote of the moment, and conceptual relativity as the ground under both.
