In 2010 [Stephen Hawking](/positioning/persons/h/hawking/) and Leonard Mlodinow wrote it down in *The Grand Design*: "philosophy is dead. Philosophy has not kept up with modern developments in science, particularly physics." Others had said much the same. Steven Weinberg knew of no one in postwar physics "whose research has been significantly helped by the work of philosophers". Lawrence Krauss said that "science progresses and philosophy doesn't", and a few days later took back any "blanket condemnation of philosophy as a discipline". Neil deGrasse Tyson wondered why philosophers concern themselves "with the meaning of meaning".

It sounds like news. It isn't. Philosophers have been announcing the end of philosophy for two hundred years, and hardly any of them called it a death.

Listen to the words they used. [Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/) wanted philosophy to "lay aside the name of love of knowledge and be actual knowledge": an end by arriving. [Marx](/positioning/persons/m/marx/): "You cannot abolish philosophy without making it a reality", where the German for abolish, *aufheben*, also means to keep and to raise. [Carnap](/positioning/persons/c/carnap/) set out to overcome metaphysics by logical analysis, and asked what was left for philosophy: "only a method". [Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/) called the independence of the sciences the end of philosophy in the sense of its completion, and added that "completion does not mean perfection". [Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/), glimpsing the closure of an epoch of metaphysics: "I do not say the end."

Arrival, realisation, overcoming, completion, closure. Each of these words keeps something. And look at what it is that ends. Almost always it is metaphysics, or philosophy as the court that judges every other kind of knowledge: what [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) spelled with a capital P, "the Platonic attempt to sift out the merely contingent and conventional truths from the Truths which were something more than that". Most of the announcements also name what carries on: positive philosophy for Comte, a method for Carnap, thinking for Heidegger, philosophy with a small p for Rorty.

The scientists aim elsewhere. Their target is the discipline as it stands, and what it does for physics. The replies came from close to physics itself. Tim Maudlin: the philosophy of physics "has become seamlessly integrated with the foundations of physics work done by actual physicists". [Carlo Rovelli](/positioning/persons/r/rovelli/): "Those who deny the utility of philosophy, are doing philosophy." There is a history behind Maudlin's point. By the 1960s, as Christian Wüthrich tells it, "most of the people working in fundamental physics had turned away from the foundational problems of quantum mechanics". A few, John Bell, Abner Shimony and [Hilary Putnam](/positioning/persons/p/putnam/) among them, kept at those problems, and today most philosophers and many physicists agree that a central one is still open.

Nor is the scientists' claim new. In 1886 [Nietzsche](/positioning/persons/n/nietzsche/) was already writing against "the declaration of independence of the scientific man, his emancipation from philosophy".

Two observations.

The first: every end tells you what its author took philosophy to be. To say that it is finished, you need a picture of what it was for: the foundation under the sciences, the completed system, a rival account of nature that lost. So the announcements are a record of how philosophy saw itself at each point, not only of how it was meant to stop.

The second: philosophy has been giving its subjects away for a long time. [Bertrand Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) said so plainly in 1912: "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science." Astronomy went that way, then psychology; and Newton's great work, he reminds us, was called "the mathematical principles of natural philosophy". What cannot yet be answered remains "to form the residue which is called philosophy". On that view philosophy will always look as if it is losing, because its successes leave and take other names. Natural philosophy is called physics now.

So, dead? The verdict needs a philosophy that stays put and loses its ground. The record shows one that kept changing what it was. That may be the better question to put to it: not whether it has ended, but what it has become.
