"Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution." The definition of life most people know comes from a NASA panel working on exobiology in 1994. But it has its troubles. It "might include viruses while excluding mules", as [Carlos Mariscal](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/life/) notes. [Gerald Joyce](https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/forming-a-definition-for-life/), who was on the panel, answers that a virus doesn't make the cut, and that "capable" belongs to the system and not to the single mule: "It's the population, or even the ecology, that makes the living system."

There are many definitions of life, and they fall into families. Two questions help to tell them apart. Is it about life, or about being alive? And how far does it reach beyond the biology we know?

Some go by what life is made of. "Life is the mode of existence of albuminous bodies," wrote [Engels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Engels). The word is life, the thing meant is whatever is alive, and the reach is short: this is the family most tied to our own chemistry.

Some make a list of what it does. [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) began it: "by life we mean self-nutrition and growth (with its correlative decay)". [Daniel Koshland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_E._Koshland_Jr.)'s seven pillars of 2002 continue it, and they aim further, at any life, artificial and alien included.

Some look at what keeps it running. [Schrödinger](/positioning/persons/s/schrodinger/): "What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy." Here the reach arrives uninvited: "Life processes energy, but so does fire," as Mariscal puts it.

Some look at its form. [Margulis](/positioning/persons/m/margulis/): "To be alive, an entity must first be autopoietic—that is, it must actively maintain itself against the mischief of the world". That is about being alive, and it says so. [Langton](/positioning/persons/l/langton/) takes form all the way: life is "a property of the organization of matter, rather than a property of the matter which is so organized".

Some look outward, from the organism to what surrounds it: "The continuous adjustment of internal relations to external relations," in [Spencer](/positioning/persons/s/spencer/)'s words. Some look at what it creates: "La vie, c'est la création," said [Claude Bernard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Bernard), life is creation. Some look at how it acts: "biological systems act on their environment", says [Friston](/positioning/persons/f/friston/), "unlike a petrified stone".

Some look at how it evolves, and that is NASA's family. It speaks most plainly about life and not about the single living thing, while its word "chemical" keeps it close to the biology we know.

And some decline. Bernard himself taught that life can be characterised but not defined. [Carol Cleland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Cleland) and [Christopher Chyba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Chyba) compare the search to defining water before molecular theory.

The route to a more satisfactory definition and understanding of life may well lie in the clear separation of life and alive, and the role that what is not alive plays in this. The two have been running together all along. Schrödinger asked both in one breath: "What is the characteristic feature of life? When is a piece of matter said to be alive?" [Sara Imari Walker](/positioning/persons/w/walker/) in an [essay](https://www.noemamag.com/ai-is-life/) put it as follows: "Some objects — like a dead cat — are representative of 'life'... but not themselves 'alive'." She defines life as "all objects that can only be produced in our universe through a process of evolution and selection", a definition that sits on top of the [assembly theory](/positioning/subjects/a/assembly-theory/) she developed with [Lee Cronin](/positioning/persons/c/cronin/). That takes in what we build as well: "Technology is not artificially replacing life — it is life."

Life is more than what is alive. Anything alive depends on substances that are not alive themselves. And as life evolved so did those substances, both in quantity and complexity. More interestingly, life couldn't start without them. Evolution on the early Earth made the precursors that made life possible. [Robert Hazen's mineral evolution](/positioning/subjects/m/mineral-evolution/) is so tied in with life itself that it makes the origin of life far fuzzier than it already was. Where, in all that, do life and aliveness begin?

It may turn out that we need to look beyond biology to answer that question.
