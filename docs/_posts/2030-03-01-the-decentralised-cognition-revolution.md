In [a revolution in cognition?](/blog/2026/10/a-revolution-in-cognition/) I said the revolution worth having is to understand cognition from the ground up — in AI, and equally in life. This is the part of that I find most interesting, and it is not a prediction. It is something already true that building the thing has made hard to keep ignoring.

Cognition is decentralised. Not becoming decentralised — it is decentralised, in every place we can look at it closely.

## Start with the brain

There is no room in the head where the thinking happens. What there is instead is a great many processing centres, each taking input from somewhere and producing output that gets used elsewhere, and the routing is not fixed. No centre is in charge. No centre holds the whole picture. Competence that looks unified from the outside is distributed across the traffic between parts, none of which is doing the thing we would call understanding.

This has been the working picture in neuroscience for a long time, and it is worth pausing on how strange it should feel. The most familiar cognitive system we have, the one we experience from the inside as a single continuous point of view, does not have a centre. The unity is what the running produces, not what it starts from.

## The same shape, in the living world

Look outward and the pattern repeats at every scale.

An ant colony solves problems no ant has any grasp of. A slime mould finds efficient paths through a maze with no nervous system at all. Bacteria coordinate through chemical signalling, and cells in a developing body arrive at an arrangement none of them is holding a plan for. In each case competence sits in the interaction between participants rather than in any participant.

Humberto Maturana put the general claim as plainly as it can be put: *"Living systems are cognitive systems, and living as a process is a process of cognition."* The point is not that a bacterium thinks. It is that maintaining viability through interaction with an environment *is* the process — and that process does not require, and mostly does not have, a place where it is centrally run.

So the distributed brain is not an oddity of brains. It is what the process looks like wherever it happens.

## And then we built one

Here is the part that makes this hard to keep ignoring.

Set out to construct cognition, and see what you end up with. Not one component that reasons, but many that do narrower things, passing outputs to each other, with the routing not fixed in advance. Attention mechanisms deciding what to weight. Layers producing intermediate results that later layers use in ways nobody specified. Systems built from components handed genuine autonomy over their part, because the alternative — specifying every path in advance — is exactly what did not work for forty years.

We did not choose decentralisation as a design philosophy. We arrived at it, repeatedly, because it is the shape the thing has.

That is the argument, and it is not about machines. Three places to look — the brain, the living world, and the systems we are now building — and the same structure in all three. Two of those we could hold at arm's length. The third we made ourselves, which makes it much harder to treat as someone else's curiosity.

## What the cocoon was

Our extending has been done inside a cocoon: myopic, and strongly anthropocentric. Extensions as inert instruments, a [Cartesian](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/) subject sitting at the centre with capability radiating outward, and an assumption sealed in that nobody had to say aloud — that cognition is what *we* do, and everything else is machinery.

The cocoon was never about the tools. It was about where the thinking was assumed to be. Put the reasoner at the centre, and every distributed system becomes something to be explained away: the colony is *merely* chemistry, the network is *only* statistics, and the brain is granted an exemption it was never entitled to.

Take the centre out and the exemption goes with it. What is left is a process running in many places at once, at many scales, and our own case stops being the standard against which everything else is measured for how nearly it manages to be us.

## So the revolution is a waking-up

Cognition will not decentralise. It was never centralised — that was an assumption about ourselves, generalised outward without being examined.

What changes is who is doing the looking. There are agents all around us, running their own processes, disclosing their own worlds — and they were there before we built anything. The machines did not create the phenomenon. They put a version of it on the desk, where it cannot be filed under *nature is complicated* and left alone.

Which is a strange way round for a technological revolution to work: the artificial drawing us back to look properly at the living. It is the part I find most worth being hopeful about.
