Two claims sit next to each other in [Mark Moffett](/positioning/persons/m/moffett/)'s account of human societies, and the interesting thing is that he holds both without ranking them.

The first is unsurprising. Our evolutionary past predisposes us to sort people into us and them — to favour our own, treat strangers with suspicion, and rank outsiders below us.

The second is his actual thesis, and he thinks it has been overlooked. Our ease among strangers — the thing that lets an airport concourse full of people who have never met remain uneventful — is not a modern veneer over that first disposition. It is an ancient adaptation, and it *had to be there already* before human societies could grow past the size where everyone knows everyone. A piece of the equipment, not an achievement laid on top of it.

What he does not do, and this is worth noticing, is decide which one is the real us. Most accounts of this pairing do. The pessimistic version has tribalism as our nature and openness as a thin veneer, maintained by effort, lost under stress. The optimistic version is the same structure inverted: openness is our nature, tribalism a distortion produced by scarcity or manipulation, removable in principle. Moffett's framework has no place for that question, because his interest is in the *mechanism of membership* rather than in which part of us is authentic. And the mechanism is not an affect at all.

That is the part of his account I had wrong before reading him carefully, and it is the part most people get wrong. The us/them distinction, on his telling, is affectively neutral. It is a boundary, not a hostility. Societies may have "neutral or friendly relationships" with one another, he writes, and still retain their separation — separation is the invariant, warmth or antipathy the variable. His minimal case is what he calls a *mere acceptance group*: nothing beyond mutual acceptance linking its members. He devotes a section of his framework to the observation that a society may permit cordial visits from non-members, and deliberately widens his notion of territoriality to avoid ruling out all the interesting shades of grey. The word "xenophobia" is not his; it belongs to his reviewers.

So what makes a society, for him? Three things coincide: members recognise one another as members through shared markers, the identity is *primary* and abiding rather than one affiliation among many, and the group claims exclusive say over a physical space. The markers do the scaling work, and here he is bolder than I expected — with markers rather than faces doing the certifying, a society's size is limited "virtually ad infinitum", and not by brain size. Dunbar's number is a ceiling on how many people you can know, and Moffett's point is that anonymous membership makes knowing unnecessary. An ant colony of billions runs on a scent; the cognitive cost of a marker is near zero.

Which sets up the claim that makes his position contentious, and it follows from the definition rather than from pessimism. A single human "us" — cosmopolitanism, everyone's primary loyalty being to the species — he calls a pipe dream. Not because people are too nasty. Because the conditions of his definition do not obtain: where a wide grouping lacks primary abiding identification and exclusive territorial control, what you have is an *alliance*, which in his scheme is a different category rather than a bigger society. The European Union is his worked example: the member states hold the territory and, he argues, the loyalty. Space aliens landing, he has said, would no more dissolve nations than the arrival of Europeans in Australia caused Aboriginal Australians to dispense with their tribes.

This is contested, and not quietly. When he put the framework to peer review, commentators went straight at it. Marilynn Brewer and Linnda Caporael argued, under a heading about nested versus cross-cutting groups, that people recognise membership in many identity groups "on an equal par", and that his definition is quietly shaped by the nation-state as its prototype. Paul Smaldino pressed the cosmopolitan case with awkward examples — France sits inside the EU; which is the society? — and objected that the scheme "does not acknowledge the fuzzy nature of a society in a cosmopolitan world". Others wanted territoriality dropped so that religions and ethnic groups could count. Moffett's reply to the complaint that his concept excludes those other tiers is one sentence: *that is my intent*.

I find the framework's restraint more interesting than its verdict. Moffett declines to say which disposition is authentically human, and he declines to let a definition do explanatory work — it marks, and the explaining happens elsewhere. Both refusals are of a piece with how he handles everything, which was [the subject of the last post](/blog/2026/10/the-biologist-who-studies-vocabularies/). Whether the definition should be as strict as he makes it is precisely what his critics are asking, and that question deserves more than a paragraph here. It is going on the list.
