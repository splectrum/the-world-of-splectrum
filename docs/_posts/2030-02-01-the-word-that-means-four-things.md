Entropy is the most quoted quantity in science. It has a formula on a tombstone, it explains why your coffee goes cold, and most people who use the word can offer a definition without pausing: entropy is disorder. Or, from a slightly different crowd: entropy is missing information.

Both are in circulation. Both are said with confidence. And each of them takes a side in a dispute that physicists and philosophers of physics have been having for a century and have not settled.

Start with what is not in dispute. Rudolf Clausius coined the word in 1865 for a quantity in heat engines — heat exchanged, divided by temperature. Ludwig Boltzmann then gave it a microscopic reading: count the arrangements of molecules that would look the same from outside, take the logarithm, and you have a number that goes up. Josiah Willard Gibbs produced a version defined over a probability distribution instead of a count. And in 1948 Claude Shannon, working on communication, wrote down a measure of how surprising a message source is — and his formula was Gibbs's, character for character.

Four quantities, then, and a formula shared between two of them. The question is whether they are one thing seen from four angles, four things that happen to share a name, or something in between.

Here is where the popular glosses start doing work they have not earned.

**Entropy is disorder** is the objective reading. The microstates are out there; the system is in one of them; the count of the ones that look alike from outside is a fact about the world, independent of anybody. Nobody needs to be watching. This is roughly Boltzmann's line, and its modern defenders put the case crisply: every system has a definite microscopic state even if we do not know which, whereas no system *has* a probability distribution. Distributions are things we write down. Microstates are things that are the case.

**Entropy is missing information** is the epistemic reading, and its most forceful advocate was E. T. Jaynes, who in 1957 argued that statistical mechanics is a form of inference — that it is "about our knowledge of the world and not about the world itself." Entropy measures what you do not know about which microstate obtains. Jaynes called it subjective statistical mechanics and did not flinch from the word.

You can see why this is attractive. It explains something otherwise odd: why a method that makes no commitment about the actual microscopic state predicts macroscopic behaviour so well. And it makes the Shannon connection natural rather than coincidental — of course the two formulas match, because both are measuring the same thing, which is uncertainty.

The objection is equally simple, and it has been pressed by Michael Redhead, David Albert and Barry Loewer in much the same form: ice melts in an empty room. Processes happen because of how the molecules behave, not because of what anyone happens to know or fail to know about them. Put more sharply by one group of physicists: in distant stars, does heat flow from the cooler to the hotter? In the days before humans existed? After the human race becomes extinct?

There is a case that sharpens this into something you can hold. Imagine a sealed room containing a battery-powered heater which may or may not have been switched on, and suppose nobody knows which. If entropy tracks your ignorance, a fifty-fifty credence spreads across two macroscopically different situations and gives you a number sitting between the two. But the room's actual entropy is one value or the other. It is never the average. And — this is the part that closes off the obvious escape — install a genuine coin-tossing mechanism so the uncertainty is real rather than merely personal, and the distribution is identical. The intermediate number is still wrong.

The Jaynesian answer is not a shrug. Condition on the macrostate actually prepared, and a properly posed problem never spreads credence across distinct situations in the first place; an intermediate entropy is the signature of a badly framed question rather than of the method. As for the empty room: the theory says what any observer with given information would find on looking, and that holds whether or not anyone looks. What is indexed is the information available *about* the system, not a mental state that has to be occupied for the ice to melt.

Whether that answer works is what the argument consists of. It has been running since the fifties and it has not closed.

Which brings me to a third option, and the reason I find this dispute more interesting than a stand-off. Carlo Rovelli proposes that entropy is relative — but relative to a physical coupling rather than to a knower. Entropy depends on a coarse-graining, on which variables count as the macroscopic ones. That much everyone grants. His point is that this choice is not ours:

> A cup of hot tea does not cool down because of what I know or do not know about its molecules. The "choice" of macroscopic observables is dictated by the ways the system under consideration couples.

Or, in the case he uses: what matters about a volume of air is not our abstract knowledge of the ratio of oxygen to nitrogen, but whether that ratio is physically coupled to anything outside. The resulting quantity he calls objective but relative to whatever it is relative *to* — the ignorance reading, as he puts it, stripped of its anthropocentric and subjectivist parts.

He is not alone in the general move. Katie Robertson and Carina Prunkl have argued that the anthropocentric-looking features of thermodynamics point not to subjectivity but to a relativity to the *physical means of manipulation available* — what you can actually do to a system, which is a fact about your coupling to it rather than about your beliefs.

I should say plainly that Rovelli's version has had limited uptake, and that he himself grades his confidence: after setting out the relative-entropy account he writes, "So far, I think all this is solid. Let me now take a speculative step," and the speculative step — that the low-entropy past might itself be perspectival — is offered as a conjecture rather than a result.

So where does that leave the two glosses?

Not exactly wrong. Each names a real position held by serious people. But each is a *position*, offered as though it were a definition — and a reader who picks one up casually has joined a side without noticing there was one. The honest summary is duller and more interesting at once: there are several entropies, related by more than coincidence and less than identity, and whether they are one quantity under different descriptions is open.

Which is a strange thing to be true of the most quoted quantity in science. Though perhaps not so strange. Words that get used everywhere are exactly the ones that stop being asked about.
