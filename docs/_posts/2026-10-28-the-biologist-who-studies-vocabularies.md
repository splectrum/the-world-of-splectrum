In 2000, a field biologist published a paper in *Biotropica* titled "What's 'up'? A critical look at basic terms of canopy biology." It is not about a species or a site. It is about the word *canopy*, and a handful of others like it — and about the fact that ecologists, botanists, zoologists, anthropologists and aerodynamics researchers all use them while carving the forest's vertical space differently. The author, [Mark Moffett](/positioning/persons/m/moffett/), had spent years in that canopy with a camera and a notebook, and had come to think the field's vocabulary was doing quiet work nobody had examined — and that examining it was itself a scientific task.

That is an unusual thing for a field biologist to write, and it is not a one-off. It is how he works. [The last post](/blog/2026/10/the-society-that-cannot-recognise-itself/) followed him asking, in the title of a paper, what a society is; the next follows him answering. This one is about the habit in between — the way he handles words — because the answer is only worth having if the habit holds.

Take the comparison he is famous for: human societies organised more like certain ant societies than like the troops of our nearest relatives. He does not say ants are like us, or that we are like ants. He says something narrower and harder: two different mechanisms producing the same organisational solution, and the comparison *structural, not psychological*. Look at how a society is held together rather than at what it feels like from inside, and the same shape turns up in two places that share no history of it. That is analogy with the difference kept in view, and it is rarer than it sounds. Most cross-domain comparison either collapses into identity — *the brain is a computer*, and the *is like* is forgotten within a sentence — or is abandoned as loose talk. Holding the middle takes constant work: the resemblance stated precisely enough to be useful, the difference stated clearly enough that nobody mistakes one for the other.

The same discipline shows in how he defines. His definition of a society is offered as a *neutral descriptor*, and he says so: it is not a definition's job to explain the phenomenon it names, and he words it to keep assumptions about function from creeping in. Some readers want a definition to do explanatory work and find this unsatisfying. But it is the canopy paper's stance carried over: a term is a mark you make in order to see, not an essence you have discovered, and what it earns you is what it lets you notice next.

And it does earn. Because the definition says nothing about *how* members recognise one another, only that they do, it let him see something a functional definition would have hidden: in the evolutionary shift from recognising faces to reading badges, the ingroup/outgroup boundary itself passed through unaltered. The means of drawing the line changed completely. The line did not. That observation is the ground the next post stands on, and it was available only to someone who had refused to let the word carry more than it needed to.

So a biologist who spends his life photographing ants stops, at one point, to write a paper about a preposition. It is not a digression from the science. It is the equipment being checked before use — and the next post is where he uses it.
