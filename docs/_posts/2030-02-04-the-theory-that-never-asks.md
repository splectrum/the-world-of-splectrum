Thermodynamics has a strange position among physical theories. Its laws were worked out in the nineteenth century, before it was settled whether matter is made of atoms. When the atoms turned up, nothing had to be withdrawn. And the same apparatus now applies to gases, magnets, chemical reactions, living cells, computation, and black holes — domains with almost nothing in common at the level of what they are made of.

Most of physics does not travel like this. Newtonian mechanics has a domain and loses it at high speeds. Quantum mechanics has a scale. Each of the major theories is indexed to a regime — a speed, a size, an energy — and stops where the regime stops. Thermodynamics is not indexed that way, which is why it keeps turning up in places nobody expected it, and why when it did turn up in black hole physics the argument was not whether the mathematics worked but what it could possibly mean.

Einstein thought he knew why, and said so in 1919 in a distinction he drew for a different purpose. There are, he suggested, two kinds of physical theory.

**Constructive theories** build complex phenomena up from a simple hypothesised scheme. The kinetic theory of gases is the model case: posit molecules with certain properties, derive pressure and temperature from their motions. The advantages, in his words, are "completeness, adaptability, and clearness" — you can see the machinery, and you can extend it.

**Principle theories** work the other way. They start from general features of natural processes that have been discovered empirically — that perpetual motion is impossible, say — and deduce what any process must satisfy given that. They are analytic rather than synthetic. Their advantages are "logical perfection and security of the foundations."

Thermodynamics was his paradigm case of the second kind. And this, I think, is the answer to why it travels. A principle theory never commits to a substrate. It says what cannot happen, without saying what the thing is made of that cannot do it. So when the substrate changes — gas to magnet to black hole to bit — the constraint survives, because the constraint was never about the substrate.

Einstein was making the distinction to explain what he had done with relativity, not to praise thermodynamics. But he praised it anyway, in a line that gets quoted often and usually with a clause removed:

> It is the only physical theory of universal content concerning which I am convinced that, within the framework of applicability of its basic concepts, it will never be overthrown.

The missing clause is *within the framework of applicability of its basic concepts*, and it is doing real work. He is not saying thermodynamics is eternally true. He is saying that where its concepts apply, it will hold — which is a much more careful claim, and a much more interesting one, because it puts the question where it belongs: not on whether the theory is right but on where its concepts get a grip.

That is the shape I want to draw out, because it is one this site keeps running into from other directions.

A principle theory is a set of constraints that holds across contexts precisely because it declines to describe any of them in detail. What it buys with that refusal is reach. What it pays is that it cannot tell you what is going on. Thermodynamics will tell you your engine cannot beat the Carnot limit; it will not tell you how your engine works. It rules out; it does not explain. Carnot found the limit on heat engines in 1824 without knowing what heat was, which is either a triumph or an embarrassment depending on what you wanted from him.

There is a general lesson available here about vocabularies that travel, and I want to be careful about how far to take it. The one I am confident of is this: a way of speaking that commits to fewer things about its subject matter will reach further and say less, and both halves of that are consequences of the same choice. Thermodynamics is an unusually clean case because the refusal was forced on it — nobody in 1824 could commit to a substrate, because nobody knew what the substrate was — and the reach turned out to be a gift rather than a limitation.

The less confident thought, which I will mark as such: when a vocabulary turns up somewhere surprising and works, the interesting question is usually not "is this the same phenomenon" but "what did this vocabulary decline to say, such that it still applies here?" For thermodynamics the answer is on the record. For most travelling vocabularies it is not, and the absence of an answer is worth noticing before deciding the travelling means something deep.

Which brings me back to black holes, where thermodynamics turned up and the argument started immediately. A black hole has something that behaves like entropy and something that behaves like temperature, obeying relations with the form of the four laws. Whether that is *entropy* or merely something shaped like it has been argued for fifty years and is not settled — John Dougherty and Craig Callender pressing that the analogy is weaker than it looks, Robert Wald and David Wallace defending the literal reading.

The dispute is exactly the one Einstein's clause anticipates. Nobody doubts the mathematics. What is at issue is whether the basic concepts apply — which is the question a principle theory always leaves open, and the price of a theory built to travel.
