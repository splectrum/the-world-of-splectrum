Natural philosophy began as one inquiry, with the whole of nature as its object, and over two thousand years it grew into the sciences, each under a name of its own. That leaves a question: does anything still hold them together?

Start with what each of them took along when it left: a language. Physics speaks mathematics. Chemistry has its formulas, biology and medicine their vocabularies, built up over centuries for what each needs to say. A specialism is to a large extent a specialised language, a way of expressing what its field works with that the others do not need. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life. The growth of knowledge has been a multiplication of languages.

Those languages did not grow outside nature. SPLectrum starts from a simple observation: to be at all is to be in interaction with what surrounds you, and that interaction is a language. A stone holding its shape against weight and weather has one. So does a bacterium swimming up a trace of food, holding the level of a few seconds ago against the level arriving now. Bacteria signal to each other across a population, with a molecule that microbiologists themselves call a common language. Communities have their customs. The language of a science belongs to the same web as all of these, made by subjects who are part of nature, in the same way. Nothing in it stands outside, and the human case is one region of the web, not its centre.

That changes how the old story ends. When nature narrowed to what could be measured, the one doing the measuring was left outside it. What stayed with philosophy, the questions of knowing and meaning and value, Russell called the residue: what was left over. But if the languages are nature too, nothing was ever outside. The residue is not a remainder. It is where the whole can be seen from.

So natural philosophy can be taken up again, in the old sense: nature as all that is. Only now the whole is found in language. A modern natural philosophy is the inquiry into those languages: how they relate and meet, and how together they grow into a web of growing complexity. It does not take over the content of the sciences, and it has no quarrel with them. It attends to the ground they share, the language each of them is made of.

Seen that way, every field of inquiry, science included, is something like a voice among interrelated voices, each with equal standing in potential. A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth. What it cannot do on its own is see what its neighbours see. And where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science. A view that holds the many together is the counterweight to the narrow ones, and the cross-pollination it allows makes each specialism stronger.

The first inquirers wanted the whole. The whole is still there to be had: not as one philosophy on top, but as the many in contact.
