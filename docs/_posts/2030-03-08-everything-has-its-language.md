Natural philosophy began as one inquiry, with the whole of nature as its object, and over two thousand years it grew into the sciences, each under a name of its own. That leaves a question: does anything still hold them together?

Start with what each of them took along when it left: languages. Physics speaks the languages of mathematics, itself a bundle of them. Chemistry has its formulas, biology and medicine their vocabularies, built up over centuries for what each needs to say. A specialism is to a large extent a set of specialised languages, ways of expressing what its field works with that the others do not need. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life. The growth of knowledge has been a multiplication of languages.

What stayed behind with philosophy shrank with every departure. Russell called it the residue: knowing, meaning, value, the mind, the questions no science had taken. The one doing the knowing, put outside nature in the seventeenth century, stayed outside. The residue split into specialisms of its own, and in the twentieth century philosophy went back to the sciences one at a time, as the philosophy of physics, of biology, of mind. Each looks at one field. The core and the parts stayed apart.

A few voices have asked for the whole back, under its old name, and each sees the whole in its own way. For Nicholas Maxwell it is the universe with "ourselves as a part of it". For Alister McGrath it is nature as something to learn from as well as about. For Roberto Mangabeira Unger and [Lee Smolin](/positioning/persons/s/smolin/) it is the one universe, in which time is real, and natural philosophy, they write, "must turn into something else".

SPLectrum starts from another place: not from what the whole is, but from how we reach it, how everything relates to everything else. Those relations can be put into language, and that is the way we come to know more. And "we" is meant broadly. Anything that is relates to what is around it, and that relating is a language. A stone lying in the sun has one. So does a bacterium swimming towards food, comparing the level of a few seconds ago with the level arriving now. Bacteria signal to each other across a population, with a molecule that microbiologists themselves call a common language. A village has its customs. The languages of the sciences did not grow outside nature either. They belong to the same web, made by people who are part of nature. Nothing in it stands outside, and the human case is one region of the web, not its centre.

Put all those languages together, every one through which something comes to be known, and they cover the whole of nature, from the stars to the stone to the village. That whole is the field of a modern natural philosophy. Every language belongs to it, and none has a special place: not science, and not philosophy either. The inquiry is into how they relate and meet, and how together they grow into a web of growing complexity. It is natural the way the first inquiry was, with the whole as its object. It is modern in where it finds the whole: not in one description over everything, but in the relating of the many.

Seen from there, every field of inquiry is a voice among interrelated voices, each with equal standing in potential. A modern natural philosophy does not take over what they say. A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth. What it cannot do on its own is see what its neighbours see. Where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science. A view that holds the many together is the counterweight to the narrow ones, and what passes between them makes each specialism stronger.

The first inquirers wanted the whole. The whole is still there to be had: not as one philosophy on top, but as the many in contact.
