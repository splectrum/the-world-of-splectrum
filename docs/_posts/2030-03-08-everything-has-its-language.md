Philosophy's [end](/blog/2026/11/the-end-of-philosophy-really/) has been announced again and again, and the inquiry that [started with the whole of nature](/blog/2026/11/from-an-inquiry-into-the-whole/) is now many inquiries, each with its own name and its own languages. Physics speaks the languages of mathematics, itself a bundle of them. Chemistry has its formulas, biology and medicine their vocabularies, built up over centuries for what each needs to say. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life: the painter's colours, the cook's recipes. The growth of knowledge has been a multiplication of languages.

Take that as it is, not as a loss. Each of those languages catches reality from its own side. A specialism is to a large extent a set of specialised languages, ways of saying what its field works with that the others do not need, and so of seeing what the others do not see. Each is a facet of the whole, and the whole is nowhere but in its facets and in how they relate.

That is where SPLectrum starts: not from what the whole is, but from how we reach it, how everything relates to everything else. Those relations can be put into language, and that is the way we come to know more. And "we" is meant broadly. Anything that is relates to what is around it, and that relating is a language. A stone lying in the sun has one. So does a bacterium swimming towards food, comparing the level of a few seconds ago with the level arriving now. Bacteria signal to each other across a population, with a molecule that microbiologists themselves call a common language. A village has its customs. The languages of the sciences did not grow outside nature either. They belong to the same web, made by people who are part of nature. Nothing in it stands outside, and the human case is one region of the web, not its centre.

Put all those languages together, every one through which something comes to be known, and they cover the whole of nature, from the stars to the stone to the village. That whole is the field of a modern natural philosophy. Every language belongs to it, and none has a special place: not science, and not philosophy either. The inquiry is into how they relate and meet, and how together they grow into a web of growing complexity. It is natural the way the first inquiry was, with the whole as its object. It is modern in where it finds the whole: not in one description over everything, but in the relating of the many.

Seen from there, every field of inquiry is a voice among interrelated voices, each with equal standing in potential. A modern natural philosophy does not take over what they say. A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth. What it cannot do on its own is see what its neighbours see. Where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science. A view that holds the many together is the counterweight to the narrow ones, and what passes between them makes each specialism stronger.

The first inquirers wanted the whole. The whole is still there to be had: not as one philosophy on top, but as the many in contact.
