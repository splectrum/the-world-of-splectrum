Most enquiries into meaning begin by deciding where meaning could possibly be, and then look only there. Minds, or language users, or at a stretch animals with nervous systems — the boundary gets drawn early and quietly, and everything outside it is not so much ruled out as never considered. The question "does this mean anything to a rock?" is not usually answered in the negative. It is not usually asked.

Which is why I find one recent piece of physics interesting for a reason that has little to do with whether it succeeds.

[Artemy Kolchinsky and David Wolpert](/blog/2030/02/the-empty-chair/) set out in 2018 to define meaningful information in physical terms — to say which of the correlations a system carries with its surroundings actually matter to it, using nothing but the system's own dynamics. They could have tested it inside the usual boundary: a bacterium against a bird against a person, producing a gradient of sophistication among things everyone agrees have a stake in their own continuation.

They did not. Their three worked examples are a rock, a hurricane and a food-caching bird, and the same question is put to each without adjustment.

## The middle case does the work

It would be easy to read that as a rhetorical arrangement — two poles and something in between, for illustration. It is doing more than that.

A hurricane is dynamic. It maintains itself, it has a structure that persists while its matter passes through, it exists only because energy flows through it. On any intuitive ordering it belongs firmly on the interesting side of the line, with the living things rather than the stones. And on their measure it scores nearly nothing: its correlations with its surroundings are not what keeps it in being.

That result is what stops the axis being a boundary in disguise. If the gradient ran rock-nothing, hurricane-nothing, bird-everything, you would have a life/non-life line with extra steps. If it ran rock-nothing, hurricane-something, bird-more, you would have a complexity ordering. What you get instead cuts across both: the thing that is unmistakably dynamic and self-maintaining lands almost where the stone does, because whatever the measure is tracking, it is not aliveness and it is not dynamism. It is something else, which living things happen to have a great deal of.

You cannot get that result by deciding in advance where to look. It is available only because the question was put to everything.

## The same refusal, in a different medium

This is the move this site makes, and it is worth stating plainly rather than leaving as a resemblance.

SPLectrum takes language as the relational medium of everything that is in existence. Not a faculty installed in certain beings with the rest of the world outside it, but the medium in which any subject's reality is disclosed at all — with differences between subjects being differences of degree and of implementation rather than of kind. A bacterium and a person are not on opposite sides of a line with the interesting phenomena all on one side. They are at different places on a range, and where a thing sits on that range is a finding rather than a premise.

Two very different quantities, then. Kolchinsky and Wolpert's is thermodynamic: correlation bearing on persistence, which grips wherever something is at stake for a system's continuation. The position's is meaning made and shared, which grips where a subject is disclosing a world. The measures reach different things and neither translates into the other.

But the methodological decision underneath is identical, and it is the decision that matters. Do not install the boundary first. Run the measure across the range and see what the range says.

What this buys, in both cases, is that the boundaries become results. It is still entirely possible that the world divides sharply — that there really is a line, with meaning on one side of it and mere correlation on the other. But that would then be something the enquiry found, rather than the shape of the net it was caught in. And the alternative is a kind of question that cannot deliver a surprise: draw the line, look inside it, and report that everything interesting is within.

## Process, not thing

One consequence follows immediately, and it is the reason the universal approach is harder than it sounds.

If you ask whether something's correlations matter to its persistence, you have to say over what stretch of time. Their framework names the interval as one of its choices, and the examples show how much rests on it. The rock scores nothing over the interval one naturally gives a rock — but extend it to geological time, and its persistence turns out to depend heavily on water, temperature and chemistry. Contract it far enough, and the bird's knowledge of its caches stops bearing on whether it is alive at the end of the interval too.

The interval is not arbitrary, though, and the reason is that what is being evaluated is a *process* rather than a thing sitting there. A process that maintains itself has a rate — a cycle time, a turnover, whatever it does to go on being what it is. That rate is the interval. It is read off the system rather than chosen for it, and it is why a rock and a bird cannot be compared on the same clock without distorting both.

Which is the discipline the universal approach demands, and the thing that makes it more than a gesture of inclusiveness. You do not get to run one measure across everything by being generous about what counts. You get to do it by finding what, in each case, the process actually is — and then asking your question at the rate that process runs.
