In 2010 [Stephen Hawking](/positioning/persons/h/hawking/) and Leonard Mlodinow wrote in *The Grand Design*: "philosophy is dead. Philosophy has not kept up with modern developments in science, particularly physics." Others had said much the same. Steven Weinberg knew of no one in postwar physics "whose research has been significantly helped by the work of philosophers".

Philosophy as a discipline judged by what it does for physics. Some replies came from within physics itself. [Carlo Rovelli](/positioning/persons/r/rovelli/): "Those who deny the utility of philosophy, are doing philosophy." The scientists' sentiment is not new. In 1886 [Nietzsche](/positioning/persons/n/nietzsche/) was already writing against "the declaration of independence of the scientific man, his emancipation from philosophy".

But the negative opinion does not only reside with scientists. Philosophers have been announcing the end of philosophy for two hundred years.

[Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/) wanted philosophy to "lay aside the name of love of knowledge and be actual knowledge": an end by arriving.

[Marx](/positioning/persons/m/marx/): "You cannot abolish philosophy without making it a reality", where the German for abolish, *aufheben*, also means to keep and to raise: an end by realising.

[Carnap](/positioning/persons/c/carnap/) set out to overcome metaphysics by logical analysis, and asked what was left for philosophy: "only a method". An end by overcoming.

[Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/) called the independence of the sciences the end of philosophy in the sense of its completion, and added that "completion does not mean perfection": an end by completing.

[Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/), glimpsing the closure of an epoch of metaphysics: "I do not say the end."

Arrival, realisation, overcoming, completion, closure. Look at what it is that ends, almost always metaphysics. The end of philosophy as the court that judges every other kind of knowledge: what [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) spelled with a capital P, "the Platonic attempt to sift out the merely contingent and conventional truths from the Truths which were something more than that". Most of the announcements also name what carries on: positive philosophy for Comte, a method for Carnap, thinking for Heidegger, philosophy with a small p for Rorty.

Philosophy has been giving its subjects away for a long time. [Bertrand Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) made the point in 1912: "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science." Astronomy went that way, then psychology; and Newton's great work, he reminds us, was called "the mathematical principles of natural philosophy". Natural philosophy is called physics now.

Once a subject reaches what Hegel called actual knowledge, it leaves and takes a name of its own. But what has become clear is that it does not leave philosophy behind altogether. It keeps some at its foundations, a 'philosophy of'. The rest stays behind as a residue.

This was not yet in view when Russell wrote. In 1912 the subjects had gone, but the 'philosophy of' had not yet taken up its position. A general philosophy of science came first, in the 1930s, with the Vienna Circle and its journals: Carnap's method made into a discipline. The philosophies of the separate sciences came later, from the 1960s on, after Kuhn: philosophy of mind, of biology, and of physics as a field of its own. These are the years of Heidegger's end of philosophy and Derrida's closure of an epoch. By the time Hawking wrote, the philosophy of physics had been a specialism for a generation, and the replies came from inside it.

Residue makes it sound like what is left in the pan. But knowledge grows, and the quest for it keeps making specialisms. Each one sends questions back as it goes: what its terms mean, what its results show, how it joins up with the others. So the part of philosophy no specialism has claimed may itself grow, on feedback from the specialisms it gave away. Philosophy will always look as if it is losing, because its successes leave and take other names. What stays behind is not shrinking.

So, dead? The verdict needs a philosophy that stays put and loses its ground. The record shows one that kept changing what it was. That may be the better question to put to it: not whether it has ended, but what it has become.
