In 2002, three researchers at Lausanne did something with unusual thoroughness. Tatiana Giraud, Jes Pedersen and Laurent Keller sampled Argentine ants from thirty-three populations along the coasts of Italy, France, Spain and Portugal, and then put workers from every possible pairing of those populations together to see what happened. Over eleven hundred combinations, scored on a scale from *ignores the other* to *fights to the death*.

The result, [published in *PNAS*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC122904/), was that across some six thousand kilometres of coastline, aggression never occurred. Ants taken from Portugal and ants taken from Italy — separated by a distance no individual ant could cross in a hundred lifetimes, with no possibility of ever having met — antennated each other briefly and got on with it. The authors called it the largest cooperative unit ever recorded.

What makes this interesting is not tolerance. These ants are not peaceable. In the same study, workers from the *other* European supercolony — a smaller one on the Spanish east coast — were also introduced, and in ninety-eight per cent of those trials an ant died. Later work extended the test intercontinentally: [paired in Petri dishes](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/7kb7m85z), Californian ants met ants from Japan, from Europe, from New Zealand, and in fifty trials each, fought not once. Against a secondary Californian population, from the same state, they fought in forty-two out of fifty.

So the discrimination is exact. It simply does not track anything we would expect it to track — not distance, not acquaintance, not shared food, not nationality in any sense a human would recognise. Ants from different supercolonies keep killing each other after months on identical diets.

The mechanism is a smell. Each ant carries a profile of hydrocarbons on its cuticle, and that profile is largely genetically determined rather than acquired — which is why it stays uniform across a range no ant traverses, with no contact required to keep it synchronised. The chemical distance between populations predicts the aggression: measure the profiles, and you can predict the fights.

Here is where [Mark Moffett](/positioning/persons/m/moffett/) comes in, and where a biological finding turns into an argument about a word. In a 2012 paper in *Behavioral Ecology* titled, plainly, "Supercolonies of billions in an invasive ant: What is a society?", he argued that the supercolony is a single society — and that our reluctance to say so reveals an assumption we did not know we were making.

His definition: a society is a cooperative group whose members can distinguish members from outsiders and reject outsiders on that basis. Notice what is absent. Nothing about interaction. Nothing about proximity, or acquaintance, or a member ever encountering another. If recognition runs on a shared cue rather than on remembering a face, then two members will accept each other whether or not they have ever met, and whether they live a metre or a thousand kilometres apart. Distance is not a membership criterion, so distance cannot break the society. He calls these *anonymous* societies, and contrasts them with the individual-recognition societies most vertebrates run — dolphins, elephants, chimpanzees — which are capped at roughly a hundred members by the plain requirement that everyone remember everyone.

The paper was published as a forum piece, with commentaries, and the commentaries did not agree. Deborah Gordon, who has studied Argentine ants in California for years, holds that there is no functional supercolony at all — that the real units are nest clusters, defined by where ants and food actually travel, and that calling the whole range one colony describes a genetic relationship rather than a society. Philip Lester and Monica Gruber sided with her, and attacked Moffett's population threshold as unworkable: you cannot count ants. Andrew Suarez and Erin Suhr accepted the supercolony but rejected the numbers, since some single-nest species exceed a million workers too.

And the ants themselves have complicated the picture since. A third European supercolony was confirmed on Corsica in 2012. A 2022 study on the northwest Iberian coast found [lethal fights between workers nominally of the same supercolony](https://neobiota.pensoft.net/article/90852/) — and found aggression in autumn where spring assays showed none, meaning the season you run the test can change the answer you get. The seamless empire of 2002 is looking more like a mosaic.

What I find worth keeping is not a verdict on the ants. It is the shape of the disagreement. Nobody disputes the behaviour: the assays are the assays, and they have been replicated by independent groups on three continents. Gordon does not think the ants fight when Moffett says they do not. The disagreement is entirely about whether non-aggression plus a shared badge is *enough* to be called one society, given that ants and resources demonstrably do not circulate across the whole range.

That is a disagreement about a word, conducted by people who agree about the world. Which is not a criticism of either side — the word has to be settled before the question can even be asked, and settling it is the substance, not a preliminary to the substance. Moffett's definition makes the supercolony one society; Gordon's makes it many. Both definitions are coherent. Each licenses different research: his invites comparison across species that solved membership the same way, hers keeps attention on where things actually move.

The ants, meanwhile, are not participating in this. They are carrying a smell that certifies them as members of something no one of them will ever see the edge of, and they are getting on with it.
