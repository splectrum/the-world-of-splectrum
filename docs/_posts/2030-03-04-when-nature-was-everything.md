The first Greek thinkers had a name for what they were doing, and it was not philosophy. Plato's Socrates remembers it from his youth: "the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature", *historia peri physeos*, inquiry into nature. Its object was the whole. Looking back, [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) placed its beginning in wonder "about the changes of the moon and of the sun, about the stars and about the origin of the universe". Pythagoras, asked what human beings are born for, is said to have answered: "to be an observer of the sky".

The first split came with Socrates. As Cicero told it, he "was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil". Once life had a place of its own, nature was one part among others. Aristotle drew the map: "three speculative philosophies: mathematics, physics, and theology", with the practical philosophies of conduct beside them. Physics took the things that move.

For the next two thousand years, natural philosophy was something you read. In the universities its books were Aristotle's, the *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *On the Soul*, read with the commentators. [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) gave its subject as *ens mobile*, movable being, and noted that the *Physics* was also called *On Natural Hearing*, because it was handed down by way of teaching to listeners. Theology had a faculty of its own.

In the seventeenth century natural philosophy grew, and nature narrowed. [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/) made physics the trunk of the tree of philosophy, with metaphysics its roots and the other sciences its branches; Bacon called natural philosophy "the great mother of the sciences". Mathematics, a separate philosophy for Aristotle, moved inside it, and [Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/) called his book the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. At the same time [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) put tastes, odours and colours into the sensing body, and Descartes set the thinking thing apart from extended matter: "toute ma Physique n'est autre chose que Géométrie", all my physics is nothing but geometry. Collingwood later summed up the principle: "nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable". Not that the old world had gone. Discourse of God, Newton wrote, "does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy", and he spent [decades on alchemy](/blog/2026/11/the-last-of-the-magicians/).

Then it left. In the nineteenth century the name narrowed to physics: Thomson and Tait's *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1867) defined it as "the investigation of laws in the material world". The workers on nature took a name of their own, "scientist". [Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) gave the rule in 1912: "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science." Astronomy had gone that way, then psychology. What stayed with philosophy he called "the residue". In the twentieth century philosophy came back to the sciences from outside, as the philosophy of science, and then of biology and of physics.

Read as loss, this is the end of philosophy, announced again and again. Read as growth, it is the love of knowledge delivering: every science that left was a success. Nicholas Maxwell put the cost plainly: natural philosophy "died. It split into science on the one hand, and philosophy on the other". The inquiry that started as one is now many, each with its own name, its own methods and its own language. Does anything still hold them together?
