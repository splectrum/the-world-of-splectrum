In 1865 Rudolf Clausius needed a name for a quantity he had been working with for fifteen years, and he explained his choice in the paper rather than leaving it to posterity:

> since I think it is better to take the names of such quantities as these, which are important for science, from the ancient languages, so that they can be introduced without change into all the modern languages, I proposed to name the magnitude S the entropy of the body, from the Greek word ἡ τροπή, a transformation. I have intentionally formed the word entropy so as to be as similar as possible to the word energy, since both these quantities… are so nearly related to each other in their physical significance that a certain similarity in their names seemed to me advantageous.

Greek, so it would travel unchanged into every modern language. Shaped to rhyme with *energy*, deliberately, because the two belong together. It is a small masterpiece of naming, and the reasoning is entirely on the record.

Eighty-three years later, Claude Shannon needed a name for a quantity in his theory of communication — a measure of how surprising a message source is. He called it entropy. His formula turned out to be, character for character, the one Gibbs had written for statistical mechanics.

Why did he choose that word? The famous answer is that he did not: that John von Neumann told him to. In the standard telling, von Neumann advised him to call it entropy on two grounds — that the mathematics already carried the name, and that, as the punchline goes, "nobody knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage."

It is a wonderful story. It has been repeated in textbooks, popular science books and lecture courses for fifty years. It was put into print in *Scientific American* in 1971 by Myron Tribus and Edward McIrvine, and it has been travelling ever since.

In 1985, Robert Price interviewed Shannon and put it to him. Price had noticed something: Shannon's classified cryptography report of 1945 already used the word entropy, and Shannon had not been in contact with von Neumann then. The chronology left no room.

Shannon's answer, from the transcript:

> No, I don't think he did. … Now, I'm not sure where I got that idea, but I think I, somebody had told me that. But anyway, I think I can, I'm quite sure that it didn't happen between von Neumann and me.

Note what he is saying in the middle there. He is not merely denying the story. He is reporting that *someone told him the story about himself*, and that he may have picked it up from them and repeated it. Price identifies the someone as E. T. Jaynes — a physicist who, as it happens, spent his career arguing that thermodynamic entropy is really about information, which makes him an unusually motivated carrier of an anecdote linking the two.

The story had circulated back to its own supposed source, and its supposed source had accepted it.

Shannon does offer an alternative, and it is undramatic. He knew entropy from thermodynamics; "that goes way back," he says, not from a class but from reading. Which is to say: the most likely explanation is that a mathematician who had read some physics noticed his formula was one he had seen before and used the name that came with it. No epigram, no advice, no strategic advantage in debates.

I find this more interesting than a piece of trivia, for two reasons.

The first is what the anecdote has been doing all these years. It is not a neutral story. It says, in a form short enough to remember, that the two entropies are connected by a joke rather than by a physical fact — that Shannon borrowed a word to win arguments, and the resemblance is a naming accident dressed up. That is a *position* on one of the genuinely open questions in the foundations of physics, smuggled in as an amusing aside. Whether thermodynamic entropy and information entropy are the same quantity is disputed; the anecdote answers it in passing, and disarms the reader with a laugh so nobody checks.

The second is the shape of the failure. This is not a case of a story being embellished in transmission. It is a case of a story being *constructed*, attaching itself to a plausible pair of names, and then being fed back to one of them, who could not quite rule it out. Shannon's own denial is hedged — "I think I can, I'm quite sure" — because he is trying to recall what he was thinking forty years earlier, and the story has been in his head for most of that time. Memory is not a sealed archive. It takes deliveries.

There is a tidy contrast here, and I will let it stand as the ending. The man who named the thermodynamic quantity told us exactly why, in the paper, in words we can still read. The man who named the informational one never explained — and the explanation that grew to fill the gap turned out to be about a conversation that did not happen.
