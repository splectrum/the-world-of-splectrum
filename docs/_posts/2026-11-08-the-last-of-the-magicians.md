In 1936 [Isaac Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/)'s private papers were sold at auction. The economist [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes) bought about half of them, and came to a verdict of his own. "In the eighteenth century and since," he wrote, "Newton came to be thought of as the first and greatest of the modern age of scientists, a rationalist." He saw it differently: "Newton was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians, the last of the Babylonians and Sumerians, the last great mind which looked out on the visible and intellectual world with the same eyes as those who began to build our intellectual inheritance rather less than 10,000 years ago."

Newton was born on Christmas Day 1642 at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire, two months after his father died. As a student at Trinity he headed a notebook of questions in philosophy "Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas": Plato is a friend, Aristotle is a friend, truth is a greater friend. Then the plague sent him home. Fifty years later, in the middle of a quarrel with [Leibniz](/positioning/persons/l/leibniz/) over who had come first, he looked back: "in those days I was in the prime of my age for invention & minded Mathematicks & Philosophy more then at any time since." The calculus, his first discoveries about light, gravity reaching out to the moon: all of it, he said, in two plague years.

Alongside the public work, from 1669, he took up work he kept to himself. Over some thirty years he wrote and copied about a million words on alchemy. Humphrey Newton, his assistant in the years of the *Principia*, remembered six weeks each spring and autumn in the laboratory, "the ffire scarcely going out either Night or Day". What Newton was after, Humphrey could not tell: "What his Aim might be, I was not able to penetrate into but his Paine, his Diligence at those sett times, made me think, he aim'd at som thing beyond the Reach of humane Art & Industry". The theology was just as private. He did not hold the doctrine of the Trinity, set out his convictions in letters to his friend [Locke](/positioning/persons/l/locke/), and refused the last rites when he died.

In a tract from the early 1670s the old and the new sit in a single sentence: "Natures actions are either vegetable or purely mechanicall". The mechanical went into the *Principia* of 1687. Gravity is there, measured and universal, but he would not say what it was; its cause, he told Richard Bentley, "is what I do not pretend to know". Of his own part he wrote to Bentley too: "if I have done the publick any service this way 'tis due to nothing but industry & a patient thought".

The vegetable never left him. In his book on light, inertia is "a passive Principle", and "By this Principle alone there never could have been any Motion in the World": motion is kept going "by active Principles, such as are the cause of Gravity ... and the cause of Fermentation", and nature "seems delighted with Transmutations". Even the *Principia* ends with God. To speak of God "from the appearances of things", its last pages say, "does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy".

The alchemy, marked "not fit to be printed" when he died, waited two centuries for the sale. Keynes's phrase for the man he found there was one "with one foot in the Middle Ages and one foot treading a path for modern science". The new did not come from someone who had left the old behind. It came from someone still living in it, who kept the fire going in the same years he was writing down the laws.
