The posts on [Maxwell's demon](/blog/2026/11/maxwells-demon/) and on [what a part can do](/blog/2026/12/what-a-part-can-do/) followed the demon from a thought experiment to a network, in the field's own terms. This one reads them with SPLectrum, because the demon, looked at again, is like something SPLectrum has been talking about all along: a being in exchange with its surroundings, knowing something about them, and doing something with what it knows.

The simple demon first. It watches the molecules, holds what it has seen, and uses it to open a door at the right moment. Knowing is worth work. The looking can in principle be free; the bill comes when the memory has to be cleared to look again. A demon that never forgets cannot go on, and one that forgets has to pay.

Then the bacterium. *E. coli* swims up a food gradient by holding the level of a few seconds ago against the level now. The record of a few seconds ago is chemical: the methylation state of its receptors. Sosuke Ito and Takahiro Sagawa, working out the thermodynamics of this network, write that the methylation level plays "a similar role to the memory of Maxwell's demon". It takes in information about the cell's own signalling and feeds it back, and that keeps the signalling steady while the signal outside is noisy. What the bacterium gets from what it knows is not work, as the demon did, but robustness: it stays on course in a world that jitters. There is a bill here too, in a different place. In one account it is paid continuously, to keep the memory intact against the noise; where exactly it falls is still argued over.

And then the many demons. Inside the cell there is no single watcher. Parts take in information about other parts and act back on them, and in a network a part can be a demon to some of its neighbours and a molecule to others. What a part can do is then set by what passes between it and the rest: a matter of where it sits.

Read with SPLectrum, three things stand out.

The first is where the seed starts: [being implies language](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/being-implies-language/). To be is to be in interaction with your surroundings; a stone that held no shape against the weather would not be there. The bacterium's robustness is like that, made measurable. What it gains from what it knows is staying itself against what goes on around it.

The second is cognition. SPLectrum defines it as [the process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through experience](/seed/cognition/), and never mentions a brain. The bacterium's few seconds of chemical memory, held, compared and acted on, at a price, look like a small case of exactly that. The field that studies [cognition below the nervous system](/blog/2026/10/where-basal-cognition-and-splectrum-meet/) already treats it as its textbook floor. What the physics adds is that this small cognition has a cost and a return, and both can be counted.

The third is knowledge itself. The physics speaks of information: a correlation between one part and another, measured in bits. We read the information that passes between the parts as knowledge. Not knowledge in an observer's head, which is how the word usually enters the argument about entropy, but a relation between parts that is there whether anyone looks or not, and that does something.

The demon was imagined as a being that could cheat the second law. It turned out to be a being that knows, and pays for knowing. Read SPLectrum's way, that looks a lot like a living thing: something that holds a little of its surroundings in itself, uses it to keep going, and pays to keep it fresh.
