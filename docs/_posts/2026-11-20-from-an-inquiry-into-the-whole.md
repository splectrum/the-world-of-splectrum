The first Greek thinkers called their activity inquiry into nature, *historia peri physeos*. Its object was the whole. It had no parts yet, and it was not even called philosophy; that name came later. Plato's Socrates remembers it from his youth: "the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature". Looking back, [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) placed the beginning of the inquiry in wonder "about the changes of the moon and of the sun, about the stars and about the origin of the universe". Pythagoras, when asked what human beings are born for, is said to have answered: "to be an observer of the sky".

It did not stay whole for long. The first split came with Socrates. As Cicero told it, he "was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil". With life having a place of its own, nature was one part among others. Aristotle drew the map: "three speculative philosophies: mathematics, physics, and theology", with the practical philosophies of conduct beside them. Physics took the things that move: the philosophy of nature, *physis*, or in Latin, natural philosophy.

For the next two thousand years, natural philosophy was something you read. In the universities its books were Aristotle's, the *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *On the Soul*, read with the commentators. [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) gave its subject as *ens mobile*, movable being.

In the seventeenth century natural philosophy extended its reach and branched. [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/) made physics, as natural philosophy was also called, the trunk of the tree of philosophy, with metaphysics its roots and the other sciences its branches, medicine, mechanics and morals; the fruit, he wrote, is gathered "only from the ends of the branches". Bacon called natural philosophy "the great mother of the sciences", and Locke could already name the "master-builders" of the sciences one by one: Boyle, Sydenham, Huygens, Newton. Mathematics, a separate philosophy for Aristotle, moved inside it, and [Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/) called his book the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*.

As its reach extended, its focus narrowed. [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) put tastes, odours and colours into the sensing body, and Descartes set the thinking thing apart from extended matter and wrote that "my whole physics is nothing other than geometry". Collingwood later summed up the principle: "nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable". Not that the old world had gone. Discourse of God, Newton wrote, "does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy", and he spent [decades on alchemy](/blog/2026/11/the-last-of-the-magicians/).

Then in the nineteenth century the scope of physics narrowed: Thomson and Tait's *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1867) defined natural philosophy as "the investigation of laws in the material world". Much of what it had stood for went elsewhere: astronomy, cosmology, biology, psychology. Each facet is still pursued but by a science of its own. [William Whewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whewell) saw the unity going in a review of Mary Somerville in 1834: "physical science itself is endlessly subdivided, and the subdivisions insulated". There he reported that the members of the British Association had "no general term" for what they did, and that someone had proposed "scientist".

With natural philosophy now meaning physics, the all-encompassing reach had gone. The sciences took up its facets without thinking of them as one, and much of what kept the word philosophy stayed with the old ambition: to say what reality is, and to say it with certainty. The scientific method was to make that good: one language, one method, one world, as the Vienna Circle's unity of science still had it. But the focus had turned to language, and there, from [Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) on, it became clear that the certainty could not be carried through logically. The specialisms were proliferating and absolutism was failing. "[Philosophy is dead](/blog/2026/11/the-end-of-philosophy-really/)", [Stephen Hawking](/positioning/persons/h/hawking/) and Leonard Mlodinow wrote in 2010, though many philosophers go on positing. What failed is what [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) spelled with a capital P, philosophy as judge and jury over every other kind of knowledge. What was natural philosophy goes on, scattered across many sciences that hardly remember their common origin. Does anything still hold them together?
