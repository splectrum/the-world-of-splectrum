The first Greek thinkers had a name for their work: inquiry into nature, *historia peri physeos*. Its object was the whole. It had no parts yet, and it was not even called philosophy; that name came later. Plato's Socrates remembers it from his youth: "the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature". Looking back, [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) placed its beginning in wonder "about the changes of the moon and of the sun, about the stars and about the origin of the universe". Pythagoras, asked what human beings are born for, is said to have answered: "to be an observer of the sky".

It did not stay whole for long. The first split came with Socrates. As Cicero told it, he "was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil". Once life had a place of its own, nature was one part among others. Aristotle drew the map: "three speculative philosophies: mathematics, physics, and theology", with the practical philosophies of conduct beside them. Physics took the things that move.

For the next two thousand years, natural philosophy was something you read. In the universities its books were Aristotle's, the *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *On the Soul*, read with the commentators. [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) gave its subject as *ens mobile*, movable being, and noted that the *Physics* was also called *On Natural Hearing*, because it was handed down by way of teaching to listeners.

In the seventeenth century natural philosophy extended its reach and branched. [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/) made physics the trunk of the tree of philosophy, with metaphysics its roots and the other sciences its branches, medicine, mechanics and morals; the fruit, he wrote, is gathered "only from the ends of the branches". Bacon called natural philosophy "the great mother of the sciences", and Locke could already name the "master-builders" of the sciences one by one: Boyle, Sydenham, Huygens, Newton. Mathematics, a separate philosophy for Aristotle, moved inside it, and [Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/) called his book the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*.

As its reach extended, its focus narrowed. [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) put tastes, odours and colours into the sensing body, and Descartes set the thinking thing apart from extended matter and wrote that "my whole physics is nothing other than geometry". Collingwood later summed up the principle: "nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable". Not that the old world had gone. Discourse of God, Newton wrote, "does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy", and he spent [decades on alchemy](/blog/2026/11/the-last-of-the-magicians/).

Then in the nineteenth century the name narrowed to physics: Thomson and Tait's *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1867) defined it as "the investigation of laws in the material world". The name stayed with one science. Much of what it had stood for went elsewhere. The changes of the moon and the sun went to astronomy, the origin of the universe to cosmology, what lives to biology, the soul to psychology. Each facet of the first inquiry is still pursued, harder than ever, by a science of its own. What thinned out was the place where they met. [William Whewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whewell) saw it going in a review of Mary Somerville in 1834: "physical science itself is endlessly subdivided, and the subdivisions insulated". There he reported that the members of the British Association had "no general term" for what they did, and that someone had proposed "scientist".

With the name gone, it was forgotten that there was a project at all. The sciences took up its facets without thinking of them as one, and much of what kept the word philosophy stayed with the old ambition: to say what reality is, and to say it with certainty. The scientific method was to make that good: one language, one method, one world, as the Vienna Circle's unity of science still had it. But the focus had turned to language, and there, from [Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) on, it became clear that the certainty could not be carried through logically. The specialisms had gone, absolutism was failing, and it felt as if the whole thing was falling apart. "[Philosophy is dead](/blog/2026/11/the-end-of-philosophy-really/)", [Stephen Hawking](/positioning/persons/h/hawking/) and Leonard Mlodinow wrote in 2010, though many philosophers go on positing. What failed is what [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) spelled with a capital P, philosophy as judge and jury over every other kind of knowledge. The project of natural philosophy goes on, in many sciences, without the room they used to share, and mostly without knowing that it is one. Does anything still hold them together?
