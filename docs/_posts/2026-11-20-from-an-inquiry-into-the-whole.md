The first Greek thinkers had a name for their work: inquiry into nature, *historia peri physeos*. Its object was the whole. It had no parts yet, and it was not even called philosophy; that name came later. Plato's Socrates remembers it from his youth: "the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature". Looking back, [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) placed its beginning in wonder "about the changes of the moon and of the sun, about the stars and about the origin of the universe". Pythagoras, asked what human beings are born for, is said to have answered: "to be an observer of the sky".

It did not stay whole for long. The first split came with Socrates. As Cicero told it, he "was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil". Once life had a place of its own, nature was one part among others. Aristotle drew the map: "three speculative philosophies: mathematics, physics, and theology", with the practical philosophies of conduct beside them. Physics took the things that move.

For the next two thousand years, natural philosophy was something you read. In the universities its books were Aristotle's, the *Physics*, *On the Heavens*, *On the Soul*, read with the commentators. [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) gave its subject as *ens mobile*, movable being, and noted that the *Physics* was also called *On Natural Hearing*, because it was handed down by way of teaching to listeners. Theology had a faculty of its own.

In the seventeenth century natural philosophy extended its reach, and that sped up a branching that had been going on for centuries: new centres formed within it, and parts of it began to gather around them. [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/) made physics the trunk of the tree of philosophy, with metaphysics its roots and the other sciences its branches, medicine, mechanics and morals; the fruit, he wrote, is gathered "only from the ends of the branches". Bacon called natural philosophy "the great mother of the sciences", and Locke could already name the "master-builders" of the sciences one by one: Boyle, Sydenham, Huygens, Newton. Mathematics, a separate philosophy for Aristotle, moved inside it, and [Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/) called his book the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. At the same time its focus narrowed. [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) put tastes, odours and colours into the sensing body, and Descartes set the thinking thing apart from extended matter: "toute ma Physique n'est autre chose que Géométrie", all my physics is nothing but geometry. Collingwood later summed up the principle: "nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable". Not that the old world had gone. Discourse of God, Newton wrote, "does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy", and he spent [decades on alchemy](/blog/2026/11/the-last-of-the-magicians/).

Then it left. In the nineteenth century the name narrowed to physics: Thomson and Tait's *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1867) defined it as "the investigation of laws in the material world". The name stayed with one science. What it had stood for went elsewhere.

The intent did not stop. The changes of the moon and the sun went to astronomy, the origin of the universe to cosmology, what lives to biology, the soul to psychology. Each facet of the first inquiry is still pursued, harder than ever, by a science of its own. What thinned out was the place where they met. [William Whewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whewell) saw it going in 1834: "physical science itself is endlessly subdivided, and the subdivisions insulated". In the same review he reported that the members of the British Association had "no general term" for what they did, and that someone had proposed "scientist".

Nicholas Maxwell says natural philosophy "died". The name may have. In 2010 [Stephen Hawking](/positioning/persons/h/hawking/) and Leonard Mlodinow opened a book by asking "Where did all this come from?". "Traditionally these are questions for philosophy," they wrote, "but [philosophy is dead](/blog/2026/11/the-end-of-philosophy-really/)." That is the wonder Aristotle described, asked from the science that kept the name.

What has died may be something else: what [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) spelled with a capital P, philosophy as judge and jury over every other kind of knowledge. The turn to facets, each with its own languages, left no seat for such a judge. The scientific method that was to unify everything only sped that along, each success making another specialism. It may have ended the named field, natural philosophy at the head of the sciences. It has not ended the project of natural philosophy. That goes on, in many places, without the room they used to share. Does anything still hold them together?
