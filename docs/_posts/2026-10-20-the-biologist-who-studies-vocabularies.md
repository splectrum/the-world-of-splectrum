In 2000, a field biologist published a paper in *Biotropica* titled "What's 'up'? A critical look at basic terms of canopy biology." It is not about a species or a site. It is about the word *canopy*, and a handful of others like it — and about the fact that ecologists, botanists, zoologists, anthropologists and aerodynamics researchers all use them while carving the forest's vertical space differently. The author, [Mark Moffett](/positioning/persons/m/moffett/), had spent years in that canopy with a camera and a notebook, and had come to think the field's vocabulary was doing quiet work nobody had examined — and that examining it was itself a scientific task.

That is an unusual thing for a field biologist to write. It is also, I think, more interesting than the claim he is famous for.

The famous claim is that human societies are, in their organisation, more like certain ant societies than like the troops of our nearest relatives — both having solved the same problem, cooperation among anonymous strangers at scale, by different means: colony scent in the one case, and in ours the whole constellation of language, dress, ritual and flag that lets strangers read each other as members. It is contested, properly and in print, and I have no verdict to offer in that argument.

What draws me is how he holds it. Not "ants are like us," not even "we are like ants," but something narrower and harder: two different mechanisms producing the same organisational solution, with the comparison *structural, not psychological*. He is not claiming ants think as we do. He is claiming that if you look at how a society is held together rather than at what it feels like from inside, the same shape turns up in two places that share no history of it.

That is analogy with the difference kept in view, and it is a rarer discipline than it sounds. Most cross-domain comparison either collapses into identity — *the brain is a computer*, and then people forget the *is like* — or gets abandoned as loose talk. Holding the middle takes constant work: the resemblance stated precisely enough to be useful, the difference stated clearly enough that nobody mistakes the one for the other.

The same discipline shows up in how he handles definitions. His definition of a society is offered as a *neutral descriptor*, and he is explicit that this is deliberate: it is not a definition's job, on his view, to explain the phenomenon it names, and he words it to keep assumptions about function from creeping in. Some readers find that unsatisfying and want a definition to do explanatory work. But the stance is the canopy paper's stance: a term is a mark you make in order to see, not an essence you have discovered, and what it earns you is what it lets you notice next.

And it does earn. The marker mechanism let him notice something I keep turning over: in the evolutionary shift from recognising faces to reading badges, the ingroup/outgroup boundary itself passed through unaltered. The means of drawing the line changed completely. The line did not.

I have written elsewhere on this site about comparing different vocabularies without one being made to swallow the other, and about treating definitions as chosen tools rather than discovered essences — commitments I arrived at from the philosophical side, working on how meaning gets shared. Moffett reached his from the other end entirely: from thirty years in canopies and colonies, from having to write down what "up" means when five disciplines disagree, from needing a definition of "society" that would let him put an ant supercolony and a nation-state on the same page without claiming they are the same thing. Two routes with nothing in common, and the working discipline at the end of them rhymes.

There is a difference I have not mentioned, and it is a real one. A marker is a test: match and you are in, fail and you are out — near-absolute in ants, considerably looser in us. Whether human belonging is well described by a badge and a threshold, or whether it is graded and multiple all the way down, is a genuine question his framework raises without settling. That deserves its own treatment rather than a paragraph here.

For now, the smaller observation is the one worth keeping. A biologist who spends his life photographing ants stops, at one point, to write a paper about a preposition — not as a digression from the science, but as part of it.
