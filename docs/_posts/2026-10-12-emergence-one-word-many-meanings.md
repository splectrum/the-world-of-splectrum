There is a word many researchers dread — and they say so in print. The physicists Sophia and Steven Kivelson, [introducing an attempt to define it properly](https://www.nature.com/articles/npjquantmats201624), call it "appealingly intuitive but problematically ill-defined," and observe that it sometimes appears simply as a colloquial signal that a property is "newsworthy and highly fundable." A [recent survey of the concept](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04951) puts the complaint more bluntly: lacking consensus on a definition, "authors often cloak conceptual gaps with terms that sound profound but remain undefined." And when [NASA's astrobiology programme convened a workshop on the concept](https://astrobiology.nasa.gov/news/all-about-emergence/), more than a hundred scientists spent four days presenting, debating and — not infrequently — disagreeing about what it meant. The word is *emergence*: dreaded because it arrives trailing a century of confusion, and using it invites arguments you did not mean to start.

And yet the word will not go away, because it does daily work in a remarkable number of places. A condensed-matter physicist uses it for what happens when matter near a critical point forgets its microscopic details. An ant researcher uses it for the colony's competence that no ant has. A sociologist uses it for institutions that outlive and constrain every member. An AI lab uses it for abilities that seem to appear suddenly as models grow. A philosopher of mind uses it for the relation between brain and experience. And here is the fact underneath the dread: they do not mean the same thing by it.

The history explains some of this. The word entered philosophy in 1875, when George Henry Lewes needed a term for effects that could not be traced as the sum of their causes — John Stuart Mill had described the phenomenon three decades earlier using chemistry as his example: the properties of a compound are nothing like the properties of its elements. A British school built on it in the 1920s — Samuel Alexander, C. Lloyd Morgan, and most carefully [C. D. Broad](/positioning/persons/b/broad/), who held that some properties of wholes cannot be derived, even in principle, from full knowledge of their parts. Chemistry was their confident example. Then quantum mechanics arrived and began deriving chemical bonding, and the school's stock fell with its example — though philosophers of chemistry still argue, today, about how complete that derivation really is. For decades the word was nearly retired. It returned — through the sciences of complexity, separately through the philosophy of mind for consciousness, and in 2022 through AI, in a dispute about scaling curves that is still running.

So the word now lives in many homes, and in each it has a precise local meaning. In physics: behaviour insensitive to micro-detail, with a mathematical account of *why* the details wash out. In complexity science: what your simulation shows that its rules don't mention — derivable, but only by running it. In biology: what life does at each new scale of organisation. In sociology, the oldest working usage of all: Durkheim's social facts, exterior to every individual and constraining each, a full working concept by 1895. The reference library now carries the full catalogue — [several dozen named types with their definitions](/positioning/subjects/e/emergence/the-types/) — and a census of [the overviews the field has produced of itself](/positioning/subjects/e/emergence/the-overviews/), which shows the same pattern from above: mostly philosophy's overviews, with the sciences at their edges.

The field's own surveyors are candid about the situation. Paul Humphreys, in [the Oxford handbook chapter on emergence](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/files/paul-humphreys/files/emergence_8.pdf), states plainly that a unified definition of emergence is not currently feasible, and — the sharper observation — that unlike causation, the field has no agreed set of core examples against which any proposed account could be tested. Different communities, each with a locally exact notion, no settled common one.

It is tempting to read this as somebody's failure. I don't. It reads, to me, as a fact about words and the contexts they get their meaning in. Each discipline built its sense of "emergence" inside its own language — its own instruments, cases, and standards — and within that context each sense is exact and earns its keep daily. What has not happened is the next step: the senses converging across contexts into one shared meaning, the way "energy" eventually did across mechanics, chemistry and biology — the mid-nineteenth-century convergence of heat, work and chemical energy under conservation. That step is neither automatic nor guaranteed; it is its own achievement, and for this word it has not yet been achieved.

Which leaves a small observation to end on, offered as a fact about language rather than a complaint about anyone. Emergence is the word communities use for something new arising from many parts. As a meaning, across its many communities, that has not yet happened to the word itself.
