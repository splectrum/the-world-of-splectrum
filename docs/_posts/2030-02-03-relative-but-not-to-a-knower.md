Most people who use the word can offer a definition without pausing: entropy is disorder. Or, from a slightly different crowd: entropy is missing information. Both are said with confidence, and each takes a side in a dispute that physicists and philosophers of physics have been having for a century and have not settled. [Where the word came from](/blog/2026/10/the-word-travels/) was an earlier post; this one is about what it names.

**Entropy is missing information** is the epistemic reading, and its most forceful advocate was E. T. Jaynes. In 1957 he argued that statistical mechanics is a form of inference rather than a physical theory — the probabilities express what we know about a system, not a feature of the system — and he called the result "subjective statistical mechanics", with the scare quotes his own. Entropy, on this reading, measures what you lack: how far the macroscopic description leaves the microstate undetermined. The attraction is real. It explains why a method that commits to nothing about the actual microscopic state predicts macroscopic behaviour so well, and it makes the match with Shannon's formula natural rather than a coincidence, since both measure uncertainty.

**Entropy is disorder** is the objective reading — Boltzmann's line — and its defenders are not shy. Joel Lebowitz put the case in *Physics Today* in 1993; David Albert in *Time and Chance*; and Sheldon Goldstein, Lebowitz, Roderich Tumulka and Nino Zanghì, in a 2019 paper defending the Boltzmann entropy, state the ground plainly: it is a function on phase space, defined for an individual system, not for a distribution someone writes down. The objection to the epistemic reading is equally plain, and Albert and Barry Loewer both press it: ice melts in warm water whether or not anyone knows the microstate, and an explanation that runs through what we happen to know cannot be what makes it melt. Goldstein and his co-authors put it as a run of questions — in the absence of observers, does heat flow from the cooler to the hotter? In distant stars? Before humans existed? After the human race becomes extinct? — and if not, why would observers be relevant to the explanation at all.

They have a case that sharpens it. An isolated room contains a battery-powered heater that may or may not have been switched on, and nobody knows which. If entropy tracks ignorance, a fifty-fifty credence spreads over two macroscopically different situations and gives a value between them. But the room's entropy is one value or the other; it is never the average. Install a coin-tossing mechanism to decide whether the heater runs, so that the uncertainty is in the world rather than in a head, and the distribution is the same — and the intermediate number is still wrong.

A reply is available, and its ground is Jaynes's own. In 1965 he wrote that entropy is a property not of the physical system but of the particular experiments you or I choose to perform on it — an anthropomorphic concept, he said, even at the phenomenological level. Read that way, the heater case spreads credence across two experiments, and a properly posed problem conditions on the macrostate actually prepared; the intermediate number marks a badly framed question rather than a fault in the method. As for the empty room: the theory says what any observer with the given information would find on looking, whether or not anyone looks. Whether that answers the objection or restates the disagreement is what the argument consists of, and it has not closed.

Which is why a third voice interests me more than the stand-off. Carlo Rovelli proposes that entropy is relative — but to a physical coupling, not to a knower. Entropy depends on a coarse-graining, on which variables count as macroscopic; everyone grants that. His point is that the choice is not ours: "A cup of hot tea does not cool down because of what I know or do not know about its molecules." The macroscopic variables are dictated by the ways the system couples to what is around it. His example is a volume of air. It is not our abstract knowledge of the ratio of oxygen to nitrogen that matters, but whether that ratio is physically coupled to anything outside — a piston with a filter, say — and different couplings give different entropies, each of them real. The quantity that results he calls objective but relative to the system it is relative to. He is careful about how far he takes it: the perspectival arrow of time he builds on top he marks as speculative and open-ended, and the coupling point stands without it.

Katie Robertson and Carina Prunkl reach similar ground from thermodynamics itself. The features that look anthropocentric, they argue, point not to subjectivity but to a relativity to the physical means of manipulation available — what can actually be done to a system, which is a fact about the coupling rather than about anyone's beliefs. Their word is *resource-relative*, and the subjective gloss falls away from it.

This is a third voice, not a verdict; the dispute has two serious positions and a hundred years of argument, and I am not refereeing it. What the coupling move does is undo a pairing that had gone unexamined. *Relative* had been heard as *subjective*, and the objective side's best objections — the empty room, the distant stars — are objections to subjectivity, not to relativity. Relative to what a system is coupled to is as far from a knower as a thermometer is.

So the two glosses are not exactly wrong. Each names a real position held by serious people. But each is a *position*, offered as though it were a definition, and a reader who picks one up casually has joined a side without noticing there was one. Several entropies, related by more than coincidence and less than identity — and whether they are one quantity under different descriptions is a question still open.
