Much of the talk about artificial intelligence treats it as something arriving from outside: a new species, a rival, possibly a successor. [Sara Imari Walker](/positioning/persons/w/walker/), a physicist and astrobiologist at Arizona State University, puts it the other way round. "Technology is not artificially replacing life," she wrote in 2023, "— it is life."

The claim rests on how she uses the words. The [post on life and alive](/blog/2026/11/life-alive-or-what/) took her split from the same essay. By life she means "all objects that can only be produced in our universe through a process of evolution and selection". Being alive is "the active implementation of the dynamics of evolution and selection". A dead cat is life but no longer alive. So is a chair, a book, a phone: none of them could exist unless evolution had first produced something able to make them.

From there the rest follows. "Human technology would not exist without humans, so it is therefore part of the same ancient lineage of information that emerged with the origin of life." Four years earlier, with Michael Lachmann, she had put it in one line: "anything we create is part of our own lineage, and therefore represents the same life." The unit is not the individual organism but the lineage. "We are lineages, not individuals."

[Assembly theory](/positioning/subjects/a/assembly-theory/), developed with Lee Cronin's group in Glasgow, is the formal side of this: a measure of how many steps an object takes to build, and of how many copies of it exist. Objects that are hard to make and turn up many times point to selection. In the first measurements on molecules the team found that all the high-scoring ones came from "living (or technological) processes". The measure has its critics, on minerals that score high and on whether it is new at all, but the lineage view does not hang on the threshold. Technology sits on the life side from the start.

Walker is direct about the fear. "This fear of the artificiality of technology misses the potentially far-reaching role technologies may play in the evolutionary trajectories of living worlds." In a long conversation in 2024 she said: "We're really good at identifying things as other. We're not really good at understanding when we're the same." What we live in, she calls the technosphere: "a system that is both human and technological. It's not human or technological."

Read this way, technology is part of the body. That is less strange than it sounds. The body has never been only our own cells: the bacteria we carry are about as many as the cells we are made of, and we could not digest, develop or defend ourselves without them. They are part of us, and like everything else that makes up the body they need managing. Technology is like that. Managing it well is not border control. It is looking after a part of ourselves.

Her split also takes the edge off the talk of superintelligence. Walker does not say AI is alive. In her terms it is life: made by the process, not running it. What a large language model knows, it has mined from what we already know. It may be superhuman on that record, on the past, but the record is ours. To become super apart from us it would need the other side, being alive: its own evolution and selection, out in the world, open-ended. Nothing like that has been realised.

Walker ends her essay on the future: "life is what we are evolving into." Technology is part of that. Not a species at the gate, but the latest of our own extensions, made from our record, and ours to look after.
