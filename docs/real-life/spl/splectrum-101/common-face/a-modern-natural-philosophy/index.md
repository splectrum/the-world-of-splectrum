# A Modern Natural Philosophy

The first Greek thinkers had a name for their work: inquiry into nature, *historia peri physeos*. Its object was the whole: the moon, the sun and the stars, the origin of the universe. It had no parts yet. It was not even called philosophy; that name came later.

It has been delivering ever since. As the love of knowledge started to yield actual knowledge, the inquiry formed units, and the units grew to independence. Aristotle already counted physics as one part of philosophy, beside mathematics and theology. Natural philosophy kept its name for some two thousand years (Newton's great book is the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*) and then left philosophy as the sciences, each under a name of its own. Russell put the rule plainly in 1912: as soon as definite knowledge of a subject becomes possible, it "ceases to be called philosophy". Read as loss, this is the end of philosophy, announced again and again. Read as growth, it is philosophy's yield. The story is told in [Natural Philosophy in Evolution](/positioning/wider-landscape/natural-philosophy-in-evolution/).

Each unit took languages with it and made them its own. Physics speaks the languages of mathematics; chemistry has its formulas; biology, medicine and law have vocabularies built up over centuries for what each needs to say. A specialism is to a large extent a set of specialised languages: ways of expressing what its field works with. And this is not limited to the sciences. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life. The growth of knowledge has been a multiplication of languages.

What stayed behind with philosophy shrank with every departure. Russell called it the residue: knowing, meaning, value, the mind, the questions no science had taken. Its reach narrowed, and the one doing the knowing, put outside nature in the seventeenth century, stayed outside. The residue split into specialisms of its own, each with its own languages. In the twentieth century philosophy went back to the sciences, as the philosophy of science and then of physics, of biology, of mind, one science at a time. Each of these looks at one field without joining back into a whole, the core and the parts remaining apart.

A few voices have called for the whole to be taken up again, under its old name, and each sees the whole in its own way. For Nicholas Maxwell it is the universe with "ourselves as a part of it", which science and philosophy once studied as "one mutually interacting, integrated endeavour of natural philosophy" (2012). For Alister McGrath (2023) it is nature as something to learn from as well as about. For Roberto Mangabeira Unger and [Lee Smolin](/positioning/persons/s/smolin/) (2015) it is the one universe, in which time is real; natural philosophy, they write, "must turn into something else".

SPLectrum starts from another place: not from what the whole is, but from how we reach it, how everything relates to everything else. Those relations can be put into language, and that is the way we come to know more ([The Medium of Experience](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/the-medium-of-experience/)). And "we" is meant broadly. Anything that is relates to what is around it, and that relating is a language ([Being Implies Language](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/being-implies-language/)). A stone lying in the sun has one. So does a cell finding its way to food, a crowd of bacteria signalling to each other, and a village with its customs. The languages of the sciences did not grow outside nature either. They belong to the same web, made by people who are part of nature, in the same way. Nothing in it stands outside. The human case is one region of that web, not its centre.

Put all those languages together, every one through which something comes to be known, and they cover the whole of nature, the cosmos, from the stars to the stone to the village. That whole is the field of a modern natural philosophy. Every language belongs to it, and none has a special place: science has none, and neither has philosophy. The inquiry is into how they relate and meet, and how together they grow into a [web of growing complexity](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/web-of-complexity/). It is natural in the sense the first inquiry was, the whole as its object. It is modern in where it finds the whole: not in one description over everything, but in the relating of the many.

Seen from there, every field of inquiry is a voice among interrelated voices, each with [equal standing in potential](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/interrelational-equal-standing/). A modern natural philosophy does not take over what they say. A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth; what it cannot do on its own is see what its neighbours see. Where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science. A view that holds the many together is the counterweight to the narrow ones, and what passes between them makes each specialism stronger. Any voice can build its own philosophy on this ground; SPLectrum's own does, with [pillars](/reality/) of its own. That is the whole taken up again: not one philosophy on top, but the many in contact.
