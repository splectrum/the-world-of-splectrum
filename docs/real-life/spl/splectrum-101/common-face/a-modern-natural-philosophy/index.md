# A Modern Natural Philosophy

The first Greek thinkers had a name for their work: inquiry into nature, *historia peri physeos*. Its object was the whole: the moon, the sun and the stars, the origin of the universe. It had no parts yet. It was not even called philosophy; that name came later.

It has been delivering ever since. As the love of knowledge started to yield actual knowledge, the inquiry formed units, and the units grew to independence. Aristotle already counted physics as one part of philosophy, beside mathematics and theology. Natural philosophy kept its name for some two thousand years (Newton's great book is the *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*) and then left philosophy as the sciences, each under a name of its own. Russell put the rule plainly in 1912: as soon as definite knowledge of a subject becomes possible, it "ceases to be called philosophy". Read as loss, this is the end of philosophy, announced again and again. Read as growth, it is philosophy's yield. The story is told in [Natural Philosophy in Evolution](/positioning/wider-landscape/natural-philosophy-in-evolution/).

Each unit took a language with it and made it its own. Physics speaks mathematics; chemistry has its formulas; biology, medicine and law have vocabularies built up over centuries for what each needs to say. A specialism is to a large extent a specialised language: a way of expressing what its field works with, which the others do not need. The arts have theirs, and so does everyday life. The growth of knowledge has been a multiplication of languages.

Those languages did not grow outside nature. In SPLectrum's view, to be at all is to be in interaction with what surrounds you, and that interaction is a language ([Being Implies Language](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/being-implies-language/)). A stone holding its shape has one. So does a cell finding its way along a trace of food, and a population of bacteria signalling to each other, and a community with its customs. The language of a science belongs to the same web as theirs, made by subjects who are part of nature, in the same way. Nothing in it stands outside. The human case is one region of that web, not its centre.

That is where a modern natural philosophy finds its field. It takes nature in the old sense, all that is, and finds it already in language. The inquiry is into those languages: how they relate and meet, and how together they grow into a [web of growing complexity](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/web-of-complexity/). It is natural in the sense the first inquiry was, the whole as its object. It is modern in where it finds the whole: not in one description over everything, but in the relating of the many.

Seen from there, it underpins rather than competes. Each specialism is a language game grown up in a context of its own, with its own terms and its own work to do. A modern natural philosophy does not take over their content. It attends to the ground they share: the language each of them is made of. SPLectrum's own voice builds its philosophy on that ground, with [pillars](/reality/) of its own; so can any other voice.

That makes every field of inquiry, science included, something like a voice among interrelated voices, each with [equal standing in potential](/real-life/spl/splectrum-101/seed-principles/interrelational-equal-standing/). A specialism's narrow view is how it gets its depth; what it cannot do on its own is see what its neighbours see. And where languages meet, something new can start: cooking meets chemistry, and there is food science. A view that holds the many together is the counterweight to the narrow ones, and the cross-pollination it allows makes each specialism stronger. That is the whole taken up again: not one philosophy on top, but the many in contact.
