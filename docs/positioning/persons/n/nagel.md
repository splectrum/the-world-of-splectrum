# Thomas Nagel (1937–)

Nagel made the strongest case from within analytic philosophy that subjective experience cannot be eliminated. "What is it like to be a bat?" insists that the subject's experience is irreducible, inaccessible to an experiencer of a different kind, structurally first-person. His "view from nowhere" names the standpoint that no subject can actually occupy. Where [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) dissolved the view from nowhere by rejecting the question, Nagel took it seriously and argued that the tension between subjective and objective cannot be resolved by choosing one side.

[Thomas Nagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Nagel) (1937–). University Professor at NYU until his retirement in 2013, now emeritus; previously at Princeton. Named the 2026 laureate of the Berggruen Prize for Philosophy & Culture (announced 17 November 2026). One of the most widely read analytic philosophers. Working within the analytic tradition at a time when physicalism and functionalism dominated philosophy of mind, Nagel was the internal critic: he argued that the tradition had left out the most important thing. There is something it is *like* to be a conscious subject — and that something cannot be captured by any objective description.

## Key concepts

**What is it like to be a bat?** The question that made the point. A bat perceives the world through echolocation — there is something it is like to be that bat, to have that experience. No amount of knowledge about bat neurology, sonar physics, or behavioural data tells you what it is like from the inside. The barrier is not privacy: "I am not adverting here to the alleged privacy of experience to its possessor. The point of view in question is not one accessible only to a single individual. Rather it is a type." It lies between kinds of experiencer, human and bat. And the usual road to knowledge leads away from the experience. Objectivity proceeds by "reducing our dependence on individual or species-specific points of view", but applied to experience, any shift to greater objectivity "does not take us nearer to the real nature of the phenomenon: it takes us farther away from it". The paper closes with a proposal: "an objective phenomenology not dependent on empathy or the imagination", which would describe at least the structure of experience "in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences".

**The subjective–objective gap.** Objective knowledge proceeds by abstracting away from particular points of view. But consciousness *is* a point of view. In *Mind and Cosmos* (2012) the physical sciences "leave out something essential, without which there would be no mind. And what they leave out is just what was deliberately left out of the physical world by Descartes and Galileo in order to form the modern concept of the physical, namely, subjective appearances" (as quoted in H. Allen Orr's review, *New York Review of Books*, 2013).

**The reality of the subjective.** Nagel does not deny that consciousness depends on the brain. He denies that a complete physical description of the brain would constitute an explanation of consciousness. Something is left out — the subjective character of experience. His positive claim: a complete account of reality must include the subjective, not as an add-on to physics but as something physics in its current form cannot capture. Physicalism is not wrong about what it includes; it is incomplete about what it leaves out.

**The view from nowhere.** The book of that title (1986) opens on "a single problem: how to combine the perspective of a particular person inside the world with an objective view of that same world, the person and his viewpoint included". Objectivity is not a place but a method: one steps back from one's initial view and forms "a new conception which has that view and its relation to the world as its object… The process can be repeated, yielding a still more objective conception." It comes in degrees — a view is more objective "if it relies less on the specifics of the individual's makeup and position in the world" — and reality appears as "a set of concentric spheres, progressively revealed as we detach gradually from the contingencies of the self". The capacity to step back is the *objective self*, which each person has, "developed to different degrees in different persons and at different stages of life and civilization" ("The Objective Self", 1983). The full view from nowhere is never reached: it is the ideal the pursuit of objectivity aims at, not a standpoint anyone occupies. And the method has limits built in: "Realism underlies the claims of objectivity and detachment, but it supports them only up to a point."

**The last word.** Nagel extends the argument from consciousness to reason itself, in logic, science and ethics alike. *The Last Word* (1997) argues that reason has an authority "not merely personal, or societal, but universal". Its validity "is unconditional because it is necessarily employed in every purported challenge to itself": there are "some thoughts which we cannot get outside of". The title names the dispute — whether "the last word in philosophical disputes about the objectivity of any form of thought" lies with our language and practices, or with "unqualified thoughts about how things are". Nagel gives it to the second. His targets are a general tendency he calls subjectivism, found in [Hume](/positioning/persons/h/hume/), [Kant](/positioning/persons/k/kant/), [Wittgenstein](/positioning/persons/w/wittgenstein/) "on a common reading", [Quine](/positioning/persons/q/quine/), [Goodman](/positioning/persons/g/goodman/), [Putnam](/positioning/persons/p/putnam/), [Bernard Williams](/positioning/persons/w/williams-b/), and [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/), whom he answers at length. The book ends: "If we think at all, we must think of ourselves, individually and collectively, as submitting to the order of reasons rather than creating it."

**Objectivity and intersubjectivity.** Nagel gives agreement a place, but downstream. "Because a centerless view of the world is one on which different persons can converge, there is a close connection between objectivity and intersubjectivity" ("The Objective Self"). Agreement is not what makes a view objective, since even what is most subjective can be agreed on ("Subjective and Objective", 1979); consensus is an effect of reasoning, not its ground: "where consensus is available, it arises from the convergence among different individuals, all reasoning to get at the truth" (*The Last Word*). Consensus alone is "the wrong kind of generality — since at its outer bounds it is statistical, not rational". In *Mind and Cosmos* the growth of objective thought "has become a collective cultural process as well", while an individual's correct reasoning stays correct "even independent of the community of thinkers to which he belongs".

**Teleological naturalism.** *Mind and Cosmos* — subtitled "Why the Materialist Neo-Darwinian Conception of Nature Is Almost Certainly False" — argues that nature has an inherent tendency toward the development of consciousness. Not design, not religion, but a teleology built into the natural order. The book was explosively controversial: materialists rejected the critique, creationists tried to claim him, fellow philosophers were divided. Nagel accepted none of the appropriations.

**On artificial intelligence.** *Mind and Cosmos* already assumed "that the attribution of knowledge to a computer is a metaphor". In an interview with Steven Levy for *Wired* (18 November 2026), Nagel called the readiness of some philosophers to take seriously whether language models are conscious "just ridiculous": it assumes "that the physical substrate is irrelevant", while consciousness more likely requires "something analogous to a nervous system", and "language by itself is not a natural manifestation of consciousness". He did not exclude conscious life forms physically very different from us, but judged it "premature" to think such a thing can now be made: "We know so little about how this happens, even in the human brain."

## Where Nagel stops

Nagel holds the subjective–objective tension as permanent. The view from nowhere cannot be reached, yet we cannot stop striving toward it; the subjective cannot be captured objectively, yet it cannot be dismissed. His programme stops at the holding — maintaining both sides of the tension in full force rather than resolving it in either direction.

## Key works

- [*"What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Is_It_Like_to_Be_a_Bat%3F) (1974) — the essay that framed the problem
- *Mortal Questions* (1979) — essays on death, the absurd, moral luck, and the subjective
- [*The View from Nowhere*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_View_from_Nowhere) (1986) — subjective and objective, the tension at the heart of philosophy
- *The Last Word* (1997) — reason's authority, against subjectivism about reason
- [*Mind and Cosmos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_and_Cosmos) (2012) — against neo-Darwinian reductionism, the case for teleological naturalism
