# Isaac Newton (1642–1727)

Newton put natural philosophy in the title of his main work, *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, a title he changed to in 1686, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s account, in allusion to [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/)'s *Principia Philosophiae*. His laws of motion and universal gravitation came with a method that would admit only what could be drawn from the phenomena, and refused to "feign" hypotheses about causes it could not find. The same natural philosophy still held absolute space and time, argued from the world to "the very first Cause, which certainly is not mechanical", and counted discourse of God as part of its business.

[Isaac Newton](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/newton/) was born at Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, on 25 December 1642 by the old calendar, two months after his father's death. He went up to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661; his student notebook of questions in philosophy is headed "Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas", Plato is a friend, Aristotle is a friend, truth is a greater friend. In the plague years 1665–67, at Woolsthorpe, came his early optical discoveries, the inverse-square relation in circular motion and the calculus. Some fifty years later, in his dispute with Leibniz, he wrote: "All this was in the two plague years of 1665 & 1666. For in those days I was in the prime of my age for invention & minded Mathematicks & Philosophy more then at any time since." He became a fellow in 1667, built a reflecting telescope in 1669 and took the Lucasian chair of mathematics that October. His paper on light and colours (1672) drew disputes with Hooke and Huygens that made him withdraw from public exchanges in natural philosophy; from 1669 he worked privately on alchemy and theology. Halley's visit in 1684 led to *De motu corporum in gyrum* and then to the *Principia*, printed in 1687; of his own part he wrote to Bentley: "if I have done the publick any service this way 'tis due to nothing but industry & a patient thought" (1692). He sat as member of Parliament for the university in 1689–90, counted [Locke](/positioning/persons/l/locke/) among his friends, moved to London as Warden of the Mint in 1696 and Master from 1699, was President of the Royal Society from 1703 and was knighted in 1705. He quarrelled with Flamsteed, and with Leibniz over the calculus, which Newton had in 1665–66, "most of a decade before Leibniz did so independently, and ultimately more influentially", in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s words; the Royal Society's report of 1712, in which Newton was "a dominant figure", found for Newton. He died on 20 March 1727, having refused the last rites. His private papers, the alchemical ones marked "not fit to be printed" at his death, were sold at auction in 1936; John Maynard Keynes, who bought about half of them, set his own picture against the received one — "In the eighteenth century and since, Newton came to be thought of as the first and greatest of the modern age of scientists, a rationalist" — and called him "not the first of the age of reason" but "the last of the magicians, the last of the Babylonians and Sumerians, the last great mind which looked out on the visible and intellectual world with the same eyes as those who began to build our intellectual inheritance rather less than 10,000 years ago", a man "with one foot in the Middle Ages and one foot treading a path for modern science" ("Newton, the Man", read 1946).

## Key concepts

**The laws of motion.** "Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed thereon"; "The alteration of motion is ever proportional to the motive force impressed; and is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed"; "To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction" (*Principia*, Axioms, or Laws of Motion, tr. Motte). He called the force inherent in matter by a name of his own: it "may, by a most significant name, be called vis inertiæ, or force of inactivity" (Definition III). He credited [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) with part of it: "By the first two Laws and the first two Corollaries, Galileo discovered that the descent of bodies observed the duplicate ratio of the time, and that the motion of projectiles was in the curve of a parabola". Corollary V gives the relativity of uniform motion: "The motions of bodies included in a given space are the same among themselves, whether that space is at rest, or moves uniformly forwards in a right line without any circular motion".

**Universal gravitation.** "That there is a power of gravity tending to all bodies, proportional to the several quantities of matter which they contain" (Book III, Proposition VII). He would not call it essential to bodies: "Not that I affirm gravity to be essential to bodies" (comment on Rule III). To Richard Bentley: "pray do not ascribe that notion to me, for the cause of gravity is what I do not pretend to know" (17 January 1692/3), and "That gravity should be innate inherent & essential to matter so that one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum without the mediation of any thing else … is to me so great an absurdity that I believe no man who has in philosophical matters any competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it. Gravity must be caused by an agent acting constantly according to certain laws, but whether this agent be material or immaterial is a question I have left to the consideration of my readers" (25 February 1692/3). Huygens and Leibniz saw the theory as invoking an occult action at a distance.

**The Rules of Reasoning.** Book III opens with rules for natural philosophy: "We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances" (I); "to the same natural effects we must, as far as possible, assign the same causes" (II); qualities "found to belong to all bodies within the reach of our experiments, are to be esteemed the universal qualities of all bodies whatsoever", for "We are certainly not to relinquish the evidence of experiments for the sake of dreams and vain fictions of our own devising" (III); and "In experimental philosophy we are to look upon propositions collected by general induction from phænomena as accurately or very nearly true … till such time as other phænomena occur, by which they may either be made more accurate, or liable to exceptions" (IV; tr. Motte). The first two stood in the first edition of 1687, there called "hypotheses"; the other two came with the later editions.

**"Hypotheses non fingo" and experimental philosophy.** In the General Scholium, added in 1713, Newton says that he has not yet been able to deduce the cause of gravity's properties from the phenomena, "& Hypotheses non fingo": in Motte's English, "I frame no hypotheses"; Newton's own English, in the *Opticks*, speaks of "feigning Hypotheses". Whatever is not deduced from the phenomena is to be called a hypothesis, and hypotheses, metaphysical or physical, of occult qualities or mechanical, "have no place in experimental philosophy. In this philosophy particular propositions are inferr'd from the phænomena, and afterwards render'd general by induction" (tr. Motte, 1729). The *Opticks* gives the order of work: "As in Mathematicks, so in Natural Philosophy, the Investigation of difficult Things by the Method of Analysis, ought ever to precede the Method of Composition. This Analysis consists in making Experiments and Observations, and in drawing general Conclusions from them by Induction … And although the arguing from Experiments and Observations by Induction be no Demonstration of general Conclusions; yet it is the best way of arguing which the Nature of Things admits of" (Query 31). The *Principia*'s preface states the task: "all the difficulty of philosophy seems to consist in this—from the phænomena of motions to investigate the forces of nature, and then from these forces to demonstrate the other phænomena".

**Fluxions.** The calculus he worked out in 1665–66 is set down in a tract of October 1666. He called the rates of change fluxions and marked them with a dot over the letter; his method was printed only late, as *A Treatise of the Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series* (1737). The dispute with Leibniz over priority is in his life above.

**Alchemy and theology.** Over some thirty years Newton wrote and transcribed about a million words on alchemy (in the count of the Chymistry of Isaac Newton project), his "chymistry", and, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s words, put "no less effort into chemical and alchemical research and into theology and biblical studies than he put into mathematics and physics". In a tract of the early 1670s: "Natures actions are either vegetable or purely mechanicall (grav. flux. meteors. vulgar Chymistry)" ("Of Natures obvious laws & processes in vegetation"). He kept it private: a method of Boyle's "may possibly be an in let to something more noble, not to be communicated without immense dammage to the world if there should be any verity in the Hermetick writers"; "pray keep this letter private to your self" (to Oldenburg, 1676). Humphrey Newton, his assistant in 1684–89, remembered that for about six weeks each spring and autumn he worked in his laboratory, "the ffire scarcely going out either Night or Day": "What his Aim might be, I was not able to penetrate into but his Paine, his Diligence at those sett times, made me think, he aim'd at som thing beyond the Reach of humane Art & Industry". In theology he was an anti-Trinitarian, and set out these convictions privately in letters to Locke in 1690. In the *Opticks* the *vis inertiae* is "a passive Principle", and "By this Principle alone there never could have been any Motion in the World": motion is conserved "by active Principles, such as are the cause of Gravity ... and the cause of Fermentation", which are "general Laws of Nature" known "by Phænomena, though their Causes be not yet discover'd" (Query 31); and nature "seems delighted with Transmutations" (Query 30).

**Absolute space and time.** "Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external, and by another name is called duration: relative, apparent, and common time, is some sensible and external … measure of duration by the means of motion." "Absolute space, in its own nature, without regard to anything external, remains always similar and immovable. Relative space is some movable dimension or measure of the absolute spaces; which our senses determine by its position to bodies" (Scholium to the Definitions, tr. Motte). In the bucket experiment the spinning water rises up the sides "(as I have experienced)", and "the true and absolute circular motion of the water … discovers itself, and may be measured by this endeavour". God "is not duration or space, but he endures and is present … and by existing always and every where, he constitutes duration and space" (General Scholium). [Leibniz](/positioning/persons/l/leibniz/) answered, in his exchange with Samuel Clarke of 1715–16, with space as an order of coexistences, and [Mach](/positioning/persons/m/mach/) later criticised absolute space itself.

**Light and colours.** In 1666, he reports, "I procured me a Triangular glass-Prisme, to try therewith the celebrated Phænomena of Colours", and the spectrum came out "in an oblong form"; the *experimentum crucis* followed, and the conclusion: "Light it self is a Heterogeneous mixture of differently refrangible Rays", the colours "Original and Connate properties" of the rays (*Philosophical Transactions*, 1672). The *Opticks* adds that "the Rays to speak properly are not coloured. In them there is nothing else than a certain Power and Disposition to stir up a Sensation of this or that Colour … in the Sensorium they are Sensations of those Motions under the Forms of Colours". [Goethe](/positioning/persons/g/goethe/)'s *Theory of Colours* set itself against this account.

**Natural philosophy including God.** "This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets, could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being"; "And thus much concerning God; to discourse of whom from the appearances of things, does certainly belong to Natural Philosophy" (General Scholium, tr. Motte). In 1713 the Latin had "experimental philosophy" here; the third edition of 1726 made it "philosophiam naturalem". In the *Opticks* "the main Business of natural Philosophy is to argue from Phænomena without feigning Hypotheses, and to deduce Causes from Effects, till we come to the very first Cause, which certainly is not mechanical", and to ask "for what ends" the parts of animals were made (Query 28); and "if natural Philosophy in all its Parts, by pursuing this Method, shall at length be perfected, the Bounds of Moral Philosophy will be also enlarged" (Query 31). To Bentley he wrote that in the *Principia* he "had an eye upon such Principles as might work with considering men for the belief of a Deity". The historian Andrew Cunningham argued that natural philosophy was not modern science under an older name: in a catalogue's summary of his 1991 article, it was "about God" and "God's universe".

## Where Newton stops

Newton set his own limits and said so. The cause of gravity was "what I do not pretend to know", and the question whether its agent was material or immaterial he left to his readers. The nature of light stayed a question: whether rays are "very small Bodies" is asked in a query, not asserted. Absolute space and time are defined in the *Principia* but, in George Smith's reading of it, never presupposed in Newton's empirical reasoning. Much that became "Newtonian" was not his. The clockwork universe, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s account, "is not to be found in the *Principia*; it was added by Laplace"; Newton's own system, in the *Opticks*, could need a "Reformation" from its maker. And the mechanics taught under his name, in the same account, "consists mostly of results achieved on the continent between 1740 and 1800".

## Key works

- "A New Theory about Light and Colours", *Philosophical Transactions* (1672) — the prism experiments, light as a mixture of rays
- [*Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophi%C3%A6_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica) (1687; 2nd ed. 1713; 3rd ed. 1726) — the laws of motion, universal gravitation, the Rules of Reasoning, the General Scholium; English by Andrew Motte (1729)
- [*Opticks*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks) (1704; 4th ed. 1730) — light and colours, the Queries
- *Arithmetica Universalis* (1707) — algebra
- *A Treatise of the Method of Fluxions and Infinite Series* (1737) — the calculus
- Letters to Richard Bentley (1692–93; printed 1756) — gravity, its cause, the design of the system
- *The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended* (1728) and *Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St. John* (1733) — the private Newton
