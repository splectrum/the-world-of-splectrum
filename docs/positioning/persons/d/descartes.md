# René Descartes (1596–1650)

Descartes placed the thinking subject at the centre of philosophy. Radical doubt left only the doubter standing, and from there he rebuilt knowledge, with God as guarantor that clear and distinct ideas correspond to reality. He divided mind and body into separate substances and so set the problem of how they interact. The picture he built was taken up and contested by [Kant](/positioning/persons/k/kant/), [Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/), [Merleau-Ponty](/positioning/persons/m/merleau-ponty/) and [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/), among others; Rorty traced to it the picture of the mind as a mirror of nature.

[René Descartes](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes/) (1596–1650). Philosopher, mathematician, scientist — often called the father of modern philosophy. Educated by Jesuits at La Flèche, he spent much of his adult life in the Netherlands, seeking the freedom to think undisturbed. His method of radical doubt — stripping away everything that could be doubted until only the thinking subject remained — produced the most famous sentence in philosophy: "I think, therefore I am" (*cogito ergo sum*). From there he rebuilt, using God as guarantor that clear and distinct ideas correspond to reality. He also founded analytic geometry, linking algebra to spatial reasoning. He died in Stockholm in 1650, having been invited by Queen Christina of Sweden — the cold, the early hours, and possibly arsenic.

## Key concepts

**The cogito.** "I think, therefore I am." The one thing that survives radical doubt: the act of doubting proves the existence of a doubter. This places the thinking subject at the centre of philosophy — a move whose consequences are still being worked through.

**The mind-body split (substance dualism).** Mind (*res cogitans*) and body (*res extensa*) are separate substances. "Sed quid igitur sum? Res cogitans": "But what then am I? A thing which thinks. What is a thing which thinks? It is a thing which doubts, understands, … affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels" (*Meditations* II, tr. Haldane and Ross). The sixth Meditation draws the line: "I have a clear and distinct idea of myself inasmuch as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and as, on the other, I possess a distinct idea of body, inasmuch as it is only an extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that this I … is entirely and absolutely distinct from my body, and can exist without it" ("res cogitans, non extensa … res extensa, non cogitans"). It also created the problem that haunted philosophy for centuries: how do mind and body interact?

**Body as extension, physics as geometry.** In the *Principles* each substance has one principal attribute: "extension in length, breadth, and depth, constitutes the nature of corporeal substance; and thought the nature of thinking substance" (I.53, tr. Veitch). So the nature of body consists not "in its being hard, or ponderous, or coloured, or that which affects our senses in any other way, but simply in its being a substance extended in length, breadth, and depth" (II.4). Physics follows from it: "plane profiteor me nullam aliam rerum corporearum materiam agnoscere, quam illam omnimode divisibilem, figurabilem & mobilem, quam Geometrae quantitatem vocant", I frankly declare that I recognise no matter of corporeal things other than that which is divisible, shapeable and movable in every way, which the geometers call quantity; and "nulla alia Physicae principia puto esse admittenda, nec alia etiam optanda", I think no other principles of physics should be admitted, nor even wished for (II.64; English rendered from the Latin). To Mersenne in 1638 he put it in one line: "toute ma Physique n'est autre chose que Géométrie", my whole physics is nothing other than geometry (English rendered from the French).

**Clear and distinct ideas.** Descartes assumed that clear and distinct ideas — when God is not deceiving us — correspond to how things really are. [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) called the resulting picture the mind as a mirror of nature, with the philosopher's task to polish the mirror, traced it through Locke, Kant and the empiricist tradition, and set out to dismantle it.

**Method.** The *Discourse on the Method* (1637, Part II) gives four rules: the first "never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such"; the second, to divide each difficulty "into as many parts as possible"; the third, to proceed from "the simplest and easiest to know" by steps "to the knowledge of the more complex"; the last, "to make enumerations so complete, and reviews so general, that I might be assured that nothing was omitted" (tr. Veitch). Descartes sought a universal method for arriving at certain knowledge — breaking problems into parts, proceeding from simple to complex, checking for completeness. The method is the ancestor of modern scientific methodology, though Descartes himself grounded it in [metaphysics](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphysics/) rather than experiment.

**Philosophy as a tree.** The letter that prefaces the French edition of the *Principles of Philosophy* defines the word: "the word PHILOSOPHY signifies the study of wisdom, and … by wisdom is to be understood not merely prudence in the management of affairs, but a perfect knowledge of all that man can know, as well for the conduct of his life as for the preservation of his health and the discovery of all the arts", and such knowledge "must necessarily be deduced from first causes". Its image: "all Philosophy is like a tree, of which Metaphysics is the root, Physics the trunk, and all the other sciences the branches", the branches chiefly medicine, mechanics and ethics (tr. Veitch).

## Key works

- [*Discourse on the Method*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_the_Method) (1637) — the method of doubt, "I think therefore I am"
- [*Meditations on First Philosophy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations_on_First_Philosophy) (1641) — the cogito, God's guarantee, substance dualism
- [*Principles of Philosophy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_of_Philosophy) (1644) — the systematic statement; physics, cosmology, method
