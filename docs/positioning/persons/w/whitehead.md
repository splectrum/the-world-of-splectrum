# Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947)

Whitehead built a relational ontology. Nothing exists in isolation — every actual entity is constituted by its relations to other actual entities, and reality is [process](/positioning/subjects/p/process-philosophy/), not substance: events arising, achieving satisfaction, perishing, taken up by what follows. His rejection of the bifurcation of nature — the split between the world physics measures and the world we experience — runs from his philosophy of nature into his metaphysics, where it widens into a refusal to split a world of facts from a world of values.

[Alfred North Whitehead](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/whitehead/) (1861–1947). Three distinct careers, each producing major work. The first thirty years at Cambridge (Trinity College) as a mathematician — *Principia Mathematica* with [Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/), foundational work in logic and algebra. Then London (University College London from 1911, Imperial College from 1914), where he turned toward the philosophy of science and of education, reconstructing the space-time of Einstein's special relativity from events and, in *The Principle of Relativity* (1922), offering an alternative to Einstein's general theory of gravitation. At 63 he moved to Harvard, where he built the speculative metaphysical system of *Process and Reality*. He called the system the [philosophy of organism](/positioning/subjects/p/philosophy-of-organism/); it inspired no school in his lifetime and was carried on chiefly by process theology, and later by readers in continental philosophy such as [Stengers](/positioning/persons/s/stengers/).

## Key concepts

**Speculative philosophy.** His name for the project: "Speculative Philosophy is the endeavour to frame a coherent, logical, necessary system of general ideas in terms of which every element of our experience can be interpreted" (*Process and Reality*, Part I, ch. I, §I).

**Actual occasions.** The basic units of reality — not things but happenings. Each actual occasion arises through *concrescence*: the process of drawing together past occasions, unifying them into a new moment of experience. When concrescence is complete, the occasion becomes a datum for future occasions. "'Actual entities'—also termed 'actual occasions'—are the final real things of which the world is made up", and "God is an actual entity, and so is the most trivial puff of existence in far-off empty space" (*Process and Reality*, Part I, ch. II, §I). Each is "essentially bipolar, physical and mental" (Part II, ch. III). An enduring thing is a *society* of occasions: "an 'enduring object,' or 'enduring creature,' is a society whose social order has taken the special form of 'personal order'" (Part I, ch. III, §II).

**Prehension.** How one actual occasion takes account of another. A prehension is not perception in the human sense — it is any mode of grasping, incorporating, or being affected by what has already occurred. Positive prehensions include; negative prehensions exclude. Together they shape what the new occasion becomes. Because every actual entity prehends its world, experience-like taking-up goes all the way down — nothing in reality is a mere passive object — and human [cognition](/positioning/subjects/c/cognition/) is a highly elaborated case of something utterly general, not an exception to nature. In Whitehead's words, "consciousness presupposes experience, and not experience consciousness" (*Process and Reality*, Part II, ch. I), and "apart from the experiences of subjects there is nothing, nothing, nothing, bare nothingness" (Part II, ch. VII). Of the public and private sides of prehensions: "Prehensions have public careers, but they are born privately" (Part IV, ch. I).

**Creativity.** The ultimate category — "the universal of universals" (*Process and Reality*, Part I, ch. II, §II). Creativity is the principle by which the many become one and are increased by one. Every new actual occasion is a creative act: something genuinely new arising from what was given. Not repetition but advance.

**Eternal objects.** Pure potentials, which Whitehead also calls forms of definiteness. They enter actual occasions by what he calls *ingression*: "The eternal objects are the pure potentials of the universe; and the actual entities differ from each other in their realization of potentials" (*Process and Reality*, Part II, ch. VI). He called those of the objective species "the mathematical platonic forms" (Part IV, ch. I). Of the European philosophical tradition he wrote that it "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato" (Part II, ch. I, §I). Whether the system needs them is disputed; see [Philosophy of organism](/positioning/subjects/p/philosophy-of-organism/).

**The bifurcation of nature.** Since [Galileo](/positioning/persons/g/galileo/) and [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/), modern science has split nature into two — the nature that is (primary qualities: mass, extension, motion) and the nature that is perceived (secondary qualities: colour, sound, warmth). Whitehead rejects this bifurcation: nature is one, and what we perceive is as real as what physics measures. The split is not in nature but in our theorising. In the later metaphysics the refusal widens, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s words, to "the bifurcation of the world into the objective world of facts … and the subjective world of values". "What I am essentially protesting against is the bifurcation of nature into two systems of reality … Thus there would be two natures, one is the conjecture and the other is the dream" (*The Concept of Nature*, 1920, ch. II). In *Science and the Modern World* (1925) he sums up, ironically, the outcome of the seventeenth century's scientific philosophy: "The poets are entirely mistaken … Nature is a dull affair, soundless, scentless, colourless; merely the hurrying of material, endlessly, meaninglessly"; and its cost: "The enormous success of the scientific abstractions, yielding on the one hand matter with its simple location in space and time, on the other hand mind, perceiving, suffering, reasoning, but not interfering, has foisted onto philosophy the task of accepting them as the most concrete rendering of fact. Thereby, modern philosophy has been ruined" (ch. III).

**Natural philosophy.** *The Concept of Nature* sets out "to lay the basis of a natural philosophy which is the necessary presupposition of a reorganised speculative physics", and finds that "The modern natural philosophy is shot through and through with the fallacy of bifurcation" (preface). Its natural philosophy is defined accordingly: "we can think about nature without thinking about thought. I shall say that then we are thinking 'homogeneously' about nature … Natural science is exclusively concerned with homogeneous thoughts about nature" (ch. I). "For natural philosophy everything perceived is in nature. We may not pick and choose. For us the red glow of the sunset should be as much part of nature as are the molecules and electric waves"; its problem "is to discuss the relations *inter se* of things known, abstracted from the bare fact that they are known. Natural philosophy should never ask, what is in the mind and what is in nature" (ch. II). The whole remains philosophy's far ideal: "some unifying concept which will set in assigned relationships within itself all that there is for knowledge, for feeling, and for emotion. That far off ideal is the motive power of philosophic research" (ch. I).

**The fallacy of misplaced concreteness.** Taking abstractions for concrete realities. Of the "simple location" of matter in space and time, the spatialisation that Bergson protested against, he writes: "There is an error; but it is merely the accidental error of mistaking the abstract for the concrete. It is an example of what I will call the 'Fallacy of Misplaced Concreteness'" (*Science and the Modern World*, 1925, ch. III). The same chapter finds that "substance and quality afford another instance of the fallacy of misplaced concreteness", and closes on "the ascription of misplaced concreteness to the scientific scheme of the seventeenth century".

**Process and permanence.** Whitehead holds both together. Each actual occasion perishes, but its achievement is taken up — *objectified* — in subsequent occasions. The past is not lost; it is the material from which the present arises. Whitehead's term is *objective immortality*: "the 'perishing' of absoluteness is the attainment of 'objective immortality'" (*Process and Reality*, Part II, ch. I). Of the pair he wrote: "In the inescapable flux, there is something that abides; in the overwhelming permanence, there is an element that escapes into flux. Permanence can be snatched only out of flux" (Part V, ch. I).

**God.** Whitehead's God is not the God of traditional theism. It is the principle that provides the initial aim for each actual occasion (the primordial nature — the ordering of all possibilities) and the preservation of all achieved value (the consequent nature — God as shaped by the world's experience). "God is not to be treated as an exception to all metaphysical principles, invoked to save their collapse. He is their chief exemplification" (*Process and Reality*, Part V, ch. II). His reason for God is order and novelty: "Apart from the intervention of God, there could be nothing new in the world, and no order in the world" (Part III, ch. III). Process theology grew from this; secular readers of Whitehead tend to set it aside. The concept remains one of the most contested aspects of his philosophy; the dispute is set out under [Philosophy of organism](/positioning/subjects/p/philosophy-of-organism/).

## Key works

- *A Treatise on Universal Algebra* (1898) — the algebra that won him election to the Royal Society
- [*Principia Mathematica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Mathematica) (1910–13, with Bertrand Russell) — the foundations of mathematics; the Cambridge career
- *The Concept of Nature* (1920) — nature as process, against the bifurcation of nature into appearance and reality
- *Science and the Modern World* (1925) — the fallacy of misplaced concreteness, the history of scientific abstraction, and the case that "a thoroughgoing evolutionary philosophy is inconsistent with materialism" (ch. VI)
- *Religion in the Making* (1926) — lectures on religion
- [*Process and Reality*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_and_Reality) (1929) — the full metaphysical system: actual occasions, prehension, creativity, God
- *The Aims of Education and Other Essays* (1929) — essays on education
- *Adventures of Ideas* (1933) — civilisation as the interplay of ideas: beauty, truth, adventure, peace
