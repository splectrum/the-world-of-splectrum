# Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)

Wittgenstein's two books on language, thought and the world are usually read as two philosophies. The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) held that a proposition is a picture of reality, sharing its logical form with what it depicts, that the propositions of logic are tautologies, and that what cannot be said can only be shown. The [logical positivism](/positioning/subjects/p/philosophy-of-science/) of the Vienna Circle was indebted to it. The *Philosophical Investigations* (published in 1953, after his death) turned against that book and the philosophy before it. For a large class of cases, it says, the meaning of a word is its use in the language; the speaking of a language is part of an activity, or of a form of life; and philosophy describes rather than explains. Around and after the two books lie remarks on mathematics, which he once named his chief contribution, on psychology and on certainty. The later work helped to inspire Oxford ordinary-language philosophy ([Ryle](/positioning/persons/r/ryle/)). How the two relate is itself disputed: the "resolute" readers find one therapeutic aim running through both.

[Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/) (1889–1951). Born in Vienna into the family of Karl Wittgenstein, who led the iron and steel industry of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. He studied aeronautical engineering in Manchester, and his interest in the philosophy of mathematics led him to [Frege](/positioning/persons/f/frege/), on whose advice he went to Cambridge in 1911 to study with [Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/). He wrote the notes and drafts of the *Tractatus* while serving in the Austrian army in the First World War, and was a prisoner of war in Italy for nine months from November 1918. The book appeared in German in 1921 and in English in 1922, with an introduction by Russell. He gave away his share of the family fortune. Believing he had solved, "in essentials", the problems of philosophy, he left the field, working as a village schoolteacher in Austria, as a gardener, and as the architect of a house in Vienna for his sister. He returned to Cambridge in 1929. The preface of the *Investigations* credits the criticism of [Frank Ramsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_P._Ramsey) and, even more, of the economist [Piero Sraffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Sraffa): "I am indebted to this stimulus for the most consequential ideas of this book." He held the chair of philosophy at Cambridge from 1939 to 1947. He withdrew the *Investigations* from publication in 1945 and authorised only its posthumous publication. He died in Cambridge in 1951.

## The early philosophy

The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) proposed that language mirrors reality through a shared logical structure. A proposition is "a picture of reality" (4.01), representing a possible state of affairs, and "the totality of true propositions is the total natural science" (4.11). Logic is the scaffolding. What cannot be pictured cannot be said. What cannot be said may still show itself: "What can be shown cannot be said" (4.1212). Ethics is of this kind ("It is clear that ethics cannot be expressed", 6.421), and "There is indeed the inexpressible. This shows itself; it is the mystical" (6.522). Wittgenstein told Ludwig von Ficker that the book's point was ethical. Of its own propositions the book says that whoever understands him will recognise them as senseless: "He must so to speak throw away the ladder, after he has climbed up on it" (6.54). "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" (5.6), and the book closes: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." The preface holds that "the problems have in essentials been finally solved", and adds that the book's value "consists in the fact that it shows how little has been done when these problems have been solved".

The book also says what philosophy is. "All philosophy is 'Critique of language'" (4.0031). It "is not one of the natural sciences"; the word "must mean something which stands above or below, but not beside the natural sciences" (4.111). "The object of philosophy is the logical clarification of thoughts. Philosophy is not a theory but an activity" (4.112). And its method: "To say nothing except what can be said, i.e. the propositions of natural science … and then always, when someone else wished to say something metaphysical, to demonstrate to him that he had given no meaning to certain signs in his propositions" (6.53). Back in philosophy, he was "forced to recognize grave mistakes in what I wrote in that first book", and he wanted the new thoughts published with the old, since they "could be seen in the right light only by contrast with and against the background of my old way of thinking" (preface to the *Investigations*).

## Key concepts

**Meaning is use.** *Philosophical Investigations* §43: "For a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language." This opposes the picture of language he finds in Augustine's *Confessions* (§1): "the individual words in language name objects—sentences are combinations of such names", and "Every word has a meaning. This meaning is correlated with the word. It is the object for which the word stands."

**Language games.** At §2 he imagines a language for which that picture is right: a builder A and his assistant B use the words "block", "pillar", "slab", "beam"; "A calls them out;—B brings the stone which he has learnt to bring at such-and-such a call" (§2). Wittgenstein introduces the term at §7: "I shall also call the whole, consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven, the 'language-game'." At §23 there are "countless" kinds of use, and new language-games "come into existence, and others become obsolete and get forgotten"; "the term 'language-game' is meant to bring into prominence the fact that the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life." The list that follows is deliberately open-ended: "Giving orders, and obeying them— … Making a joke; telling it— … Asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying."

**The ancient city.** "Our language can be seen as an ancient city: a maze of little streets and squares, of old and new houses, and of houses with additions from various periods; and this surrounded by a multitude of new boroughs with straight regular streets and uniform houses" (§18). The symbolism of chemistry and the notation of the infinitesimal calculus are, so to speak, "suburbs of our language"; and "how many houses or streets does it take before a town begins to be a town?"

**Family resemblance.** §66–67: "Consider for example the proceedings that we call 'games'. ... What is common to them all?—Don't say: 'There must be something common, or they would not be called "games"'—but look and see whether there is anything common to all." What emerges is "a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing: sometimes overall similarities, sometimes similarities of detail. I can think of no better expression to characterize these similarities than 'family resemblances'."

**Forms of life.** "To imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" (§19). "What has to be accepted, the given, is—so one could say—forms of life" (Part II, p. 226). Language belongs to human natural history: "Commanding, questioning, recounting, chatting, are as much a part of our natural history as walking, eating, drinking, playing" (§25), and "What we are supplying are really remarks on the natural history of human beings" (§415). Readers divide on whether there is one human form of life or many. Newton Garver reads the concept as biological: for him the identification of forms of life with cultures "has no basis whatsoever in the text" (as Danièle Moyal-Sharrock quotes him). P. M. S. Hacker ("Forms of Life", 2015) reads it as cultural: "There is no uniquely human form of life, characteristic of the species – rather there are multiple human forms of life, characterized by different languages, and characteristic of different cultures and epochs." "If a lion could talk, we could not understand him" (Part II, p. 223) follows, on the same page, the remark that one human being can be "a complete enigma to another", as in "a strange country with entirely strange traditions … We cannot find our feet with them".

**Understanding is not a mental process.** §154: "Try not to think of understanding as a 'mental process' at all. — For that is the expression which confuses you." Understanding shows itself in what one can do — go on with the series, apply the word, follow the rule — not in an inner event accompanying the doing. The grammar of "knows", he notes, is "closely related to that of 'can', 'is able to'. But also closely related to that of 'understands'. ('Mastery' of a technique.)" (§150); and "To understand a language means to be master of a technique" (§199).

**Rule-following.** "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule". The paradox rests on a misunderstanding, he goes on, and what this shows is that "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases" (§201). What connects a sign-post with my actions is training: "I have been trained to react to this sign in a particular way, and now I do so react to it". And "a person goes by a sign-post only in so far as there exists a regular use of sign-posts, a custom" (§198). "To obey a rule, to make a report, to give an order, to play a game of chess, are customs (uses, institutions)" (§199). The learning of language starts the same way: "Here the teaching of language is not explanation, but training" (§5). One influential reading, [Saul Kripke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Kripke)'s (*Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, 1982), takes Wittgenstein to voice a sceptical paradox and to offer a sceptical solution; G. P. Baker and P. M. S. Hacker are among those who reject it.

**The private language argument.** The sections after the rule-following remarks, from §243, are what interpreters call "the private-language argument" (Wittgenstein never labelled it one). They ask whether there could be a language whose "individual words … are to refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language" (§243). On the usual reading, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it, such a language "is not a genuine, meaningful, rule-governed language", since a meaningful utterance must be open in principle to "public standards and criteria of correctness". In §258 someone keeps a diary of a recurring sensation, marking it "S" and fixing the sign by attending to the sensation inwardly. "But in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." The rule-following remarks had reached the same point: "To think one is obeying a rule is not to obey a rule. Hence it is not possible to obey a rule 'privately': otherwise thinking one was obeying a rule would be the same thing as obeying it" (§202).

**Agreement in judgments.** "It is what human beings say that is true and false; and they agree in the language they use. That is not agreement in opinions but in form of life" (§241). "If language is to be a means of communication there must be agreement not only in definitions but also (queer as this may sound) in judgments" (§242). When justifications run out: "I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: 'This is simply what I do'" (§217).

**Certainty.** *On Certainty* collects the notes of his last eighteen months on [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore)'s claims to know such things as "Here is one hand". "The questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (§341). Nor is the picture of the world reached by checking it: "I did not get my picture of the world by satisfying myself of its correctness; nor do I have it because I am satisfied of its correctness. No: it is the inherited background against which I distinguish between true and false" (§94), and "The child learns to believe a host of things" (§144). The giving of grounds ends not in propositions that strike us as true but in "our acting, which lies at the bottom of the language-game" (§204). Readers call these certainties "hinge propositions".

**Seeing aspects.** The remarks printed as Part II describe "noticing an aspect": seeing that something "has not changed; and yet I see it differently" (Part II, p. 193), as with the duck-rabbit, seen now as a duck, now as a rabbit.

## The therapeutic stance

"We may not advance any kind of theory. There must not be anything hypothetical in our considerations. We must do away with all explanation, and description alone must take its place … The problems are solved, not by giving new information, but by arranging what we have always known. Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language" (§109). Philosophy "leaves everything as it is" (§124). "Philosophy simply puts everything before us, and neither explains nor deduces anything" (§126). "The work of the philosopher consists in assembling reminders for a particular purpose" (§127).

Philosophical problems arise when "language goes on holiday" (§38); "the confusions which occupy us arise when language is like an engine idling, not when it is doing work" (§132). The philosopher's task is "to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309). "The clarity that we are aiming at is indeed complete clarity. But this simply means that the philosophical problems should completely disappear. The real discovery is the one that makes me capable of stopping doing philosophy when I want to. — The one that gives philosophy peace" (§133). The same section: "There is not a philosophical method, though there are indeed methods, like different therapies".

## Key works

- [*Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractatus_Logico-Philosophicus) (1921; English 1922) — the picture theory, logic as tautology, saying and showing
- [*Philosophical Investigations*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Investigations) (1953, posthumous) — the later philosophy; language games, forms of life, meaning as use
- [*Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remarks_on_the_Foundations_of_Mathematics) (1956, posthumous) — notes of 1937–44 on proof, calculation and the foundations of mathematics
- [*The Blue and Brown Books*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_and_Brown_Books) (1958, posthumous) — lecture notes and dictations of 1933–35, early versions of the ideas of the *Investigations*
- [*On Certainty*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Certainty) (1969, posthumous) — Moore's certainties; propositions "exempt from doubt", like hinges
- [*Remarks on Colour*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remarks_on_Colour) (1977, posthumous) — written in 1950; the logic of colour concepts, prompted in part by [Goethe](/positioning/persons/g/goethe/)'s *Theory of Colours*
