# Galileo Galilei (1564–1642)

Galileo was a mathematician who claimed the title of philosopher. Philosophy, he wrote, is written in the book of the universe, and the book is written in the language of mathematics. He turned a telescope on the sky, found the law of falling bodies, gave the relativity of motion an image in a ship's cabin, and set tastes, odours and colours apart from what bodies cannot lack. His defence of the Copernican system ended in his condemnation in 1633, and the condemnation became a symbol in its own right.

[Galileo Galilei](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/galileo/) was born in Pisa on 15 February 1564, the son of Vincenzo Galilei, a court musician and music theorist. He enrolled in medicine at Pisa in 1580, studied mathematics with Ostilio Ricci, and left without a degree. He lectured in mathematics at Pisa from 1589 and held the chair of mathematics at Padua from 1592, where he ran an instrument workshop. In 1609 he heard of the spyglass and built an improved telescope; the *Sidereus Nuncius* followed in March 1610, and later that year he moved to Florence as "Chief Mathematician and Philosopher" to the Grand Duke. In 1616 Copernicus's *De revolutionibus* was suspended pending correction and Cardinal Bellarmine admonished Galileo. *Il Saggiatore* (*The Assayer*) appeared in 1623, the year Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII. The *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* was printed in Florence in 1632; its sale was banned and Galileo was tried. On 22 June 1633 he was sentenced as "vehementemente sospetto d'heresia", vehemently suspected of heresy, and abjured; the imprisonment was commuted to house arrest, from December 1633 at Arcetri. *Two New Sciences* was published by the Elzeviers in Leiden in 1638. He died on 8 January 1642.

## Key concepts

English without a named translator is rendered from the Italian or the French.

**Philosopher as well as mathematician.** Asking for the court post in 1610, he wrote: "io desidererei, oltre al nome di Matematico, che S. A. ci aggiungesse quello di Filosofo, professando io di avere studiato più anni in filosofia, che mesi in matematica pura", I would wish His Highness to add to the name of Mathematician that of Philosopher, since I profess to have studied more years in philosophy than months in pure mathematics (to Belisario Vinta, 7 May 1610). In the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s account this made him a "novel hybrid", licensed to draw conclusions about the natures of things from mathematical demonstrations, against the old division between the mathematician and the professor of natural philosophy.

**The book of nature, written in mathematics.** Against an opponent who treated philosophy as "un libro e una fantasia d'un uomo, come l'*Iliade* e l'*Orlando furioso*", a man's book and fancy, like the *Iliad* and *Orlando furioso*: "La filosofia è scritta in questo grandissimo libro che continuamente ci sta aperto innanzi a gli occhi (io dico l'universo) … Egli è scritto in lingua matematica, e i caratteri son triangoli, cerchi, ed altre figure geometriche, senza i quali mezi è impossibile a intenderne umanamente parola; senza questi è un aggirarsi vanamente per un oscuro laberinto": philosophy is written in this great book that stands always open before our eyes, I mean the universe; it is written in the language of mathematics, its characters are triangles, circles and other geometrical figures, without which it is humanly impossible to understand a word of it, and without these one wanders in vain through a dark labyrinth (*Il Saggiatore* §6). In the *Dialogue* human knowledge, in the few things it understands, equals God's in certainty: of the pure mathematical sciences, geometry and arithmetic, "credo che la cognizione agguagli la divina nella certezza obiettiva", I believe the knowledge equals the divine in objective certainty (Day One). The *filosofo geometra*, the geometer-philosopher, who wants to find in the concrete what was demonstrated in the abstract "bisogna che difalchi gli impedimenti della materia", must deduct the impediments of matter (Day Two).

**What bodies cannot lack.** Tastes, odours and colours "non sieno altro che puri nomi" on the side of the object, are no more than names, and "tengano solamente lor residenza nel corpo sensitivo, sì che rimosso l'animale, sieno levate ed annichilate tutte queste qualità": they reside only in the sensing body, so that if the animal were removed, all these qualities would be taken away and annihilated. What a body cannot be without is shape, size, place, time, motion or rest, contact and number, "primi e reali accidenti", first and real accidents (*Il Saggiatore* §48). Galileo's word is the sensing body, the living animal; Stillman Drake's English renders it "consciousness". The pairing of "primary and secondary" qualities is [Locke](/positioning/persons/l/locke/)'s.

**Telescopic observations.** The *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610) addressed "every one, but especially philosophers and astronomers". The Moon is "not perfectly smooth, free from inequalities and exactly spherical, as a large school of philosophers considers … but … full of inequalities, uneven, full of hollows and protuberances, just like the surface of the Earth itself". Four moons circle Jupiter, which he named the Medicean Stars: "our sense of sight presents to us four satellites circling about Jupiter, like the Moon about the Earth, while the whole system travels over a mighty orbit about the Sun" (tr. Carlos). The phases of Venus and the odd shape of Saturn followed later in 1610.

**Falling bodies and horizontal motion.** *Two New Sciences* (1638) opens its third day: "My purpose is to set forth a very new science dealing with a very ancient subject … I have discovered by experiment some properties of it which are worth knowing". The law: "The spaces described by a body falling from rest with a uniformly accelerated motion are to each other as the squares of the time-intervals employed in traversing these distances". The cause is set aside: "The present does not seem to be the proper time to investigate the cause of the acceleration of natural motion". On the horizontal, "any velocity once imparted to a moving body will be rigidly maintained as long as the external causes of acceleration or retardation are removed … motion along a horizontal plane is perpetual", and a projectile's path, compounded of the uniform horizontal and the accelerated vertical, is a parabola (tr. Crew and de Salvio). "Inertia" is not his word; [Newton](/positioning/persons/n/newton/) later named the force *vis inertiae*, and credited the law of fall and the parabola to Galileo.

**The ship.** In the *Dialogue*'s second day Salviati says: "Riserratevi con qualche amico nella maggiore stanza che sia sotto coverta di alcun gran navilio", shut yourself up with a friend in the largest cabin below decks of some great ship, with flies, butterflies, fish in a bowl and dripping water; then "fate muover la nave con quanta si voglia velocità; chè (pur che il moto sia uniforme e non fluttuante in qua e in là) voi non riconoscerete una minima mutazione in tutti li nominati effetti, nè da alcuno di quelli potrete comprender se la nave cammina o pure sta ferma": make the ship move as fast as you like; as long as the motion is uniform and not rocking, you will not see the least change in any of these effects, nor be able to tell from any of them whether the ship is moving or standing still, because "l'esser il moto della nave comune a tutte le cose contenute in essa ed all'aria ancora", the ship's motion is common to everything in it, and to the air as well. "Galilean relativity" is a later name. On the stone dropped from the mast, asked whether he has made a hundred trials, Salviati answers: "Io senza esperienza son sicuro che l'effetto seguirà come vi dico, perchè così è necessario che segua", I am sure without experiment that the effect will follow as I tell you, because it must follow so.

**Scripture and nature.** In the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615): "nelle dispute di problemi naturali non si dovrebbe cominciare dalle autorità di luoghi delle Scritture, ma dalle sensate esperienze e dalle dimostrazioni necessarie", in disputes about natural problems one should begin not from the authority of passages of Scripture but from sense-experiences and necessary demonstrations, since Scripture and nature both proceed from the divine Word. "Due verità non possono contrariarsi", two truths cannot contradict each other. And: "l'intenzione dello Spirito Santo essere d'insegnarci come si vadia al cielo, e non come vadia il cielo", the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes. Galileo credits the line to an unnamed churchman of the highest rank.

**The trial.** The *Dialogue*'s preface says he took the Copernican side "procedendo in pura ipotesi matematica", proceeding as a pure mathematical hypothesis. The sentence of 1633 found that the book's arguments for the condemned view seemed "potenti a stringer" rather than "facili ad esser sciolti", compelling rather than easy to answer, and imposed "carcere formale", formal imprisonment, and the seven penitential psalms weekly for three years. The abjuration, made "dell'età mia d'anni 70", at the age of seventy: "con cuor sincero e fede non finta abiuro, maledico e detesto li sudetti errori et heresie", with a sincere heart and unfeigned faith I abjure, curse and detest the said errors and heresies. *De revolutionibus* stayed on the Index until 1835. In 1992 Pope John Paul II told the Pontifical Academy of Sciences that Galileo, "croyant sincère, s'est montré plus perspicace sur ce point que ses adversaires théologiens", a sincere believer, had shown himself more perceptive on this point than his theologian opponents, and that from the Enlightenment on "le cas Galilée a constitué une sorte de mythe", the Galileo case had become a kind of myth.

## Where Galileo stops

Galileo kept to what demonstration could reach. The essences of things he declared beyond investigation, for near substances no less than far ones (*Letters on Sunspots*), and in *Two New Sciences* he set the cause of acceleration aside to demonstrate its properties. His physical argument for the Earth's motion rested on the tides, an argument the *Stanford Encyclopedia* calls a mistake to modern eyes and which did not persuade Bellarmine. What his method was is itself disputed, and his texts point both ways: "I have discovered by experiment" in one book, "senza esperienza son sicuro" in another. In the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s words, he has been used "as a personification of whatever the writer wished to make the hallmark of the Scientific Revolution or of good science": a mathematical Platonist for Alexandre Koyré, an experimenter for Stillman Drake and Thomas Settle, an heir of scholastic method for William Wallace, a breaker of method for [Feyerabend](/positioning/persons/f/feyerabend/). For E. A. Burtt, with Galileo "man begins to appear … as an irrelevant spectator"; for [Collingwood](/positioning/persons/c/collingwood/), he held that "nothing is scientifically knowable except what is measurable"; [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/) read the age's mathematising of nature through him.

## Key works

- [*Sidereus Nuncius*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius) (1610) — the Moon, the fixed stars, the moons of Jupiter
- *Letters on Sunspots* (1613) and the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615) — the Copernican case, Scripture and nature
- [*Il Saggiatore*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Assayer) (1623) — the book of nature, the qualities
- [*Dialogo sopra i due massimi sistemi del mondo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_Concerning_the_Two_Chief_World_Systems) (1632) — the Copernican and Ptolemaic systems, the ship
- [*Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_New_Sciences) (1638) — falling bodies, horizontal motion, projectiles
