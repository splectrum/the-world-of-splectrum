# Michel Henry (1922–2002)

Henry turned [phenomenology](/positioning/subjects/p/phenomenology/) inward until the inward needed no outside. Where [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/) and [Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/) describe how things appear in a world, Henry describes an appearing that never enters the world at all: life feeling itself, without distance, before any object shows up. He called it radical immanence, and built on it a phenomenology of the body, a reading of [Marx](/positioning/persons/m/marx/), a critique of modern culture and, late, a philosophy of Christianity.

[Michel Henry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/michel-henry/) (1922–2002). French philosopher and novelist, born in Haiphong in French Indochina and moved to France in 1929. He studied at the Lycée Henri-IV, wrote his master's thesis on [Spinoza](/positioning/persons/s/spinoza/) in 1943, and the same year joined the Resistance in the maquis of the Haut-Jura under the code name "Kant". His doctoral thesis, *L'essence de la manifestation* (1963), was written under Jean Wahl and Jean Hyppolite. He taught at Montpellier from 1960 until his retirement in 1982. His novel *L'amour les yeux fermés* won the Prix Renaudot in 1976.

## Key concepts

**Two ways of appearing.** Phenomenology, on Henry's reading, had recognised only one way of appearing: the intentional, "ek-static" one, in which something shows itself at a distance, in front of a consciousness, in the light of a world. Henry argues for a second, prior way: an appearing without distance, non-intentional and affective, in which what appears and what it appears to are the same. Every showing of a world rests on it.

**Life as self-revelation.** That second appearing is life. Life is a self-revelation, an auto-affection that refers to nothing but itself: it does not open onto anything else but undergoes itself. In his own phrase, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* quotes it: "It is not outside of itself but in itself, not transcendence but radical immanence." Life is therefore invisible: it never appears in the world, though it is felt in every experience.

**Pathos: suffering and joy.** Because life cannot step back from itself, it is radically passive with regard to itself. Its basic tonalities are suffering and joy: the living cannot escape from what they feel, and that being bound to oneself is both the weight and the joy of living.

**The subjective body.** Against the objective body that can be seen and measured, Henry describes the body as lived from within: the felt power of moving and sensing, known not by looking at it but by being it (*Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*, 1965; *Incarnation*, 2000).

**Material phenomenology and community.** In *Phénoménologie matérielle* (1990) Henry faces the standing objection to his work: how can a life enclosed in itself ever relate to another life of the same kind? His answer is not a route outward through the perceived body of the other. Community lies beneath the individuals, in the one life that all the living share and that gives each of them their own self. Such a community is invisible, like life itself, and it is not opposed to the individual: it is made of living individuals.

**Marx and politics.** In *Marx* (1976) Henry reads Marx as a philosopher of the living individual and of praxis, against a Marxism that, on his account, is the sum of the misreadings of Marx. In his later political writing (*Du communisme au capitalisme*, 1990), politics can only be a mediation for invisible life, never its ground.

**Barbarism and culture.** In *La barbarie* (1987) Henry reads modern culture as life turning against itself: a knowledge that keeps only what can be seen and measured, and leaves out the life that does the knowing. Art, by contrast, is an intensification of life (*Voir l'invisible*, 1988, on Kandinsky).

**Philosophy of Christianity.** From the 1990s Henry read Christianity as a phenomenology of life: God as absolute life, the living as generated in it (*C'est moi la vérité*, 1996; *Incarnation*, 2000; *Paroles du Christ*, 2002).

## Where Henry stops

[Zahavi](/positioning/persons/z/zahavi/) presses the point from inside phenomenology: Henry never shows how a self-presence so complete can be directed at anything other than itself, recognise other subjects or have a bodily outside. Henry's political writing meets the same edge from the other side: the *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry (Frédéric Seyler) names the tension between life as ipseity and public affairs an aporia. Dominique Janicaud's charge of a "theological turn" (1991), naming Henry alongside [Levinas](/positioning/persons/l/levinas/), Marion and Chrétien, targets the method itself, for carrying phenomenology beyond what shows itself as an object.

## Key works

- *L'essence de la manifestation* (1963) — the two ways of appearing; the founding statement of radical immanence
- *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps* (1965) — the subjective body, through Maine de Biran
- *Marx* (1976, two volumes) — Marx read as a philosopher of living individuals and their praxis
- *La barbarie* (1987) — modern culture as life turned against itself
- *Voir l'invisible. Sur Kandinsky* (1988) — abstract painting as the showing of invisible life
- *Phénoménologie matérielle* (1990) — material phenomenology; the essay on community, "Pathos-avec"
- *C'est moi la vérité* (1996) — toward a philosophy of Christianity
- *Incarnation. Une philosophie de la chair* (2000) — flesh as life's self-experience
