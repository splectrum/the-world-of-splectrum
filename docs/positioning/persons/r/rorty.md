# Richard Rorty (1931–2007)

Rorty's pragmatism had a critical side and a positive one. *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) argued against the picture of "the mind as a great mirror" and of knowledge as accurate representation, and against philosophy as the discipline that judges the rest of culture. He took his ends from the later [Wittgenstein](/positioning/persons/w/wittgenstein/), [Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/) and [Dewey](/positioning/persons/d/dewey/), and his means from [Sellars](/positioning/persons/s/sellars/), [Quine](/positioning/persons/q/quine/), [Kuhn](/positioning/persons/k/kuhn/) and, increasingly, Donald Davidson. With the essays of the 1980s and *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989), the work turned to culture and politics: the contingency of language, the liberal ironist, the split between private self-creation and public solidarity, and an avowed ethnocentrism. Readers took the *Mirror* as announcing the end of philosophy, a label he rejected. His treatment of truth drew charges of relativism from realists and from fellow pragmatists, and readers of [Peirce](/positioning/persons/p/peirce/) and Dewey contested his claim to the name of [pragmatism](/positioning/subjects/p/pragmatism/).

[Richard Rorty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/) (1931–2007). Born in New York City, he entered the University of Chicago in 1946, at fifteen, and was taught there by [Rudolf Carnap](/positioning/persons/c/carnap/), Charles Hartshorne and Richard McKeon. His MA thesis (1952), supervised by Hartshorne, was on [Whitehead](/positioning/persons/w/whitehead/). At Yale he wrote a doctoral dissertation, "The Concept of Potentiality" (1956), under the metaphysician Paul Weiss. At Princeton from 1961 he made his name in analytic philosophy, with papers in the philosophy of mind, among them proposals for eliminating the vocabulary of the mental, with papers in metaphilosophy, and with the anthology *The Linguistic Turn* (1967). Increasingly at odds with the Princeton department, he left in 1982 to become Kenan Professor of the Humanities at the University of Virginia. In 1998 he moved to the Department of Comparative Literature at Stanford. He "never ceased to engage with analytic philosophy" (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it). He died on 8 June 2007.

## Key concepts

**The linguistic turn.** His anthology *The Linguistic Turn* (1967) made Gustav Bergmann's phrase current, and its introduction already speaks of a "'post-philosophical' culture" as one possible future (as K. Mom quotes it). By 1979 he judged that the turn changed the medium, not the project: "'Analytic' philosophy is one more variant of Kantian philosophy, a variant marked principally by thinking of representation as linguistic rather than mental … For analytic philosophy is still committed to the construction of a permanent, neutral framework for inquiry, and thus for all of culture" (*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, p. 8).

**The mirror of nature.** "The picture which holds traditional philosophy captive is that of the mind as a great mirror, containing various representations—some accurate, some not—and capable of being studied by pure, nonempirical methods. Without the notion of the mind as mirror, the notion of knowledge as accuracy of representation would not have suggested itself" (*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, p. 12). Without that notion in turn, "the strategy common to Descartes and Kant—getting more accurate representations by inspecting, repairing, and polishing the mirror, so to speak—would not have made sense". The book traces the picture from [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/)'s mental substance and [Locke](/positioning/persons/l/locke/)'s mental processes to [Kant](/positioning/persons/k/kant/)'s notion of philosophy as "a tribunal of pure reason, upholding or denying the claims of the rest of culture" (p. 4). The second part, "Mirroring", joins Quine's attack on the analytic–synthetic distinction to Sellars's attack on the given. Both, he argues, "invoke the same argument", whose "crucial premise" is "that we understand knowledge when we understand the social justification of belief, and thus have no need to view it as accuracy of representation. Once conversation replaces confrontation, the notion of the mind as Mirror of Nature can be discarded" (p. 170). "Explaining rationality and epistemic authority by reference to what society lets us say, rather than the latter by the former, is the essence of what I shall call 'epistemological behaviorism,' an attitude common to Dewey and Wittgenstein" (p. 174). Wittgenstein, Heidegger and Dewey "set aside epistemology and metaphysics as possible disciplines. I say 'set aside' rather than 'argue against'" (p. 6). Their later work is "therapeutic rather than constructive, edifying rather than systematic" (p. 5), and so, "like the writings of the philosophers I most admire", is the book (p. 7).

**Truth and conversation.** The explanations epistemology offers, Rorty writes, aim "to make truth something more than what Dewey called 'warranted assertability': more than what our peers will, ceteris paribus, let us get away with saying" (*Mirror*, p. 176), and he declines them. In 1982 he still drew on [William James](/positioning/persons/j/james/), who defined the true as "the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief": on James's view, Rorty wrote, "true" is "a normative notion, a compliment paid to sentences that seem to be paying their way and that fit in with other sentences which are doing so" (*Consequences of Pragmatism*, p. xxv). Soon after, following Davidson, he came to doubt the point of any theory of truth. "Truth" has important uses, but it does not name a goal over and above justification, and the concept has, in the *Stanford Encyclopedia*'s words, "no deep or substantive criterial content at all". "The pragmatist," he wrote, "does not have a theory of truth, much less a relativistic one" ("Solidarity or Objectivity?", *Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth*, p. 24).

**Hermeneutics and edification.** Rorty distinguishes two roles the philosopher might play: "the informed dilettante, the polypragmatic, Socratic intermediary between various discourses", and "the cultural overseer who knows everyone's common ground" (*Mirror*, p. 317). "The first role is appropriate to hermeneutics, the second to epistemology." Hermeneutics sees discourses as "strands in a possible conversation, a conversation which presupposes no disciplinary matrix which unites the speakers, but where the hope of agreement is never lost so long as the conversation lasts" (p. 318). Philosophy as "a 'voice in the conversation of mankind'" is, he notes, [Oakeshott](/positioning/persons/o/oakeshott/)'s phrase (p. 264). Its understanding is a circle: "we play back and forth between guesses about how to characterize particular statements or other events, and guesses about the point of the whole situation, until gradually we feel at ease with what was hitherto strange" (p. 319). He uses "edification" "to stand for this project of finding new, better, more interesting, more fruitful ways of speaking" (p. 360), and he sets systematic against edifying philosophers: "Great systematic philosophers are constructive and offer arguments. Great edifying philosophers are reactive and offer satires, parodies, aphorisms" (p. 369). "The point of edifying philosophy is to keep the conversation going rather than to find objective truth", truth being "the normal result of normal discourse" (p. 377).

**The end of philosophy.** The *Mirror* ends on philosophy's future: "there is no danger of philosophy's 'coming to an end'", and "philosophers' moral concern should be with continuing the conversation of the West" (p. 394). He later complained that he was "still thought of … as recommending 'the end of philosophy,' despite my explicit rejection of this label on the last page of *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* and my attempts in subsequent writings to scrape it off" (*Truth and Progress*, p. 47 n. 16).

**Philosophy and philosophy.** The introduction to *Consequences of Pragmatism* (1982) separates two senses of the word: "'philosophy,' like 'truth' and 'goodness,' is ambiguous. Uncapitalized, 'truth' and 'goodness' name properties of sentences, or of actions and situations. Capitalized, they are the proper names of objects – goals or standards which can be loved with all one's heart and soul and mind, objects of ultimate concern" (p. xiv). Uncapitalised, philosophy is what Sellars calls "an attempt to see how things, in the broadest possible sense of the term, hang together, in the broadest possible sense of the term", and "No one would be dubious about philosophy, taken in this sense" (p. xiv). Capitalised, it means "following Plato's and Kant's lead, asking questions about the nature of certain normative notions (e.g., 'truth,' 'rationality,' 'goodness') in the hope of better obeying such norms" (pp. xiv–xv). Both traditions, post-positivist analytic philosophy and the line from Nietzsche through Heidegger to Derrida, are "living between a repudiated past and a dimly seen post-Philosophical future" (p. xxi). The question he raises is "whether a culture could get along without Philosophy, without the Platonic attempt to sift out the merely contingent and conventional truths from the Truths which were something more than that" (p. xxxviii).

**Pragmatism.** For Rorty the key pragmatist was Dewey, but he read Dewey selectively, separating a "good" from a "bad" Dewey and criticising *Experience and Nature* as a backslide into metaphysics. Peirce was his least favourite pragmatist (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports). The pragmatist view of truth he shared with James and Dewey was not, he wrote, dependent on Peirce's notion of an "ideal end of inquiry" or on [Habermas](/positioning/persons/h/habermas/)'s of an "ideally free community", both of which he found "insufficiently ethnocentric" (*Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth*, p. 23 n. 1). Readers of the classical pragmatists contest his title to the name. Susan Haack finds it literally unworthy of the name, and Cheryl Misak argues that in abandoning objectivity he gives up the capacity for normative critique (both as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports).

**Irony and contingency.** *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989) sketches the *liberal ironist*. "I use 'ironist' to name the sort of person who faces up to the contingency of his or her own most central beliefs and desires"; liberal ironists "include among these ungroundable desires their own hope that suffering will be diminished" (p. xv). The book opens with the contingency of language: "The world is out there, but descriptions of the world are not" (p. 5); "The world does not speak. Only we do" (p. 6); "languages are made rather than found" (p. 7). Davidson and Wittgenstein, he writes, "treat alternative vocabularies as more like alternative tools than like bits of a jigsaw puzzle" (p. 11). An ironist, in Rorty's definition, fulfils three conditions: she "has radical and continuing doubts about the final vocabulary she currently uses, because she has been impressed by other vocabularies"; she "realizes that argument phrased in her present vocabulary can neither underwrite nor dissolve these doubts"; and "insofar as she philosophizes about her situation, she does not think that her vocabulary is closer to reality than others" (p. 73). Since "there is nothing beyond vocabularies which serves as a criterion of choice between them", criticism "is a matter of looking on this picture and on that, not of comparing both pictures with the original" (p. 80). More generally, he rejects any attempt to draw a general line between what is made and what is found (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it).

**Private and public.** In the same work, Rorty splits two projects that philosophy has tried to unify: private self-creation (irony, personal vocabulary, the pursuit of autonomy) and public solidarity (reducing cruelty, expanding the circle of "us"). His private exemplars are "Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Baudelaire, Proust, Heidegger, and Nabokov", writers who show "what private perfection—a self-created, autonomous, human life—can be like". His public ones are "Marx, Mill, Dewey, Habermas, and Rawls", "fellow citizens rather than exemplars", engaged in "the effort to make our institutions and practices more just and less cruel" (p. xiv). "There is no way to bring self-creation together with justice at the level of theory", and the two kinds of writer are "as little in need of synthesis as are paintbrushes and crowbars" (p. xiv); the book is "content to treat the demands of self-creation and of human solidarity as equally valid, yet forever incommensurable" (p. xv). In "Trotsky and the Wild Orchids" (*Philosophy and Social Hope*) he recalls how he "gradually decided that the whole idea of holding reality and justice in a single vision had been a mistake" (p. 12, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* quotes it).

**Solidarity over objectivity.** People make sense of their lives, Rorty writes in "Solidarity or Objectivity?" (1984), either by telling the story of their contribution to a community or by describing themselves "as standing in immediate relation to a nonhuman reality": "stories of the former kind exemplify the desire for solidarity, and … stories of the latter kind exemplify the desire for objectivity" (*Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth*, p. 21). "For pragmatists, the desire for objectivity is not the desire to escape the limitations of one's community, but simply the desire for as much intersubjective agreement as possible, the desire to extend the reference of 'us' as far as we can" (p. 23). Moral progress, in *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, is the widening of that "us": thinking of more and more different people as "one of us" (p. 191, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports it).

**Liberalism and cultural politics.** Rorty called himself a "postmodernist bourgeois liberal": his liberalism rests on no metanarrative of transcultural rationality, and its institutions are "the lucky result of a contingent history" (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* puts it). He took Judith Shklar's definition: liberals "are the people who think that cruelty is the worst thing we do" (*Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, p. xv). "The Priority of Democracy to Philosophy" (in *Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth*) holds that liberal democracy may need "philosophical articulation" but "does not need philosophical backup"; its philosopher "is putting politics first and tailoring a philosophy to suit" (p. 178). The intellectual's task, for Rorty, is to sensitise readers to suffering, a task he gave above all to novelists, Orwell and Nabokov among them (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports). He came to call such work "cultural politics" (*Philosophy as Cultural Politics*, 2007). *Achieving Our Country* (1998) urged the academic "cultural Left" back to the reformist politics of the earlier American Left.

## Ethnocentrism and the charge of relativism

Rorty called himself an ethnocentrist. "Relativism", he wrote, is used for three views: that every belief is as good as every other; that "true" is an equivocal term; and that there is nothing to be said about truth or rationality apart from the procedures of justification which a given society uses. "The pragmatist holds the ethnocentric third view. But he does not hold the self-refuting first view, nor the eccentric second view." He denies that the third is a relativism: the pragmatist "is not holding a positive theory which says that something is relative to something else" ("Solidarity or Objectivity?", *Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth*, p. 23). "We Western liberal intellectuals should accept the fact that we have to start from where we are, and that this means that there are lots of views which we simply cannot take seriously" (p. 29).

To many readers this looked like cultural relativism. Friendly critics, among them [Putnam](/positioning/persons/p/putnam/), John McDowell and [Dennett](/positioning/persons/d/dennett/), balk at the idea that there are no constraints on knowledge save conversational ones. McDowell holds that a creature with beliefs must be rationally, not merely causally, constrained by the world. Haack sees in the abandonment of epistemology a "vulgar cynicism" (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports these). Habermas argues that Rorty's vocabulary cannot tell "motivating through reason" from "causal exertion of influence", so that critical standards are lost (as the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* reports him). Rorty's strategy, in reply to Putnam, was "to move everything over from epistemology and metaphysics into cultural politics, from claims to knowledge and appeals to self-evidence to suggestions about what we should try" (*Truth and Progress*, p. 57, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* quotes it).

## Key works

- *The Linguistic Turn* (ed., 1967) — the anthology that made the phrase current
- [*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_and_the_Mirror_of_Nature) (1979) — the critique of representationalism
- *Consequences of Pragmatism* (1982) — essays of 1972–1980 on pragmatism and truth; the introduction on Philosophy and philosophy, and a culture without Philosophy
- [*Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingency,_Irony,_and_Solidarity) (1989) — contingency of language, the liberal ironist, private and public
- *Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth* (1991) — Philosophical Papers, vol. 1: solidarity, anti-representationalism, Davidson, the priority of democracy to philosophy
- *Truth and Progress* (1998) — Philosophical Papers, vol. 3
- [*Achieving Our Country*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achieving_Our_Country) (1998) — the reformist Left and the cultural Left in twentieth-century America
- [*Philosophy and Social Hope*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_and_Social_Hope) (1999) — popular essays, with the autobiographical "Trotsky and the Wild Orchids"
- [*Philosophy as Cultural Politics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_as_Cultural_Politics) (2007) — Philosophical Papers, vol. 4: philosophy as cultural politics
