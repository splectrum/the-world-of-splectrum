# Dan Zahavi (1967–)

Zahavi brought classical [phenomenology](/positioning/subjects/p/phenomenology/) into conversation with philosophy of mind and the cognitive sciences, around one question: what is the self, and how do selves meet? His answer starts small — a minimal, experiential self given in every experience — and works outward: to empathy as the direct experience of others, to shame and other emotions in which the self is experienced through others, and to the "we" of shared experience.

[Dan Zahavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Zahavi) (1967–). Danish philosopher, born in Copenhagen. He took his doctorate at KU Leuven in 1994 under Rudolf Bernet. Professor of philosophy at the University of Copenhagen from 2002 and founding director of its Center for Subjectivity Research, established the same year; professor of philosophy at Oxford from 2018 to 2021. Co-editor-in-chief of *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* from 2007.

---

## Key concepts

**The experiential self (for-me-ness).** Every experience has a dimension of "for-me-ness or mineness". It is not a particular content but "the first-personal presence of experience": "the experiences I am living through are given differently (but not necessarily better) to me than to anybody else." To deny it "would be tantamount to a denial of the first-person perspective." This minimal self is pre-reflective: it does not need a second act of reflection to be there.

**A structure that individuates.** An objection holds that first-personal givenness is too formal to individuate anyone, since everybody's experiences have it. Zahavi answers with two clones: from the outside nothing distinguishes their qualitatively identical experiences, but "only my experiences are given in a first-personal mode of presentation to me, whereas the qualitatively identical experiences of my clone are not given first-personally to me at all." The structure is common to every subject; the self it gives is each one's own.

**Empathy as direct perception.** Against the view that we infer or simulate other minds, Zahavi, following [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/), [Stein](/positioning/persons/s/stein/) and Scheler, holds that others' experiences can be directly perceived in their expressions. The access stays asymmetric, and that is the point: "the fact that my experiential access to the minds of others differs from my experiential access to my own mind is not an imperfection or shortcoming. On the contrary, it is a difference that is constitutional." It is what makes the minds we meet other minds: "The otherness of the other is exactly manifest in his elusiveness and inaccessibility."

**Self, other and we.** *Self and Other* (2014) brings together the minimal self, empathy and shame, which he holds can be felt alone but carries an irreducible reference to others. *Being We* (2025) takes up the "we" itself: shared emotions, we-experiences and collective intentionality.

**Readings and disputes.** Against [Michel Henry](/positioning/persons/h/henry/) he has pressed that a self-presence so complete leaves unexplained how other subjects can be recognised. Against the charge, which he attributes to [Habermas](/positioning/persons/h/habermas/), that phenomenology is fundamentally incapable of addressing intersubjectivity, he answers that it is "astonishingly misguided".

**Phenomenology and the sciences of mind.** With Shaun Gallagher, *The Phenomenological Mind* (2008) presents phenomenology as a partner for cognitive science rather than a rival. Zahavi's readings of Husserl (*Husserl's Phenomenology*, 2003) present him as a philosopher of embodiment and intersubjectivity, with [Merleau-Ponty](/positioning/persons/m/merleau-ponty/) continuing rather than breaking with him.

---

## Where Zahavi stops

Zahavi grounds every "we", up to communities whose members never meet, in the face-to-face sharing of the I–you relation. On Zahavi's account, Hans Bernhard Schmid denies that the we is founded on an experience of the other at all; communitarians and social constructivists put the community first, which Zahavi rejects. On the self, no-self critics such as Thomas Metzinger and Miri Albahari deny that there is a self, Albahari while granting that unity, unbrokenness and invariability are real features of consciousness, and relational critics such as Joe Higgins find the minimal self too thin to be the first layer.

## Key works

- *Husserl und die transzendentale Intersubjektivität* (1996) — Husserl's account of intersubjectivity
- *Self-awareness and Alterity* (1999) — self-awareness and the other in phenomenology
- *Husserl's Phenomenology* (2003) — introduction and reading
- *Subjectivity and Selfhood: Investigating the First-Person Perspective* (2005) — the experiential self
- *The Phenomenological Mind* (with Shaun Gallagher, 2008) — phenomenology for the cognitive sciences
- *Self and Other: Exploring Subjectivity, Empathy, and Shame* (2014) — from the minimal self to the we
- *Being We: Phenomenological Contributions to Social Ontology* (2025) — the first-person plural
