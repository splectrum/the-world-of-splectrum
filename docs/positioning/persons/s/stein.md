# Edith Stein (1891–1942)

Stein's doctoral thesis under [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/) is a [phenomenology](/positioning/subjects/p/phenomenology/) of empathy. It asks how one consciousness experiences another, and answers that empathy is a kind of experience in its own right: not inference, not imitation, but a perceiving of another's life that is given to me without being lived by me. From there she shows how a "we" forms without dissolving the "I", and why a shared world depends on it.

[Edith Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Stein) (1891–1942). German philosopher, born in Breslau (now Wrocław) into an observant Jewish family, the youngest of eleven children. She studied under Husserl at Göttingen from 1913, served as a Red Cross nurse in a military hospital in 1915, and took her doctorate at Freiburg in 1916 with the thesis published as *On the Problem of Empathy*. She was Husserl's assistant at Freiburg from 1916 to 1918, transcribing and editing his manuscripts, among them *Ideas II* and the lectures on inner time-consciousness; her attempts to habilitate were blocked. She converted to Catholicism in 1922, taught at a Catholic school in Speyer and later in Münster, from which she was dismissed under the Nazi laws of 1933, and entered the Carmelite convent in Cologne as Teresa Benedicta of the Cross. Moved to the Carmel at Echt in the Netherlands in 1938, she was arrested by the Gestapo in 1942 and murdered at Auschwitz-Birkenau with her sister Rosa. She was canonised in 1998.

## Key concepts

**Empathy as experience.** Empathy (*Einfühlung*) is, in Waltraut Stein's translation, "a kind of act of perceiving sui generis": "the experience of foreign consciousness in general". It is not my own experience copied onto another, and not a conclusion drawn from behaviour. What makes it distinct is its double structure: "Empathy in our strictly defined sense as the experience of foreign consciousness can only be the non-primordial experience which announces a primordial one." I live the empathising act now and originally; what it gives me, the other's joy or pain, is lived originally only by the other.

**The we that keeps the I.** Shared experience does not abolish the individual. Asking whether, in feeling together, the barriers between one "I" and another break down, Stein answers: "Not entirely… from the 'I' and 'you' arises the 'we' as a subject of a higher level." The shared feeling is richer than any single one — "we empathically enrich our feeling so that 'we' now feel a different joy from 'I,' 'you,' and 'he' in isolation" — and yet "'I,' 'you,' and 'he' are retained in 'we'."

**Empathy and the world.** Empathy is also the condition of knowing a world that is more than my own appearance of it: "Were I imprisoned within the boundaries of my individuality, I could not go beyond 'the world as it appears to me'". Seeing the world as others see it, from their places, is what lets it stand as one world: "empathy as the basis of intersubjective experience becomes the condition of possible knowledge of the existing outer world."

**Community, people and state.** In two treatises in Husserl's *Jahrbuch* Stein carried the analysis past the meeting of persons. A community's experiences can outlast its members, extending "über ganze Generationen von Individuen" (over whole generations of individuals); a people shares a common world of tales, morals, law and poetry; and what makes a community is that its members are "nicht aufeinander gerichtet, sondern gemeinsam einem Gegenständlichen zugewendet": not directed at one another, but turned together toward something objective ("Individual and Community", 1922). In *An Investigation Concerning the State* (1925), communal life develops fixed forms that different individuals can fill in succession, and the state rests on its own sovereignty rather than on society or community alone.

**Phenomenology and Thomism.** After her conversion Stein translated [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) and set phenomenology in dialogue with scholastic philosophy, culminating in *Finite and Eternal Being*, written in the Cologne Carmel from 1935 and published posthumously: an account of finite being as open to eternal being.

## Where Stein stops

Stein's account of community reaches far past the face-to-face. What she holds fixed throughout is that the constituting consciousness stays individual. Communal experiences have their origin, in the end, in the individual I's that belong to the community: "Ein Gemeinschaftssubjekt als Analogon des reinen Ich besteht nicht" (there is no community subject analogous to the pure I). After her conversion, as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports, she came to criticise Husserl's transcendental philosophy for seeking objectivity from within subjectivity, and her later ontology starts from being instead.

## Key works

- *On the Problem of Empathy* (*Zum Problem der Einfühlung*, 1917) — empathy as the experience of foreign consciousness; tr. Waltraut Stein
- *Philosophy of Psychology and the Humanities* (*Beiträge zur philosophischen Begründung der Psychologie und der Geisteswissenschaften*, 1922) — psychic causality; individual and community
- *An Investigation Concerning the State* (*Eine Untersuchung über den Staat*, 1925) — the state as sovereignty, beyond society and community
- *Finite and Eternal Being* (*Endliches und ewiges Sein*, written from 1935, published 1950) — phenomenology and Thomism; finite being open to eternal being
- *The Science of the Cross* (*Kreuzeswissenschaft*, written 1941–42, published 1950) — on John of the Cross; her last work, left unfinished at her arrest
