# Phenomenology

The study of experience from the inside. Phenomenology does not ask what the world is made of — it asks how the world shows up for a subject. Founded by [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/) as a rigorous science of consciousness, it became one of the dominant movements of twentieth-century philosophy, branching into existentialism, hermeneutics, and the philosophy of embodiment.

---

## The core move

Bracket the natural attitude — the everyday assumption that the world exists independently of experience — and return to what is given in experience itself. This is the *epoché*, Husserl's phenomenological reduction. What remains is not nothing; it is the entire field of experience with its own structures, temporality, and logic. The aim is not to deny the world but to understand how it is constituted for the subject.

## Key developments

**[Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/)** established the foundations: intentionality (consciousness is always consciousness *of* something), time-consciousness (retention, protention, the flow of lived time), the lifeworld as the pre-theoretical ground beneath all science. His work on intersubjectivity — how other subjects are experienced, not inferred — opened the questions that his successors pursued in different directions.

**[Stein](/positioning/persons/s/stein/)** gave empathy its phenomenology in her thesis under Husserl: the experience of another's consciousness, given to me without being lived by me, and a "we" that keeps the "I" and the "you".

**[Heidegger](/positioning/persons/h/heidegger/)** radicalised the project. Phenomenology becomes the question of Being. The subject is not consciousness but Dasein — the being that is always already in a world, always already understanding, always already involved. The reduction is replaced by disclosure: the world is not bracketed but revealed through engagement.

**[Merleau-Ponty](/positioning/persons/m/merleau-ponty/)** placed the body at the centre. Perception is not a mental act applied to sense data — it is the body's lived engagement with its surroundings. The habitual body carries its history forward; the world arrives already structured by the body's orientation within it. The other is met through the body and in dialogue, where "there is constituted between the other person and myself a common ground". His late work on flesh and chiasm dissolves the subject-object boundary entirely.

**[Sartre](/positioning/persons/s/sartre/)** took phenomenology into existentialism. Consciousness is pure intentionality — always directed outward, always free, with no fixed essence behind it. "Existence precedes essence": we are not defined by what we are but by what we do. The other's look turns me into an object: the other is met, not constituted, and the relation is conflict. His divergence from Merleau-Ponty — radical freedom versus embodied ambiguity — defined two paths within French phenomenology.

**[Jonas](/positioning/persons/j/jonas/)** extended phenomenology into biology. The organism is where selfhood begins — metabolism as the first act of self-constitution, the boundary maintained from within. Phenomenology meets the philosophy of life.

**[Levinas](/positioning/persons/l/levinas/)** reversed the order of the tradition: ethics before ontology. The face of the Other is not constituted by me; it commands, and language institutes the common world between us.

**[Henry](/positioning/persons/h/henry/)** turned phenomenology toward an appearing that never enters the world: life as self-revelation, radical immanence, with community lying beneath the individuals.

**[Zahavi](/positioning/persons/z/zahavi/)** carries the tradition into conversation with philosophy of mind and the cognitive sciences: the experiential self, empathy as direct perception of other minds, and the "we" of shared experience.

## The tradition's reach

Phenomenology is not a doctrine but a method — and the method has been taken into psychology (the qualitative study of lived experience), psychiatry (the phenomenology of mental illness), sociology ([Schutz](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schutz/)'s lifeworld analysis), cognitive science (the enactivist programme), and the philosophy of technology. What holds it together is the commitment to starting from experience as it is lived, not as it is theorised.

---

## Where phenomenology stops

On the other and the shared world the tradition divides rather than concludes. Husserl holds that the other is experienced, not inferred, constitutes that experience from the ego's own sphere and declares solipsism dissolved; Stein and Zahavi, with him on empathy, describe a "we" that keeps its "I"s; Heidegger makes being-with as original as the self; Merleau-Ponty holds solitude and communication as two moments of one phenomenon; Sartre and Levinas deny that the other is constituted at all; Henry places community beneath the individuals. From within, Alfred Schutz argued that the other cannot be constituted in the transcendental sphere and that intersubjectivity is a given of the everyday life-world; from outside, [Habermas](/positioning/persons/h/habermas/), as Zahavi reads him, charged phenomenology with solipsism and moved the foundation of intersubjectivity to language. Dominique Janicaud's critique of a "theological turn" (1991), naming Henry, Levinas, Marion and Chrétien, disputes how far the method can stretch. What the tradition holds in common is the starting point, experience as it is lived; whether a shared world has to be reached from there, or is there from the first, is what it disputes. Other disputes run alongside: the realist phenomenologists of Husserl's Göttingen circle against his transcendental idealism, [Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/)'s critique of presence, and whether phenomenology can be naturalised in the sciences of mind.
