# Two Traditions

In the twentieth century philosophy divided a second time, not by subject but by tradition: "analytic" and "continental". What each takes philosophy to be differs, and so does what each takes the other to be doing. One reading, David Cooper's (1996), has continental philosophy marked by its attack on scientism; Glendinning argues against it.

---

## A common root

[Michael Dummett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Dummett) (*Origins of Analytical Philosophy*, 1993): "The roots of analytical philosophy are the same roots as those of the phenomenological school." He found [Frege](/positioning/persons/f/frege/) and [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/) "remarkably close in orientation despite some divergence of interests", and compared the two traditions with "the Rhine and the Danube, which rise quite close to one another and for a time pursue roughly parallel courses, only to diverge in utterly different directions and flow to different seas". For him the fork is language: "Analytical philosophy was born when the linguistic turn was taken." In an interview he said: "we have reached a point at which it is as if we're working in different subjects".

---

## Is there a continental tradition?

[Simon Glendinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Glendinning) (*The Idea of Continental Philosophy*, 2006) finds no "internal glue" for a continental tradition. "Continental philosophy" works as analytic philosophy's Other, defined by contrasts: argument against rhetoric, clear against obscure, rigorous against vague.

On science he answers Cooper directly. The scientism/anti-scientism contrast "does specify the most profound philosophical division in the sensibility of the contemporary philosophical culture", but "it cuts orthogonally across the standard analytic/Continental distinction": reverence for science is a target of some of the most influential analytic work, and the wish for the status of a science runs through important continental texts.

Cooper's reading has a voice on the physicists' side too: [Rovelli](/positioning/persons/r/rovelli/) in 2018 complained of "the post-Heideggerian atmosphere that dominates some philosophy departments on the 'continent', [where] ignorance of science is something to exhibit with pride".

[Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/) (1978), quoted by Glendinning, saw what the breakdown puts at stake: it can "make one doubt the unity of the philosophical, of the concept or project behind the word philosophy, which then constantly risks being but a homonymic lure".

## The quarrels

- **1979.** At the Eastern Division meeting of the American Philosophical Association, with [Rorty](/positioning/persons/r/rorty/) presiding, a Committee for Pluralism in Philosophy ran its own candidate against the dominance of analytic philosophy, and John E. Smith beat Adolf Grünbaum 198 to 165. The complaint, as the *New York Times* reported it, was that the association had become "monolithic" and "intolerant".
- **1992.** A Cambridge proposal to give Derrida an honorary degree drew a letter to *The Times* from Barry Smith and eighteen others, among them [Quine](/positioning/persons/q/quine/). Its ground was a boundary between disciplines: Derrida's influence had been "almost entirely in fields outside philosophy", and "in the eyes of philosophers" his work "does not meet accepted standards of clarity and rigour". After a ballot of the university, the degree was granted. The [deconstruction](/positioning/subjects/d/deconstruction/) subject tells the affair in full.
