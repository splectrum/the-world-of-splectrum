# Philosophy in China

No classical Chinese word matches "philosophy". *Zhexue*, the modern word, is [Nishi Amane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi_Amane)'s Japanese coinage *tetsugaku*, taken into Chinese at the end of the nineteenth century. In the texts below, learning (*xue*), the word the *Analects* opens its first saying on, names the activity, and the Way (*dao*) what it seeks; the two join in *Daoxue*, "the Learning of the Way", the name of the Song movement. [Feng Youlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_Youlan): "The term Neo-Confucianism is a newly coined western equivalent for Tao hsüeh." The thinkers were filed as *jia*, houses or lineages, which translators render "schools", and as *zhuzi*, the masters, a library category set apart from the classics.

The founding modern histories were Hu Shi's (1919) and Feng's (1931–34), and Feng stated his method plainly: to select from the kinds of learning in Chinese history those that could be named by what the West calls philosophy. Reviewing Feng's, the logician Jin Yuelin asked whether such a book is a history of Chinese philosophy or a history of philosophy in China, philosophy discovered in China by a Western measure; he found both books on the second road. Whether there is a Chinese philosophy at all has been argued from [Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/)'s verdict on [Confucius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucius) to the debate over its "legitimacy" around 2000 (below). The title of this page follows the series in naming a place; it is also Jin's name for the second road.

The masters argued with one another, and later stages took up earlier ones. Each gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## Confucius

**What it is.** Learning. The *Analects* opens: "Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application?" (1.1, tr. Legge; "constant perseverance and application" expands "practise at the proper times"). Knowing includes knowing its limits: "When you know a thing, to hold that you know it; and when you do not know a thing, to allow that you do not know it;-- this is knowledge" (2.17).

**Its point.** Learning for oneself: "In ancient times, men learned with a view to their own improvement. Now-a-days, men learn with a view to the approbation of others" (14.24; literally "for themselves … for others"). The aim is the *junzi*, "gentleman" or, in Legge, "superior man"; the sage is the horizon: "The sage and the man of perfect virtue;-- how dare I rank myself with them? It may simply be said of me, that I strive to become such without satiety, and teach others without weariness" (7.34).

**How it is done.** "Learning without thought is labour lost; thought without learning is perilous" (2.15). But thinking alone was "of no use. The better plan is to learn" (15.31). Confucius calls himself "a transmitter and not a maker, believing in and loving the ancients" (7.1), "not one who was born in the possession of knowledge" but "fond of antiquity, and earnest in seeking it there" (7.20).

## The Mohists

**What it is.** Disputation (*bian*). In the Mohist "Lesser Selection", by it "we clarify divisions between this (*shì*) and not-this (*fēi*) to examine the guidelines of order and disorder; clarify points of sameness and difference to discern the patterns of names (*míng*) and stuff (*shí*); and settle benefit and harm to resolve uncertainty and doubt" (tr. Fraser).

**Its point.** In the same passage, to settle benefit and harm, and order and disorder. Speech is tested by what it does for "the country and the people".

**How it is done.** By standards. "None has yet accomplished anything without standards", the book of [Mozi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozi) on method begins (tr. Mei). Every claim has three: its basis in "the deeds of the ancient sage-kings", its verification "by the senses of hearing and sight of the common people", and its application, "adopting it in government and observing its benefits to the country and the people" (*Mozi* 35). Speaking without a standard is "similar to determining the directions of sunrise and sunset on a revolving potter's wheel". And disputation has a winner: when one calls a thing "ox" and the other "not-ox", the Mohist canons hold, both cannot fit, and the side whose claim fits wins; to say that disputation has no winner does not fit (A74, B35). The *Zhuangzi*'s "who shall adjudicate?" takes the opposite view; see [Zhuangzi](/positioning/persons/z/zhuangzi/).

## Mencius and Xunzi

**What it is.** [Mencius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mencius): "He who has exhausted all his mental constitution knows his nature. Knowing his nature, he knows Heaven" (7A1, tr. Legge). [Xunzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xunzi) sees the rival masters as each blinkered by one corner of the great pattern: Mozi by utility, Huizi by words, Zhuangzi by Heaven, which Dubs renders "Nature"; Confucius alone is not blinkered ("Dispelling Obsession", ch. 21).

**Its point.** Mencius: "The great end of learning is nothing else but to seek for the lost mind" (6A11); people go after their lost fowls and dogs, but not after their lost mind. Xunzi: learning "begins by reciting the Classics and ends in learning the rites. Its purpose begins with making the scholar, and ends in making the sage" ("Encouraging Learning", ch. 1, tr. Dubs).

**How it is done.** "The superior man says: Study should never stop" (Xunzi, ch. 1). Mencius disputes only because he cannot help it, to defend the former sages against Yang Zhu and Mozi (3B9), and reads with care: "It would be better to be without the Book of History than to give entire credit to it" (7B3). Xunzi's remedy for the one-sided is a state of the heart-mind, empty, unified and still.

## The Daoists

**What it is.** The [*Daodejing*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao_Te_Ching) opens on what cannot be said: "The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name" (1, tr. Legge). The second *dao* is a verb, "to tread" for Legge and "to speak" for others.

**How it is done.** By subtraction: "He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to increase (his knowledge); he who devotes himself to the Tao (seeks) from day to day to diminish (his doing)" (48; the bracketed words are Legge's). The two words the later tradition joined, *xue* and *dao*, stand here against each other. On disputation without an arbiter and the fish trap of words, see [Zhuangzi](/positioning/persons/z/zhuangzi/).

## The schools surveyed

**What it is.** The last chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, "Tianxia" ("All under Heaven"), not by Zhuangzi himself, gives an early native account of what the masters were doing. It sets the art of the Way (*daoshu*), which the ancients had and which is everywhere, against the particular methods (*fangshu*) of its own day, each held to be beyond improvement. Like ears, eyes, nose and mouth, each school has its own clarity and none connects with the others; each is a scholar of one corner. The hundred schools go off and do not return, and the art of the Way is about to be torn apart.

**Its point.** The same chapter names the way that has grown dark: "sageliness within and kingliness without" (*neisheng waiwang*), the phrase Feng would make the task of philosophy. The historian Sima Tan (in the *Shiji*) quotes the commentary on the *Book of Changes* on one aim and a hundred deliberations: all six schools work at bringing about order (*zhi*), differing in the road their words take.

**How it is done.** The imperial bibliographer Liu Xin (in the *Hanshu*) has each school take up one end and exalt what it is good at; taken together, with their blind spots, they are branches of the Six Classics.

## Chan

**How it is done.** Chan Buddhism is known by four phrases: "a special transmission outside the scriptures …; not established upon words and letters …; directly pointing to the human heartmind …; seeing nature and becoming a Buddha" (tr. Hershock). In Peter Hershock's account the four were the phrasing "that came to be used in Song dynasty China to characterize Chan distinctiveness", not a saying of its legendary founder, and the "anti-philosophical" reading is a reading: Chan literature is full of "references to canonical texts". Feng's example: asked "What is the First Principle?", Wenyi answered "If I were to tell you, it would become the second principle."

## The Learning of the Way

**What it is.** *Daoxue*, which at its outset aimed, in Justin Tiwald's summary, "to shift the emphasis of formal education away from literary attainments and refocus it on the moral Way". It divided into the Learning of Pattern (*Lixue*) of the Cheng brothers and [Zhu Xi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Xi) and the Learning of Heart-mind (*Xinxue*) of Lu Xiangshan and [Wang Yangming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Yangming). *Lixue* is the word Chinese scholars had matched with the Jesuits' *philosophia* in the seventeenth century.

**Its point.** Sagehood, which can be learned. Zhou Dunyi's *Tongshu* asks whether sagehood can be learned, and answers that it can (ch. 20). The *Great Learning*: "all must consider the cultivation of the person the root of everything besides" (tr. Legge).

**How it is done.** The *Great Learning* runs back from an ordered world to a cultivated person, a rectified heart, sincere thoughts and extended knowledge, and "Such extension of knowledge lay in the investigation of things" (*gewu zhizhi*). Zhu Xi, in Kirill Thompson's account, "adapted and distilled the methodology of investigating things to extend knowledge", reading and classical study joined with "practical grasp of and personal resonance with" nature and society; his reading of the canon became the basis of the civil service examination in 1313. Wang Yangming set out with a friend to investigate the bamboos in front of a pavilion and fell ill. He taught the unity of knowing and acting (*zhixing heyi*): to know and not act is not yet to know. "My current teaching regarding the unity of knowing and acting is a medicine directed precisely at this disease" (*Chuanxilu*, tr. Van Norden), the disease of those who think "one must first know and only then can one act". In the Qing the dispute between Han learning and Song learning was, for Feng, "really one as between the philosophical and scholarly interpretation of the ancient texts. The scholarly interpretation emphasized what it believed was their actual meaning; the philosophical interpretation emphasized what it believed they ought to have meant".

## The modern question

**What it is.** Feng's own definition (*A Short History of Chinese Philosophy*, 1948): "what I call philosophy is systematic, reflective thinking on life". In 1958 four New Confucians, among them [Mou Zongsan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mou_Zongsan) and Tang Junyi, issued a manifesto to the world on Chinese culture. Its account: the Chinese past had no view that a philosopher must build a system of thought by himself and set it out wholly in writing; philosophy, science, religion, politics and ethics shared one cultural source.

**Its point.** Feng: the task of philosophy is to enable man to develop the character of "sageliness within and kingliness without"; "Philosophy is not simply something to be known, but is also something to be experienced", and its function "is not the increase of positive knowledge of matters of fact, but the elevation of the mind". For the manifesto, the learning of heart-mind and nature (*xinxing zhi xue*) is the core of Chinese thought.

**How it is done.** Feng: Chinese philosophers wrote in "aphorisms, apothegms, or allusions", and what they lack in articulateness "is compensated for … by their suggestiveness". The manifesto: the works aim at few and essential words, careless of fixing definitions and building arguments, and cannot be understood apart from the philosopher's whole person and whole life.

## Is there a Chinese philosophy?

[Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/) judged Confucius "a moralist as such, not actually a philosopher". In 1999–2000 Zheng Jiadong raised the "legitimacy" of Chinese philosophy, aiming at the method of interpretation since Hu Shi. In 2001, in conversation in China, [Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/) said "中国没有哲学，只有思想", which can be read as "China has no philosophy, only thought" or "no philosophy, but it does have thought"; and Zheng and others paired his remark with the legitimacy question.

Carine Defoort ("Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Philosophy?", 2001) finds the question "seldom explicitly stated", though implicit answers dominate institutions; denial is "primarily, though not exclusively, implicit and Western", affirmation "most explicitly represented in contemporary China". She sorts four positions:

1. **It does not exist.** There is no Chinese philosophy, on historical or theoretical grounds.
2. **It exists.** Feng Youlan's history is the founding case.
3. **Redefine the word.** Widen the content of "philosophy" so that the masters fall within it.
4. **Question the project.** "Why should China even have had a philosophy?"

Her paradox: "Chinese philosophy" "seems to have died of its own birth", the masters' tradition having largely ended just as the modern discipline named it.
