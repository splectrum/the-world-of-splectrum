# Philosophy in India

No Sanskrit word matches "philosophy". *Darśana*, "view", from the root "to see", is the word modern Indian languages use to translate it, but in the classical doxographies it named a school's view, and the philosophers themselves, the historian [Wilhelm Halbfass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Halbfass) notes, "hardly ever use it" for what they do; reading it as "right vision" or "realization" "is a symptomatic innovation of Neo-Hinduism". *Ānvīkṣikī*, "investigation", is for Halbfass a method more than a field. The philosophers' own words for what they did, he notes, were *tantra*, *mata*, *vāda*, *siddhānta* and *śāstra*; several texts below open on *vidyā*, knowledge, or *jijñāsā*, the desire to know.

Whether India had philosophy has been argued since the nineteenth century. Hermann Jacobi equated *ānvīkṣikī* with philosophy; Paul Hacker (1958) rejected the equation, concluding that Hindu culture "knew the concept of philosophy, but no word which expressed this concept fully and exclusively". Halbfass (*India and Europe*, 1988) finds in India "constructive metaphysics as well as critical epistemology", with a reflexivity "fully commensurable with what we find in Europe", while neither *darśana* nor *ānvīkṣikī* "has played a role in India which would be historically comparable to the role of 'philosophy' in Europe".

The schools below, Buddhist, Jaina and materialist as well as Vedic, with some texts in Pāli, ran side by side and argued with one another for centuries, more than they followed one another. Each gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

## The Upaniṣads

**What it is.** The Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad opens on "the knowledge of Brahman, the foundation of all knowledge" (1.1.1). Its question is "Sir, what is that through which, if it is known, everything else becomes known?", and its answer: "Two kinds of knowledge must be known … the higher and the lower knowledge". The lower is the four Vedas and the auxiliary sciences, grammar and astronomy among them; "the higher knowledge is that by which the Indestructible (Brahman) is apprehended" (1.1.3–5, tr. Müller).

**Its point.** In the Chāndogya, Nārada lists all he has learned and says: "with all this I know the Mantras only … I do not know the Self. I have heard … that he who knows the Self overcomes grief. I am in grief" (7.1.2–3).

**How it is done.** With a teacher: having examined the worlds gained by works, and since "nothing that is eternal (not made) can be gained by what is not eternal (made)", the seeker is to "take fuel in his hand and approach a Guru who is learned and dwells entirely in Brahman" (Muṇḍaka 1.2.12). In the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, "the Self is to be seen, to be heard, to be perceived, to be marked" (2.4.5). The three steps the later tradition took from this verse, hearing (*śravaṇa*), reflection by reasoning (*manana*) and meditation (*nididhyāsana*), are [Śaṅkara](/positioning/persons/s/sankara/)'s gloss in his commentary.

## The Buddha and the Jina

**Its point.** In the Cūḷamālukya Sutta (MN 63) Māluṅkyaputta asks whether the world is eternal, whether it is finite, whether soul and body are one, whether a realised one exists after death. The Buddha leaves these "undeclared", "because they aren't beneficial or relevant to the fundamentals of the spiritual life. They don't lead to disillusionment, dispassion, cessation, peace, insight, awakening, and extinguishment". What he declares instead is suffering, its origin, its cessation and the practice that leads there. Whatever the view held, "there is rebirth, there is old age, there is death, and there is sorrow … And it is the defeat of these things in this very life that I advocate" (tr. Sujato). The Jaina *Tattvārthasūtra*, the oldest extant Jaina treatise in Sanskrit, opens: "Right belief, (right) knowledge, (right) conduct, these (together constitute) the path to liberation" (1.1, tr. Jaini). Marie-Hélène Gorisse renders the first term "right view (*darśana*)" and glosses the path as liberation "not by modifying one's environment, but by modifying oneself".

**How it is done.** The Buddha's image is the arrow: a man struck by a poisoned arrow who will not have it removed until he knows who shot it, of what clan, with what bow, "would still not have learned these things, and meanwhile they'd die". The Jaina answer is the standpoint: knowledge is attained through the means of knowing and through *naya*, "a stand-point which gives partial knowledge of a thing in some particular aspect of it" (1.6). In the doctrine of standpoints (*nayavāda*), Gorisse reports, Jaina philosophers came to explain the divergences between philosophical systems "as resulting from an initial choice of an epistemic perspective", each rival system "the erroneous radicalization of a perspective".

## The investigative science

**What it is.** Kauṭilya's [*Arthaśāstra*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra) counts four sciences: investigation (*ānvīkṣikī*), the three Vedas, economics and government. Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Lokāyata, the materialists, make up investigation, which examines with reasons what is right and wrong in the others (1.2.10–11), and it "has always been considered as a source of light for all sciences, an instrument for all activities, a foundation for all religious and social duties" (1.2.12, tr. Halbfass). For Halbfass this makes *ānvīkṣikī* "not a special 'science' … but a methodology which these other sciences themselves can and should utilize". The same verse is reused for Nyāya itself in Vātsyāyana's commentary.

**Its point.** The *Sāṃkhyakārikā* opens: "The inquiry is into the means of precluding the three sorts of pain; for pain is embarrassment: nor is the inquiry superfluous because obvious means of alleviation exist, for absolute and final relief is not thereby accomplished" (1, tr. Colebrooke); its first word for the inquiry is *jijñāsā*. The *Nyāyasūtra* begins by making true knowledge of its categories the way to the highest good (1.1.1), and release (*apavarga*) the end of a chain running from false knowledge to suffering (1.1.2). The materialists, as their opponents report them, denied any such end: in Mādhava's fourteenth-century survey of the schools, for the Cārvāka "the only Liberation is the dissolution of the body", and "the only end of man is enjoyment produced by sensual pleasures" (tr. Cowell and Gough).

**How it is done.** Sāṃkhya rejects Vedic ritual as "like the temporal one, ineffectual", and prefers "a discriminative knowledge of perceptible principles, and of the imperceptible one, and of the thinking soul" (2). The *Nyāyasūtra* names four means of knowing: perception, inference, comparison and testimony (1.1.3), and lists among its categories the forms of debate. Vātsyāyana defines investigation as "examination of objects by the *pramāṇa*s". The Cārvāka, in Mādhava's report, held "that perception is the only source of knowledge" and did not allow inference.

## The *pramāṇa* debate

**What it is.** The Buddhist school of [Dignāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dign%C4%81ga) and [Dharmakīrti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmakirti) took right knowledge (*samyagjñāna*) as its subject. Dharmakīrti defines a means of knowing as non-deceptive cognition, its reliability shown in what it lets one do (*Pramāṇavārttika*).

**Its point.** The *Nyāyabindu* opens: "All successful human action is preceded by right knowledge. Therefore this (knowledge will be here) investigated" (1.1, tr. Stcherbatsky); "successful human action" renders *puruṣārtha*, human aim. The commentator Dharmottara gives the reason negatively: no reasonable man would "undertake an enquiry about the teeth of the crow, because this would serve no purpose". For [Nāgārjuna](/positioning/persons/n/nagarjuna/), centuries earlier, the point of the teaching is the relinquishing of views: the victors, he writes, called emptiness the relinquishing of all views, and those for whom emptiness is itself a view incurable (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 13.8).

**How it is done.** Against Nyāya's four means of knowing, "Right knowledge is twofold": perception and inference (*Nyāyabindu* 1.2–3). Nāgārjuna answers a critic who holds him to the means of knowing that he has no thesis (*pratijñā*), and so the fault charged is not his (*Vigrahavyāvartanī* 29). The dispute over his method is on [Madhyamaka](/positioning/subjects/m/madhyamaka/).

## The inquiry into dharma and into Brahman

**What it is.** Two schools of Vedic exegesis open on the same word. The *Mīmāṃsāsūtra* begins "athāto dharmajijñāsā", "then therefore the enquiry into duty", and the *Brahmasūtra* "athāto brahmajijñāsā", "then therefore the enquiry into Brahman"; G. Thibaut notes the more literal sense, "the desire of knowing Brahman".

**Its point.** Śaṅkara separates the two by their fruit. Knowledge of dharma yields prosperity and depends on acts to be performed; knowledge of Brahman yields the highest good and depends on none. The study is begun "with a view to freeing one's self from that wrong notion which is the cause of all evil and attaining thereby the knowledge of the absolute unity of the Self" (tr. Thibaut).

**How it is done.** Not everyone may begin. Śaṅkara names the preconditions: "the discrimination of what is eternal and what is non-eternal; the renunciation of all desire to enjoy the fruit (of one's actions) both here and hereafter; the acquirement of tranquillity, self-restraint, and the other means, and the desire of final release" (*Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* 1.1.1). Reasoning serves scripture: "As the thoughts of man are altogether unfettered, reasoning which disregards the holy texts and rests on individual opinion only has no proper foundation", and "even men of the most undoubted mental eminence, such as Kapila, Kaṇāda, and other founders of philosophical schools, have contradicted one another" (2.1.11). Reasoning favoured by scripture is admitted as an aid to *anubhava*, experience (2.1.6), which Thibaut translates "intuition".

## Commentary and the new reason

**How it is done.** From the root text through commentary, subcommentary and treatise, the tradition worked by layers. Jonardon Ganeri (*The Lost Age of Reason: Philosophy in Early Modern India 1450–1700*, 2011) reads the layering as a method that continues "the process of revision and adjustment until a state of reflective equilibrium is reached": "reading philosophically is a way of thinking philosophically". The new logic of the period, Navya-Nyāya, built in Ganeri's words "a precise technical language".

## The modern question

**What it is.** [Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvepalli_Radhakrishnan) wrote in the introduction to his *Indian Philosophy* (1923), on its "General Characteristics": "Philosophy in India is essentially spiritual." The reading was contested from within. [B. K. Matilal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimal_Krishna_Matilal) (*Perception*, 1986): "Too often the term 'Indian philosophy' is identified with a subject that is presented as mystical and non-argumentative … A corrective to this view is long overdue." Daya Krishna counted the reduction of Indian philosophy to a spiritual, liberation-centred endeavour among his "Three Myths about Indian Philosophy". The dispute has a European prehistory in [Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/)'s verdict: as Halbfass quotes him, India's liberation was "only an abstract and negative liberation", and "The real philosophy begins only in the Occident".

**Its point.** [K. C. Bhattacharyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna_Chandra_Bhattacharya)'s address "Svaraj in Ideas", given at Hooghly College during 1928–30 and published in 1954, names a "subtler domination exercised in the sphere of ideas by one culture on another": "There is cultural subjection only when one's traditional cast of ideas and sentiments is superseded without comparison or competition by a new cast representing an alien culture which possesses one like a ghost. This subjection is slavery of the spirit". It is "through philosophy that we can methodically attempt to discover" the soul of India.

**How it is done.** Bhattacharyya again: "If the Modern Indian Mind is to philosophise at all to any purpose, it has to confront Eastern thought and Western thought with one another and attempt a synthesis or a reasoned rejection of either". And with a limit on the comparison: "Thought or reason may be universal, but ideas are carved out of it differently by different cultures … No idea of one cultural language can exactly be translated in another cultural language".
