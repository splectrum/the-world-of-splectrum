# Indigenous Voices Today

In the Andes, the Quechua word for knowing is *yachay*. In Javier Hernández's summary of Zenón Depaz's reading of the seventeenth-century Huarochirí manuscript, it is knowing "en tanto experiencia integral. No se trata de un conocimiento puramente teórico, sino de un modo de estar en el mundo que articula verdad, bien y belleza. Yachay implica aprender, enseñar, cuidar y actuar adecuadamente": knowing as integral experience, not a purely theoretical knowledge but a way of being in the world that joins truth, good and beauty; *yachay* means learning, teaching, caring and acting rightly (2026). The Swiss philosopher Josef Estermann, who calls this thought *filosofía andina*, puts relationality first: "La relacionalidad es la característica principal de la Filosofía Andina, su axioma directriz", relationality is the main characteristic of Andean philosophy, its guiding axiom (2025). Whether it should be called philosophy is disputed. For Estermann "lo que es 'filosofía', no se puede definir monoculturalmente (desde una sola cultura), sino solo en el diálogo (o 'polílogo') intercultural", what philosophy is cannot be defined from a single culture, only in intercultural dialogue or "polylogue" (2018, as quoted by Hernández). The Peruvian philosopher Mario Mejía Huamán answered that philosophy is something to do, not something to be granted: "haremos filosofía para resolver nuestros problemas teóricos … No nos interesa que nos levanten artificialmente, nos pongan a su nivel y nos den la palabra", we will do philosophy to solve our theoretical problems; we are not interested in being raised up artificially, put on their level and given the floor (2005, as quoted by Hernández).

The Mapuche word is *kimün*. For Juan Ñanculef Huaiquinao, a Mapuche *kimche*, a sage, "el kimün es la ciencia del conocimiento mapuche, la base de todo", *kimün* is the science of Mapuche knowledge, the basis of everything, and the whole does not divide: "Tanto la lengua mapuche, como la filosofía, el pensamiento, la religiosidad y la cultura mapuche, constituyen un gran todo inseparable, que no se puede dividir, ni separar sus contenidos por materia, y menos en ramos a estudiar o aprender como lo hacen desde occidente", Mapuche language, philosophy, thought, religiosity and culture form one great inseparable whole, which cannot be divided by subject matter, still less into school subjects as the West does. Thinking is *rakizuam*, from *raki*, to count: "la forma ordenada y lógica de la acción del pensamiento", the ordered and logical form of the act of thought. The method is *inarrumen*, slow observation, and "la filosofía mapuche es pragmática", Mapuche philosophy is pragmatic: "Nosotros no aprendemos 'cómo hacer las cosas', sino que simplemente aprendemos a 'hacer las cosas'", we do not learn how to do things, we simply learn to do things (*Tayiñ mapuche kimün*, 2016).

The aim of a good life entered two constitutions. Bolivia's (2009) takes as ethical principles of a plural society *suma qamaña*, living well, beside *ñandereko*, harmonious life, and *ivi maraei*, land without evil; Ecuador's (2008) seeks "el buen vivir, el sumak kawsay", good living, in "armonía con la naturaleza", harmony with nature, and sets *saberes ancestrales*, ancestral knowledge, beside science: its national system of science, technology, innovation and ancestral knowledge is to "Recuperar, fortalecer y potenciar los saberes ancestrales", to recover, strengthen and develop ancestral knowledge, alongside generating scientific and technological knowledge (Art. 385). Writers of what Antonio Luis Hidalgo-Capitán and Ana Patricia Cubillo-Guevara call the indigenist current hold that *sumak* and *suma* also mean the full and the complete, so "vida plena", full life, renders them better than "buen vivir" (2014).

Others asked who does the thinking, and for whom. The Bolivian Fausto Reinaga (1906–1994) set *indianismo*, a project thought by those who affirm themselves as Indians, for the liberation of the Indian, against *indigenismo*, projects thought by non-Indians to incorporate the Indian (in Enrique and Gustavo Cruz's account of *La revolución india*, 1970). For the Bolivian sociologist Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui, of Aymara descent, "no puede haber un discurso de la descolonización, una teoría de la descolonización, sin una práctica descolonizadora", there can be no discourse or theory of decolonisation without a decolonising practice (*Ch'ixinakax utxiwa*, 2010). Her own word is *ch'ixi*, a grey made of points of two colours that blend to the eye "sin nunca mezclarse del todo", without ever fully mixing: differences that coexist without fusing. In an interview she called the state's *vivir bien* "una palabra hueca", a hollow word, not grounded in "las dimensiones concretas, locales", the concrete, local dimensions in which people meet community and nature.
