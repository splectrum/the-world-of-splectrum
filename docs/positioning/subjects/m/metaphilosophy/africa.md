# Philosophy in Africa

Africa has many languages and no single word to set beside "philosophy". Each stage below has its own: the Egyptian *sbꜣyt*, "teaching", the name of the instruction genre; the Ge'ez *ḥatäta*, "inquiry"; in Timbuktu the Arabic of the Islamic sciences, logic (*manṭiq*) among them. For several stages below, "philosophy" was a later reader's label: Enno Littmann titled his edition of the Ethiopian texts *Philosophi abessini* (1904), and the Belgian missionary Placide Tempels called his reconstruction of a Bantu ontology *La philosophie bantoue* (1945). [Paulin Hountondji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulin_Hountondji) put the phrase itself in quotation marks, *Sur la « philosophie africaine »* (English 1983), and defined African philosophy as "a set of texts, specifically, the set of texts written by Africans and described as philosophical by their authors themselves".

In Souleymane Bachir Diagne's account the question was first framed from Europe: Hegel, for whom Africa stood outside reason and philosophical thinking, then Lévy-Bruhl's "primitive mentality", then Tempels. Some of what is filed as medieval and Islamic philosophy was written in Africa: Clement in Alexandria and Augustine in North Africa ([Medieval philosophy](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/medieval/)), Ibn Khaldūn in the Maghreb ([Philosophy in the Islamic world](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/islamic-world/)). Each stage below gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## The instructions

**What it is.** The Egyptian *Instruction of Ptahhotep*, attributed to an Old Kingdom vizier and generally dated to Middle Kingdom composition, calls itself "the proverbs of fair speech … when instructing the ignorant in the knowledge of exactness in fair-speaking" (tr. Gunn).

**Its point.** Its standard is *maat*, which Gunn renders "Truth": "Great is Truth, appointing a straight path; never hath it been overthrown since the reign of Osiris … it is that which a man repeateth from his father" (maxim 5).

**How it is done.** By hearing, from father to son. The closing maxims play on one root which, in Gunn's note, "includes our _hear_ (_listen_) and _obey_": "A son that hearkeneth is as a Follower of Horus … He repeateth in like manner to his sons and daughters, so renewing the instruction of his father" (41). And learning is never finished: "Be not proud because thou art learned; but discourse with the ignorant man, as with the sage. For no limit can be set to skill, neither is there any craftsman that possesseth full advantages" (maxim 1).

Whether this is philosophy has been fought out over origins. G. G. M. James's *Stolen Legacy* (1954) argued that the Greeks took their philosophy from Egypt, and Cheikh Anta Diop that Egypt was a black African civilisation, with, in Lucius Outlaw's words, "enormous implications for revisions to histories of the origins of Western Philosophy". Mary Lefkowitz answered in *Not Out of Africa* (1996). Martin Bernal, granting the Afrocentrists "have been wrong on many particulars", replied that "The term 'philosophy' is extraordinarily slippery", and that in the sense of wonder and speculation on truth "there is every reason to suppose that it was present in Ancient Egypt".

## The Hatata

**What it is.** Inquiry (*ḥatäta*). The Ethiopian Zera Yacob's treatise of the 1660s finds that the truth "is immediately revealed" to the one who seeks it: "he who investigates with the pure intelligence set by the creator in the heart of each man and scrutinizes the order and laws of creation, will discover the truth" (ch. V, tr. Sumner). 

**Its point.** "The creator himself wills that we adorn our life with science and work; for such an end did he give us reason and power … God does not create us perfect but creates us with such a reason as to know that we are to strive for perfection as long as we live in this world" (ch. VIII). His pupil Walda Heywat wrote his own book "in order that all the children of Ethiopia grow in knowledge and be counselled".

**How it is done.** By reason, against the doctrine of men. Each faith says it is right and the others false, and "who would be the judge for such a kind of argument? No single human being [can judge:] for all men are plaintiffs and defendants between themselves … As my faith appears true to me, so does another one find his own faith true; but truth is one" (ch. IV). "God has indeed given reason to all and everyone so that they may know truth and falsehood … let us seek it with our reason … We cannot, however, reach truth through the doctrine of men, for all men are liars" (ch. VII). The text's own words are inquiry, wisdom, intelligence and reason; "philosophy" is its editor's title and its translator's verdict. Its authorship was disputed from 1920; Getatchew Haile and its 2023 translators accept it as Ethiopian.

## The scholars of Timbuktu

**What it is.** In Diagne's account of the Timbuktu manuscripts, *falsafa* "had almost disappeared from the curriculum", but philosophy as "an all-encompassing way of thinking which goes far beyond the disciplinary boundaries" lived on in theology, law, Sufism and logic, the last "considered by the peripatetic followers of Aristotle as the Organon, the instrument for all knowledge in general". The twentieth-century debate, he adds, "Euro-philosophers versus ethno-philosophers", was conducted "in total ignorance of the written tradition of the kind that is revealed by the Timbuktu manuscripts".

**Its point.** For Ahmad Baba (d. 1627), as Diagne reads him, "knowledge is authentic and complete only when it is a way of life", when the accomplished jurist is also the realised sage.

## The ethnophilosophy debate

**What it is.** Tempels's book reconstructs a collective Bantu world-view of vital force; Alexis Kagame's *La philosophie bantu-rwandaise de l'être* (1956), by "an autochthonous Bantu African", set out to test it, with the stress on language and grammar. Hountondji called Tempels's book "an ethnological work with philosophical pretensions, or more simply, if I may coin the word, a work of 'ethnophilosophy'", addressed "not … to Africans but to Europeans". Philosophy for him is not "a collective and unconscious world-view": it "consists essentially in the process of analysis itself".

**Its point.** Hountondji: liberation, "in the most political sense of the word", of an ideological discourse into "a truly theoretical discourse which will be indissolubly philosophical and scientific".

**How it is done.** By analysis, explicit and argued, in written texts. The eighteenth-century [Anton Wilhelm Amo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Wilhelm_Amo), born in West Africa, wrote at Halle a *Treatise on the Art of Soberly and Accurately Philosophising* (1738); Hountondji wrote a study of him, which Jonathan Rée introduces as on a philosopher "who happened (although one would not know it from his writings) to be African". By Hountondji's definition he belongs; by place, he wrote in Germany.

## The sages

**What it is.** H. Odera Oruka, in "Four Trends in Current African Philosophy" (1978), sorted the field into ethnophilosophy, philosophic sagacity, nationalist-ideological philosophy and professional philosophy, and later added two more. He faulted Tempels and others for presenting "philosophy without philosophers". Against the "folk sages", who recount their community's beliefs, he set "philosophic sagacity", sages who take "a critical, evaluative stance toward them" (Gail Presbey's account).

**Its point.** In Presbey's account, to help Kenyans "evaluate and be able to justify their cultural practices", philosophy addressing "the concrete problems facing Kenyans and Africans".

**How it is done.** By interview: "We are seeking to unsheathe, through constant contacts and discussions with those concerned, the elaborate philosophical views and reasons from the living traditional Kenyan thinkers and sages" (Oruka, 1975). The criteria became the dispute. For Ndaba the ideal of "an individual, explicit, critical and self-critical ratiocinative consciousness" is "a Western notion"; Sophie Oluwole held that Oruka defined philosophy so narrowly that his own sages would fail it; and Hountondji in 2002 found his own earlier rejection of collective thought "excessive". The question became who counts as a philosopher.

## Decolonisation

**What it is.** [Kwasi Wiredu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwasi_Wiredu): "philosophy, or at any rate the best kind of philosophy, is a critique, for the most part, of fundamental conceptualization. That is to say, it is the critical examination of the conceptual framework upon which the thought of a culture is erected" ("Toward Decolonizing African Philosophy and Religion", 1998).

**Its point.** As the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* quotes him, the flourishing "of our peoples and, ultimately, all other peoples". And not decolonisation alone: "African philosophers in our time cannot live by decolonization alone but also by the direct interrogation of reality. What is truth, goodness, freedom, time, causality, justice?"

**How it is done.** "By decolonization, I mean divesting African philosophical thinking of all undue influences emanating from our colonial past. The crucial word in this formulation is 'undue'." Language matters but does not decide: "a language, most assuredly, is not conceptually neutral; syntax and vocabulary are apt to suggest definite modes of conceptualization. Note, however, that I say 'suggest' not 'compel'". So "African philosophers have to be doubly critical", and the work "has of necessity to be comparative". His example is the Akan "*Nokware mu nni abra*, literally, there is no conflict in truth", which "evidently, is an invocation of the principle of non-contradiction".

## In one view

| | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| The instructions | "fair speech", teaching | *maat*, "Great is Truth" | hearing, which is also obeying; "no limit can be set to skill" |
| The Hatata | inquiry with "the pure intelligence set by the creator" | "to strive for perfection" | by reason, not "the doctrine of men"; "truth is one" |
| Timbuktu | philosophy "beyond the disciplinary boundaries" | knowledge "as a way of life" (Ahmad Baba, as Diagne reads him) | — |
| The ethnophilosophy debate | a collective world-view, or "a set of texts" | liberation into theory | "the process of analysis itself" |
| The sages | philosophic, not folk, sagacity | to evaluate and justify practices | interviewing named sages |
