# Philosophy in the Islamic World

*Falsafa* is a loanword from the Greek *philosophia*; [al-Kindī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi) gives its sense as "the love of wisdom (*ḥubb al-ḥikma*)". The *falāsifa* were, in Frank Griffel's words, "heirs to the late-antique tradition of understanding the works of Aristotle in Neoplatonic terms", and falsafa "was a movement where Christians, Muslims, and even pagan authors participated". What to call the field is itself disputed. [Peter Adamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Adamson_(philosopher)) finds "Islamic philosophy" too narrow, since some of its philosophers were not Muslims, and "Arabic philosophy" too narrow, since some of the work is in Persian and Hebrew; he prefers "philosophy in the Islamic world", "a neutral designation".

The stages below run from the ninth century to the seventeenth. *Falsafa* grew from the same Greek line of thought as [medieval philosophy](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/medieval/) in Europe, ran beside it and was entwined with it; the later *ḥikma* went on largely apart from Europe. Each gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## The reception

**What it is.** al-Kindī (d. c. 870) wrote a treatise *On Definitions*, "almost certainly at least a production of al-Kindī's circle" (Adamson), gives six: from the name, the love of wisdom; from its act, imitating "the acts of God, in so far as it is in the power of man"; "concern with death", death being both natural and "bringing death to the desires"; "the craft of crafts and the wisdom of wisdoms"; "the knowledge of man himself"; and "the knowledge of all eternal things … in so far as it is in the power of man". In *On First Philosophy*, since philosophy is the study of truth, first philosophy is "the knowledge of the first truth who is the cause of all truth".

**How it is done.** By taking truth where it is found: "We must not be ashamed to admire the truth or to acquire it, from wherever it comes. Even if it should come from far-flung nations and foreign peoples…"

## The philosopher-legislator

**What it is.** [al-Fārābī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi) (d. 950), known as the Second Teacher, after Aristotle, mapped the whole. His *Enumeration of the Sciences* gives the Aristotelian order, from language and logic through mathematics and natural philosophy to metaphysics and political science, and political science "also includes kalām, i.e., non-philosophical theology, and fiqh or Islamic law" (Druart; the gloss on kalām is hers). In the *Attainment of Happiness* the Greeks called this science "unqualified wisdom" and "highest wisdom", and "the scientific state of mind 'philosophy' (by which they meant the quest and the love for the highest wisdom)"; it is the "science of sciences", "wisdom of wisdoms" and "art of arts" (§53).

**Its point.** "The human things by which nations and citizens of cities attain earthly happiness in this life and supreme happiness in the life beyond are of four kinds: theoretical virtues, deliberative virtues, moral virtues, and practical arts" (§1). Theory alone is "defective philosophy": "To be a truly perfect philosopher, one has to possess both the theoretical sciences and the faculty for exploiting them for the benefit of all others according to their capacity" (§54). So "the idea of the Philosopher, Supreme Ruler, Prince, Legislator, and Imam is but a single idea" (§58).

Religion teaches the same things another way. A thing is understood either by its essence "perceived by the intellect" or through "the similitude that imitates it", and assent comes by "certain demonstration" or by "persuasion"; the second pair is what "the ancients call … 'religion'". "Therefore, according to the ancients, religion is an imitation of philosophy", and both "give an account of the ultimate end for the sake of which man is made—that is, supreme happiness" (§55). The same things "are philosophy when they are in the soul of the legislator. They are religion when they are in the souls of the multitude" (§59). "Imitation" is Muhsin Mahdi's rendering; Nadja Germann renders a parallel passage as "symbolic representation", and reads philosophy as superior only in its level of knowledge, religion being no less important for the many.

**How it is done.** By demonstration. al-Fārābī sorts discourse into three kinds: demonstrative, after the *Posterior Analytics*, "which alone is philosophical *stricto sensu*"; dialectical, "typical of the 'mutakallimūn' or theologians"; and rhetorical and poetical, the scriptures' way "to address ordinary people" (Druart).

## The system

**What it is.** [Ibn Sīnā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna) (Avicenna, d. 1037) wrote in *The Cure* (*al-Shifāʾ*) a summa of "all the parts of philosophy in the Aristotelian tradition": logic as its instrument, the theoretical sciences (physics, mathematics, metaphysics) and the practical. In Dimitri Gutas's account he brought "all manifestations of religious life and paranormal events" inside it, so that it could "account for all reality": prophecy, dreams and the afterlife went to a metaphysics of the rational soul, and the social side became a further practical science, "the discipline of legislating". On this account, where al-Fārābī made religion philosophy's image, Ibn Sīnā made it one of philosophy's subjects.

**Its point.** "Real happiness is the perfection of the rational soul through knowledge" (Gutas's summary of the *Shifāʾ*, *Metaphysics* IX.7). In Gutas's words, "it is our cosmic duty to enable our intellects to reach their full potential and behave like the celestial ones, that is, think the intelligibles".

**How it is done.** Philosophy can be corrected and carried further. Aristotle's successors must "gather the loose ends he left, repair any breach they find in what he constructed" (*Eastern Philosophy*, tr. Gutas). Even prophetic knowledge is "not an uncritical reception … merely on authority, but rather occurs in an order which includes the middle terms" of a syllogism (*Shifāʾ*, *De anima*).

## The challenge and the defence

**What it is.** [al-Ghazālī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali) (d. 1111), in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*), examined twenty positions of the *falāsifa*. Most he called innovations; three, from Ibn Sīnā, he called unbelief: that the world has no beginning, that God knows only universals, and that the body is not resurrected. Yet he accepted demonstration, and in Griffel's account with him "philosophy and the Greek sciences were 'naturalized' into the discourse of kalām". Griffel reads the *Tahāfut*, "a book that belongs to the genre of kalām", as itself a philosophical book. Where philosophy in Islam stops is therefore disputed: at *falsafa*, or beyond it.

**Its point.** [Ibn Rushd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes) (Averroes, 1126–98) asked in *The Decisive Treatise* "whether the study of philosophy and logic is allowed by the Law, or prohibited, or commanded, either by way of recommendation or as obligatory". Philosophy is "nothing more than study of existing beings and reflection on them as indications of the Artisan", and the Law makes it obligatory. "Truth does not oppose truth but accords with it and bears witness to it"; philosophy is "the friend and milk-sister of religion" (tr. Hourani). In the same century the Jewish philosopher [Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides) (d. 1204) framed his *Guide of the Perplexed* (1190) as, in Kenneth Seeskin's words, "a letter written to an advanced student who cannot decide whether to follow philosophy or the teachings of his religion". For him the highest perfection is intellectual: in the parable of the palace (*Guide* III.51), those who "have proved all that can be proved" come nearest the ruler.

**How it is done.** Both sides argued on the falāsifa's own method. al-Ghazālī's charge, in Griffel's account, is that none of their twenty arguments meets the standard of demonstration (*burhān*): they rest "upon unproven premises that are accepted only among the falāsifa", "mere dialectical arguments", and so are *taqlīd*, repetition of the founders. Ibn Rushd answers that the Law requires study "by intellectual reasoning", the best kind being demonstration, and he sorts people into demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical classes, reserving allegorical interpretation of scripture to the first.

## Wisdom after al-Ghazālī

**What it is.** The name changed. In Griffel's account, as a reviewer quotes him, philosophers in the Greek line after al-Ghazālī "avoided the word falsafa and replaced it with another word that assumed largely the same meaning", *ḥikma*, wisdom, and theologians "engaged in philosophy just as much as the falāsifa" while rejecting the name. In the seventeenth century the bibliographer Ḥājjī Khalīfa files the whole subject under *ʿilm al-ḥikma*, the science of wisdom.

**Its point.** Sajjad Rizvi, drawing on Hadot, sums up [Mullā Ṣadrā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulla_Sadra) (d. 1640): "Philosophy is thus a practice and a way of life". Ṣadrā quotes a saying of ʿAlī: "the study of wisdom requires spiritual exercise and forsaking the world".

**How it is done.** [Suhrawardī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahab_al-Din_Yahya_ibn_Habash_Suhrawardi) (d. 1191) puts intuition first: what he "obtained through my intuition during my retreats and visions … I did not first arrive at it through cogitation … Subsequently I sought proof for it" (*Philosophy of Illumination*). Mullā Ṣadrā, in *The Transcendent Wisdom of the Four Journeys of the Intellect*, holds that metaphysical doctrines "can only be grasped by inner revelation … and cannot be really known through rehearsing discursive doctrines", yet "demonstration does not conflict with inner disclosure". A dissent comes from [Ibn Khaldūn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun) (d. 1406), in a chapter titled "A refutation of philosophy": logic "is not adequate to achieve the avowed intentions" of the philosophers, but "has only a single fruit, namely, it sharpens the mind" (*Muqaddimah* VI.30, tr. Rosenthal).

## Into the Latin West

In twelfth-century Toledo, Gerard of Cremona and Dominicus Gundissalinus translated al-Fārābī's *Enumeration*, and Gundissalinus's *On the Division of Philosophy* carried its map of the sciences into the Latin arts faculties, together with what Dag Nikolaus Hasse calls al-Fārābī's "emphasis on demonstration as the pivotal means for the acquisition of certain knowledge". Ibn Rushd's commentaries were translated from about 1220, and "already by the 1230s he is known as 'the Commentator'". al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā and Ibn Rushd shared a view of philosophy's point, happiness through the intellect's conjunction with the active intellect, possible in this life for philosophers. Aquinas rejected perfect happiness in this life, and in 1277 the bishop of Paris condemned the article that there is no more excellent state than to give oneself to philosophy ([Medieval philosophy](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/medieval/)).

Griffel traces the reading that philosophy declined in Islam after al-Ghazālī to nineteenth-century Europe, Ernest Renan among others, and reads the centuries after al-Ghazālī instead as a change of name and genre.

## In one view

| | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| The reception (al-Kindī) | six definitions: the love of wisdom, imitating God, "concern with death" | — | truth "from wherever it comes" |
| The philosopher-legislator (al-Fārābī) | the science of sciences; theology and law inside political science | "supreme happiness"; religion "an imitation of philosophy" | demonstration, over dialectic and rhetoric |
| The system (Ibn Sīnā) | all reality, prophecy and legislation included | "the perfection of the rational soul through knowledge" | correcting and amplifying Aristotle; the syllogism |
| Challenge and defence (al-Ghazālī, Ibn Rushd, Maimonides) | *falsafa* against kalām, and the border disputed | the Law makes philosophy "obligatory"; intellectual perfection highest | is it demonstration at all? |
| Wisdom (*ḥikma*) | the same pursuit under another name | "a practice and a way of life" | intuition first, then proof; demonstration with disclosure; for Ibn Khaldūn, logic only "sharpens the mind" |
