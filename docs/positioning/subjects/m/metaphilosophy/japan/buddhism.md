# Buddhism in Japan

Buddhism came to Japan from China and Korea, and for a thousand years its temples were the centres of learning. Three of its teachers give three different answers to what study is for: Kūkai in the ninth century, Hōnen and Shinran at the turn of the thirteenth, and Dōgen in the thirteenth.

Kūkai (774–835), founder of the Shingon school, ranked the teachings. His *Ten Stages of the Mind* (c. 830) places every doctrine he knew at a stage of the mind, with the esoteric teaching at the top. Study of the exoteric teachings is study of doctrines; the esoteric is done with the body. In John Krummel's summary, "exoteric Buddhism emphasizes the intellectual study and comprehension of the doctrines set forth in the sūtras", while "esoteric Buddhism provides a bodily hermeneutic whereby esoteric truths … can be experienced".

Hōnen (1133–1212) had been renowned as a Tendai monk of wide learning, and set it aside. "Although Buddhism is vast, in essence it is composed of no more than the three learnings [of precepts, meditation, and wisdom.] … But as for precepts, I myself do not keep a single one. In meditation, I have not attained even one. In wisdom, I have not attained the right wisdom" (tr. Hirota). His last testament, the *One-Sheet Document* of 1212, tells those who entrust themselves to the nembutsu, the recitation of Amida Buddha's name, that "however well versed in the lifetime teachings of the Buddha", they "should consider themselves as illiterate, stupid persons" (literally, as those "who do not know a single letter"), "and without pretensions to wisdom, should single-heartedly recite the Nembutsu" (tr. Inagaki).

His disciple Shinran (1173–1263) drew the line between two paths. In the *Tannishō*, the record of his words kept by a disciple: "It is the Path of Sages that takes learning to be essential; it is called 'difficult practice.'" To claim that those who do not read the sūtras and commentaries cannot be sure of birth in the Pure Land is "hardly worth mentioning". Learning keeps one use: "If one studies, more and more one realizes Amida's fundamental intent", so as to explain to those who doubt that the Vow excludes no one; "only then is there meaning in being a scholar". Of disputation: "Where disputation takes place, blind passions arise. The wise keep their distance." What saves is Other Power (*tariki*), not self-power (*jiriki*); "concerning the nembutsu, no working is true working. For it is beyond description, explanation, and conceptual understanding" (tr. Collected Works of Shinran). Late in life he wrote of *jinen*, being made so of itself, "not through the practicer's calculation", and ended with a warning against his own subject: "we should not be forever talking about jinen. If we continuously discuss jinen, that no working is true working will again become a problem of working."

Dōgen (1200–1253), who brought the Sōtō line of Zen from China, made the study the self. "Genjōkōan" (1233), which he set at the head of his *Shōbōgenzō*, opens: "To study the way of the buddhas is to study oneself. To study oneself is to forget oneself. To forget oneself is to be verified by the myriad dharmas. To be verified by the myriad dharmas is to slough off one's own body and mind and the body and mind of others" (tr. Sōtō Zen Text Project). The object is the Buddha Way (*butsudō*), and the direction of knowing decides: "Bringing the self to practice and verify the myriad dharmas represents delusion; the myriad dharmas proceeding to practice and verify the self [is awakening]." The way of it is seated meditation, "just sitting" (*shikan taza*), in which practice and realisation are one (*shushō*) and "meditation is not a means to an end" (as Shigenori Nagatomo describes the Sōtō teaching).
