# Medieval Philosophy

This page runs from Clement of Alexandria (c. 200) and [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) to the Renaissance. [John Marenbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Marenbon) works with a "Long Middle Ages (c. 200 – c. 1700)" that takes in the Latin and Greek Christian, Islamic and Jewish traditions together; [Philosophy in the Islamic world](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/islamic-world/) runs through the same centuries.

A central question was faith and reason: whether philosophy is one with true religion, its servant, or a life of its own. The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s entry on medieval philosophy presents its philosophers as reasoning in earnest within their religions, not simply subordinating reason to faith. In the Renaissance, in Hadot's account, the ancient way of life came back. Each stage gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## Faith and reason

**What it is.** [Clement of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria) (c. 200) set philosophy on a ladder: "as the encyclical branches of study contribute to philosophy, which is their mistress; so also philosophy itself co-operates for the acquisition of wisdom". Augustine went further: it is taught "non aliam esse philosophiam, id est sapientiae studium, et aliam religionem", that philosophy, the pursuit of wisdom, is not one thing and religion another (*De vera religione* 5.8). If wisdom is God, "verus philosophus est amator Dei", the true philosopher is a lover of God (*City of God* VIII.1). In the ninth century [Eriugena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scotus_Eriugena) drew the conclusion both ways: "veram esse philosophiam veram religionem conversimque veram religionem esse veram philosophiam", true philosophy is true religion and true religion true philosophy (*De divina praedestinatione*).

The other answer was service. In the eleventh century [Peter Damian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Damian) held that dialectic and the other human arts, applied to theology, must serve it as a maid (*ancilla*) serves her mistress. [Aquinas](/positioning/persons/a/aquinas/) has sacred doctrine use the "philosophical disciplines" as inferiors and handmaidens, *tanquam inferioribus et ancillis* (*Summa Theologiae* I q.1 a.5): "Other sciences are called the handmaidens of this one". The formula *philosophia ancilla theologiae*, philosophy the handmaid of theology, is not his wording. The historian [Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot): "Philosophy was the servant of theology from Christian antiquity onward."

**Its point.** In [Boethius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boethius)'s *Consolation of Philosophy*, Philosophy comes to the prisoner as a physician: "the time," she says, "calls rather for healing than for lamentation", and he recognises "the face of my physician … my nurse, Philosophy" (I pr. 2–3, tr. James). For Aquinas, "it was necessary for man's salvation that there should be a knowledge revealed by God besides philosophical science built up by human reason" (I q.1 a.1). Sacred doctrine "makes use even of human reason, not, indeed, to prove faith … but to make clear other things"; "since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith" (a.8).

The masters of the Paris arts faculty answered otherwise. For [Boethius of Dacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boethius_of_Dacia) (*On the Supreme Good*), "Haec est vita philosophi, quam quicumque non habuerit non habet rectam vitam": this is the life of the philosopher, and whoever does not have it does not have a right life. Hadot reads the current as rediscovering "that philosophy is not only discourse but a way of life", and quotes Zdzisław Domański that it "accorded complete autonomy to philosophy". In 1277 the bishop of Paris condemned, among 219 articles, "Quod non est excellentior status, quam vacare philosophie", that there is no more excellent state than to give oneself to philosophy (art. 40), and "Quod sapientes mundi sunt philosophi tantum", that the wise of the world are the philosophers alone (art. 154). His prologue charges the masters with saying things are true according to philosophy but not according to the faith, "quasi sint due contrarie veritates", as if there were two contrary truths.

**How it is done.** [Peter Abelard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Abelard)'s *Sic et Non* sets the Fathers' conflicting sayings side by side: "through doubting we come to questioning and through questions we perceive the truth" (tr. Lewis). In the schools the reading of authoritative texts (*lectio*) grew into the disputed question, in Bazán's definition "a regular form of teaching, apprenticeship and research, presided over by a master, characterized by a dialectical method which consists of bringing forward and examining arguments based on reason and authority which oppose one another", where "the master must come to a doctrinal solution by an act of determination". The *Summa* shows the written form: each article states the objections, then "On the contrary", then the answer ("I answer that"), then the replies to the objections. Hadot: in the thirteenth-century Faculty of Arts philosophy "became identified with Aristotelianism", the professor's career "commenting on the works of Aristotle". In his account the spiritual exercises passed into Christian spirituality, and what survived is "the idea of a philosophy reduced to its conceptual content".

## The way of life again

**What it is.** In the Renaissance the ancient sense came back, in Hadot's account as one of its recurrences. [Petrarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrarch) refused the name of philosophers to "professors sitting in a chair" and kept it for those who confirmed their teachings by their acts (Hadot). [Erasmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus), in the *Paraclesis* prefaced to his New Testament (1516), calls Christ's teaching a philosophy: "It is a life rather than a disputation. It is an inspiration rather than a science" (1529 English).

**Its point.** Petrarch, against the Aristotelians: "It is better to will the good than to know the truth"; Aristotle's *Ethics* had made him "more learned … but not better" (*On His Own Ignorance*, tr. Nachod). [Montaigne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_de_Montaigne) takes up Cicero, for whom the philosophers' whole life is *commentatio mortis*, a rehearsal of death (*Tusculans* I.74), and Plato's *Phaedo* before him: "Philosopher ce n'est autre chose que s'aprester à la mort", to philosophise is nothing but to get ready to die (*Essais* I.19 in the 1595 edition), so as not to fear it; reason's work should tend "à nous faire bien viure", to make us live well. "La Philosophie est celle qui nous instruict à viure", philosophy is what instructs us to live, and children too have their lesson in it (I.25). Hadot quotes him: "My trade and my art is living."

**How it is done.** Not by disputation. For Erasmus this philosophy "doth rather consist in the affects of the mind, than in subtle reasons". Montaigne blames the "ergotismes", the quibbles of the schools, for making philosophy, even for people of understanding, "vn nom vain et fantastique", an empty and fanciful name of no use.

## In one view

| | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Christian antiquity and the Middle Ages | one with true religion (Augustine, Eriugena); the handmaid of theology | healing (Boethius); "natural reason should minister to faith"; for the arts masters, the best life | doubting to questioning; the disputed question; the article form |
| The Renaissance | "a life rather than a disputation" | "to will the good"; learning to die, learning to live | against the disputation and the "ergotismes" |
