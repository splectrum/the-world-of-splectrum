# Early Modern Philosophy

In the seventeenth century philosophy was again the whole system of knowledge, and in the same half-century the under-labourer to the natural philosophers; in the eighteenth it was the work of reason, and the philosophe a figure of society. Several of them turned first to the knower. [Locke](/positioning/persons/l/locke/) traced his *Essay* to a discussion that stalled, when "it came into my thoughts that we took a wrong course; and that before we set ourselves upon inquiries of that nature, it was necessary to examine our own abilities"; Hume set out "to enquire seriously into the nature of human understanding"; and [Kant](/positioning/persons/k/kant/) proposed "a complete revolution in the procedure of metaphysics". The page ends with Kant, after whom [the hinge](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/western/the-hinge/) begins. Each stage gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## The whole system, and the under-labourer

**What it is.** [Descartes](/positioning/persons/d/descartes/), in the letter that prefaces the French edition of the *Principles of Philosophy*, gave philosophy back its full extent: "the word PHILOSOPHY signifies the study of wisdom". His image is a tree: "all Philosophy is like a tree, of which Metaphysics is the root, Physics the trunk, and all the other sciences the branches", the branches being chiefly medicine, mechanics and ethics. Within half a century a second self-image stands beside it. Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689), in its Epistle to the Reader, sets itself beside the master-builders of the sciences, Boyle, Sydenham, Huygens and Newton: "it is ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge".

**Its point.** Descartes's wisdom is "not merely prudence in the management of affairs, but a perfect knowledge of all that man can know, as well for the conduct of his life as for the preservation of his health and the discovery of all the arts". For [Francis Bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon), "the real and legitimate goal of the sciences is the endowment of human life with new inventions and riches" (*Novum Organum* I.81).

**How it is done.** Bacon sets two ways side by side. One "hurries on rapidly from the senses and particulars to the most general axioms"; the other ascends "continually and gradually", "the true but unattempted way" (I.19). His image: the empirics are like ants and the dogmatists like spiders, "The bee, a mean between both … The true labor of philosophy resembles hers" (I.95). Descartes gives four rules in the *Discourse on Method* (Part II), the first "never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such", the last "to make enumerations so complete, and reviews so general, that I might be assured that nothing was omitted"; in the *Principles* letter, knowledge "must necessarily be deduced from first causes". Locke describes his own as a "historical, plain method", an account of "the ways whereby our understandings come to attain those notions of things we have" (*Essay*, Introduction §2).

## Reason and the philosophe

**What it is.** [Christian Wolff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Wolff_(philosopher)) (*Preliminary Discourse on Philosophy in General*, 1728): "Philosophia est scientia possibilium, quatenus esse possunt", "the science of the possibles insofar as they can be" (§29, tr. Blackwell), and earlier "the knowledge of the reason of things which are or occur" (§6). In the *Encyclopédie*, d'Alembert's *Preliminary Discourse* (1751), building on "l'Arbre encyclopédique" he credits to Bacon, divides knowledge by memory, reason and imagination into history, philosophy and the fine arts; philosophy, "le fruit de la raison", takes in the science of God, of man and of nature. The *Encyclopédie*'s article "Philosophie" adopts Wolff's definition as "la science des possibles en tant que possibles". [Hume](/positioning/persons/h/hume/) opens his *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* on two manners of treating "moral philosophy, or the science of human nature": the easy and obvious, which considers man as an active being, and the accurate and abstruse, which considers him as reasonable (§1).

**Its point.** The article "Philosophe", usually attributed to Dumarsais, makes the philosopher a social figure: "La raison est à l'égard du philosophe, ce que la grace est à l'égard du chretien", reason is to the philosopher what grace is to the Christian; he "ne se croit pas en exil dans ce monde", does not think himself in exile in this world, but is "un honnête homme qui veut plaire & se rendre utile", a decent man who wants to please and to be useful, and "la société civile est, pour ainsi dire, une divinité pour lui sur la terre", civil society is, so to speak, a divinity for him on earth. For Hume, "ONE considerable advantage, that arises from philosophy, consists in the sovereign antidote, which it affords to superstition and false religion" ("Of Suicide"). In the *Enquiry* Nature herself speaks: "let your science be human, and such as may have a direct reference to action and society … Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man".

**How it is done.** Wolff sets out demonstration and gives a chapter to "The Freedom to Philosophize". The philosophe "demêle les causes autant qu'il est en lui", disentangles causes as far as he can, and takes "pour douteux ce qui est douteux", the doubtful for doubtful. Hume: "We must submit to this fatigue, in order to live at ease ever after: And must cultivate true metaphysics with some care, in order to destroy the false and adulterate"; the means is "mental geography, or delineation of the distinct parts and powers of the mind".

## The critique of reason

**What it is.** Kant distinguishes two concepts of philosophy. The "scholastic conception" is of "a system of cognition which we are trying to elaborate into a science". But there is also a "cosmical conception", his *Weltbegriff*, "one in which all men necessarily take an interest": "In this view philosophy is the science of the relation of all cognition to the ultimate and essential aims of human reason" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, Akademie edition III 542, tr. Meiklejohn; "cosmical" is the translator's, others say "worldly" or "cosmopolitan"). "The whole interest of reason, speculative as well as practical, is centred in the three following questions: 1. WHAT CAN I KNOW? 2. WHAT OUGHT I TO DO? 3. WHAT MAY I HOPE?" (III 522). The *Logic*, edited from his lectures by Gottlob Jäsche, adds a fourth, "Was ist der Mensch?", What is man?, and says that at bottom all of it could be counted as anthropology ("Im Grunde könnte man aber alles dieses zur Anthropologie rechnen").

**Its point.** In the cosmical sense "the philosopher is not merely an artist—who occupies himself with conceptions—but a lawgiver, legislating for human reason". The essay "What is Enlightenment?" (1784) opens: "Aufklärung ist der Ausgang des Menschen aus seiner selbst verschuldeten Unmündigkeit", enlightenment is the human being's way out of a self-incurred immaturity, the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance; its motto is "Sapere aude! Habe Muth dich deines eigenen Verstandes zu bedienen!", have the courage to use your own understanding.

**How it is done.** By critique. Kant proposes "to do just what COPERNICUS did": as Copernicus, getting nowhere with the stars revolving round the spectator, tried the spectator revolving instead, so metaphysics should try the assumption "that the objects must conform to our cognition". The *Critique* "is a treatise on the method to be followed, not a system of the science itself" (III 15). And philosophy cannot be learned, only philosophising: "We can only learn to philosophize; in other words, we can only exercise our powers of reasoning in accordance with general principles, retaining at the same time, the right of investigating the sources of these principles, of testing, and even of rejecting them".

## In one view

| | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seventeenth century | the tree of knowledge; the under-labourer | "the conduct of his life", "new inventions and riches" | four rules; the gradual ascent; the "historical, plain method" |
| Eighteenth century | "the science of the possibles"; reason's branch of knowledge | the philosophe useful to society; the "antidote" to superstition | demonstration; "mental geography" |
| Kant | the science of the relation of all cognition to "the essential aims of human reason"; four questions | the philosopher as "lawgiver"; "Sapere aude!" | critique; the Copernican experiment; "only learn to philosophize" |
