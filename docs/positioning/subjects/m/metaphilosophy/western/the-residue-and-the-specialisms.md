# The Residue and the Specialisms

By the early twentieth century one answer to "what is philosophy?" had been written out in so many words: philosophy is what is left when the sciences have taken what they can answer. Over the same decades philosophy divided into specialised fields, many of them named after a science. This page follows both, and the answers that refuse the residue view.

---

## The residue

[Russell](/positioning/persons/r/russell/) put it in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), in the chapter "The Value of Philosophy": "as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science. The whole study of the heavens, which now belongs to astronomy, was once included in philosophy; Newton's great work was called 'the mathematical principles of natural philosophy'. Similarly, the study of the human mind, which was a part of philosophy, has now been separated from philosophy and has become the science of psychology." What cannot yet be answered remains "to form the residue which is called philosophy".

He does not leave it there: "This is, however, only a part of the truth". Some questions "must remain insoluble", and "The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty".

The psychology example can be followed in the journals: the *Journal of Philosophy* was founded at Columbia in 1904 as *The Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods*, and took its present title in 1921.

---

## The philosophies of the special sciences

As the sciences left, philosophy also took many of them up as fields of its own. The neo-Kantian schools of the later nineteenth century each had a "philosophy of the special sciences", physics for Marburg, history for the Southwest school. The journal *Philosophy of Science* dates from 1934, and philosophy of biology became an independent field only in the 1960s and 1970s. The research university made philosophy a discipline among disciplines, and its old unity "shattered under the weight of increasing specialization by the turn of the 20th century" (Robert Frodeman and Adam Briggle, 2016).

The genitive form is older than these fields: Voltaire coined "philosophy of history" in 1765, and [Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/)'s *Philosophy of Right* appeared in 1820. Internal division is older still: logic, physics and ethics, from Xenocrates and the Stoics (see [The hinge](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/western/the-hinge/)).

Politics shaped the form too. George Reisch (*How the Cold War Transformed Philosophy of Science*, 2005) follows the Unity of Science movement from its politically engaged arrival in the United States in the 1930s to its de-politicisation in the 1950s. Its leading figure, [Otto Neurath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Neurath), had wanted an integration of the sciences, not a system above them: "An encyclopedia and not a system is the genuine model of science as a whole."

[Philip Kitcher](/positioning/persons/k/kitcher/)'s complaint (2011, 2023) is not against these fields. It is against an inward-turned "core", and he counts the outward-facing philosophies of the sciences as the living part of the discipline.

---

## Against the residue

- [Husserl](/positioning/persons/h/husserl/), in *The Crisis of European Sciences* (Parts I–II 1936, the whole 1954), reverses the view. The crisis is not in the sciences' results but in "what science in general had meant and can mean for human existence": "Merely fact-minded sciences make merely fact-minded people" (§2, in David Carr's translation). On this view philosophy is not left over when the sciences leave; the sciences have lost the meaning philosophy is meant to supply.
- [Ortega y Gasset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Ortega_y_Gasset) (1929) defines philosophy as knowledge of the Universe, "all there is" (*todo cuanto hay*), each thing seen as it stands facing and alongside the others.
- [Deleuze](/positioning/persons/d/deleuze/) and [Félix Guattari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9lix_Guattari) (*What Is Philosophy?*, 1991) place philosophy beside science and art, "all three being modes of thought, with no subordination among them": philosophy creates concepts, science functions, art "a bloc of sensation" (as the SEP summarises them).
