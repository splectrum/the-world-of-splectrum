# Philosophy in Japan

Before the Meiji period the enterprise had other names. In Thomas Kasulis's account *gaku*, learning, "signified learning, especially in the sense of modeling oneself after a textual or human model", and the premodern thinker was a sage with mastery in one of the traditional Ways (*michi* or *dō*): of the buddhas, of the Confucian sages, of the kami, or of the arts. *Tetsugaku*, the modern word, is [Nishi Amane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishi_Amane)'s ([The hinge](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/western/the-hinge/)); he first wrote *kitetsugaku* (1861), after Zhou Dunyi's "the scholar aspires to the worthy", and chose *tetsugaku* to keep it apart from Confucianism.

Whether Japan had philosophy was asked from the start. Inoue Tetsujirō, lecturing on "Eastern philosophy" at Tokyo from 1882, held that Western philosophers were mistaken to count only the philosophy since Greece; his studies of the Japanese Confucian schools (1900–05) are, in Mary Evelyn Tucker's words, where "the study of Japanese Confucianism as philosophy began". Nakae Chōmin answered in 1901, in *A Year and a Half*: "我日本古より今に至る迄哲学無し", our Japan, from antiquity to the present, has had no philosophy (as quoted by a Philosophical Association of Japan workshop, which sets his question beside the African, Latin American and Chinese debates). John Maraldo distinguishes "Japanese philosophy" from "philosophy in Japan", which "not only avoids ethnocentric connotations; it is also more balanced and inclusive"; this page uses the second. Each stage below gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## Kūkai

**What it is.** [Kūkai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%ABkai) (774–835) classified all teachings as ten states of mind, with his own Shingon at the top (*The Ten Stages of Mind*, c. 830); in John Krummel's account, "It is Kūkai's own invention however to associate the different doctrines evaluated and classified with specific states of mind".

**How it is done.** Krummel: "exoteric Buddhism emphasizes the intellectual study and comprehension of the doctrines set forth in the sūtras", while "esoteric Buddhism provides a bodily hermeneutic whereby esoteric truths … can be experienced".

---

## Dōgen

**What it is.** [Dōgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C5%8Dgen) (1200–1253) opens his *Shōbōgenzō* with "Genjōkōan" (1233): "To study the way of the buddhas is to study oneself. To study oneself is to forget oneself. To forget oneself is to be verified by the myriad dharmas. To be verified by the myriad dharmas is to slough off one's own body and mind and the body and mind of others" (tr. Sōtō Zen Text Project). The object is the Buddha Way (*butsudō*), not philosophy; the word rendered "verified" is "confirmed" for Waddell and Abe and "actualized" for Tanahashi.

**Its point.** To forget the self. The direction of knowing matters: "Bringing the self to practice and verify the myriad dharmas represents delusion; the myriad dharmas proceeding to practice and verify the self [is awakening]".

**How it is done.** By seated meditation. The Sōtō school, in Shigenori Nagatomo's account, "follows a method called 'just sitting' (*shikan taza*)", "practice-realization", in which "meditation is not a means to an end". Dōgen came to be read as a philosopher only in the twentieth century.

---

## The Confucian academies

**What it is.** In the Edo period, Kasulis writes, philosophy's "center of activity shifted from the Buddhist temples to secular urban academies supported by student tuition". For Tucker, Japanese Confucian philosophy "can be viewed as an East Asian philosophy of language engaged in a search for right meaning". [Ogyū Sorai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogy%C5%AB_Sorai) held in *Bendō* (*Distinguishing the Way*, 1717) that the Way of the former kings was made by the former kings, not the natural Way of heaven and earth.

**Its point.** For Sorai, to bring peace to the realm. Learning, in Tucker's summary of him, is "gained through practice in following the way … knowing how to do something rather than knowing that something is the case".

**How it is done.** By philology, doubt and emulation. [Itō Jinsai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Jinsai): "Detailed philological studies of the *Analects* and *Mencius* facilitate understanding of those sagely texts and keep students from erratically manipulating them by imposing their subjective thoughts on them" (*Gomō jigi*, preface, as translated in Tucker's account). Kaibara Ekken opens his *Record of Great Doubts* with Lu Xiangshan: "In learning, a person should be worried when he has no doubts". For Sorai the kings taught by things, not by principle, and only long practice brings the mind to true knowledge; the ancient phrasing (*kobunji*) had to be learned to understand the Way.

## Native Studies

**What it is.** A Way that is no doctrine. [Motoori Norinaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motoori_Norinaga), *Naobi no mitama* (1771): 「古の大御世には、道といふ言擧もさらになかりき」, in the great age of antiquity no one lifted up words about a "Way" at all; *michi* is only the road one takes to go somewhere, and to call every teaching "this Way" or "that Way" is the custom of foreign countries.

**Its point.** To recover the heart of antiquity. Norinaga sets the true heart (*magokoro*), "that heart with which one is born by virtue of the Musubi (creating) *kami*" (tr. Fujiwara), against the "Chinese heart" (*karagokoro*), the habit of taking one's own cleverness for one's heart. The same text calls the Chinese sages thieves who succeeded, and Native Studies later, in Kasulis's account, "went beyond its original philological and textual interests to construct a Shintō theory of racial purity".

**How it is done.** By reading the oldest texts with a purified heart. Kamo no Mabuchi recited the oldest verses aloud, so that one's "language and heart (*kokoro*) are transported back to the ancient past". Norinaga: purify away the Chinese-book heart, study the ancient texts with the clear heart of the land, and you will know of yourself that there is no Way to be practised, and that to know this is to follow the Way of the kami. And of his own argument: 「かゝれば如此まで論ふも、道の意にはあらねども」, even to argue this far is not in the spirit of the Way.

## Meiji

**What it is.** Nishi defined *tetsugaku* (1874) as making clear the Way of Heaven and the Way of man and establishing the method of teaching; in a manuscript of 1872, after Comte, he gave the philosopher the unified view of the sciences, the detail belonging to the specialists in each. [Inoue Enryō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inoue_Enry%C5%8D) founded an Academy of Philosophy in 1887 under the motto "The Basis of All Learning Lies in Philosophy", and honoured Four Sages: "Confucius, Buddha, Socrates, and Kant".

**Its point.** For Inoue Tetsujirō, philosophy also served the state: he promoted "*kokumin dōtoku*, or 'national ethics'" (Tucker). Academic *tetsugaku*, in Kasulis's account, fitted "the 'Western ingenuity' side" of the Meiji formula "Japanese spirit, Western learning", especially as it restricted itself to epistemology, logic, philosophy of science and metaphysics.

## The Kyoto School

**What it is.** [Nishida](/positioning/persons/n/nishida/)'s *An Inquiry into the Good* (1911) holds religion to be "the conclusion of philosophy" (哲学の終結), and its preface puts experience before the individual: 「個人あって経験あるにあらず、経験あって個人あるのである」, it is not that there is experience because there is an individual; there is an individual because there is experience. [Tanabe Hajime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajime_Tanabe)'s *Philosophy as Metanoetics* (1946), in James Heisig's words, "argues for the ultimate futility of all philosophy, and proposes instead a 'philosophy that is not a philosophy'". Its title is *Zangedō to shite no tetsugaku*, the Way of repentance, which Tanabe himself glossed with the Greek *metanoia*.

**Its point.** Nishida named the book for the Good because, although philosophical inquiry fills its first half, "人生の問題が中心であり、終結である", the problem of life is the centre and the conclusion. In 1932, as quoted in a University of Tokyo paper, he wrote that the motive of philosophy must be not "wonder" but the deep sorrow of life.

**How it is done.** Tanabe's call, in Bret Davis's account, is for "an 'absolute critique' of human rationality as such": reason "is inevitably driven to antinomies through which it must repeatedly die to its own self-power in order to be reborn again through the workings of an Other-power".

---

## After 1945

**What it is.** Kasulis: after the war "liberal and leftist thinkers scorned the very idea of 'Japanese' philosophy as smacking of nativism, ethnocentrism, jingoism, and State Shintō ideology", and departments "escaped into Western philosophy". Maraldo (2017) sorts four senses of "Japanese philosophy": philosophy of European vintage by people who happen to be Japanese; classical thought before the European term, as Inoue used it; Western methods applied to premodern thought; and a distinctive Japanese originality, criticised as "inverted orientalism". "Even more controversial is the question whether pre-modern Japanese thought counts as philosophy." His own answer: "If there is a defining characteristic to philosophy through the ages and across cultures, surely it is the habit of interrogating given definitions."

**How it is done.** Kasulis closes on the choice he titles "Philosophy as *Wissenschaft* or Way?": "When Japanese philosophy considers itself a Way rather than a *Wissenschaft*, it stresses engagement over detachment."

| Kūkai | teachings ranked as ten states of mind | — | a "bodily hermeneutic" against exoteric study |
| Dōgen | to study the Buddha Way is to study oneself | to forget oneself | just sitting, practice-realization |
