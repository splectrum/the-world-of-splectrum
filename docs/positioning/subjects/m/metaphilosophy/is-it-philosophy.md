# Is It Philosophy?

India, China, Africa, Latin America and Japan each met the same question once the European word arrived: is what they have philosophy? The question measures other traditions by the European word, and it has been asked from both sides, by Europeans of others and by the traditions of themselves. The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s article on metaphilosophy, the source of this subject's three questions, treats "Western" metaphilosophy only and refers readers on Eastern metaphilosophy to its entries on Chinese and Indian philosophy.

---

## Where the question came from

Peter K. J. Park dates to 1780–1830 the rewriting of the history of philosophy, in his publisher's summary, "as a march of progress from the Greeks to Kant", which supplanted accounts beginning in Egypt or Western Asia; the other traditions "were ultimately deemed outside the scope of philosophy and relegated to the study of religion" (see [The hinge](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/the-hinge/)). [Hegel](/positioning/persons/h/hegel/)'s verdicts: as Wilhelm Halbfass quotes him, India's liberation was "only an abstract and negative liberation", and "The real philosophy begins only in the Occident"; Confucius was "a moralist as such, not actually a philosopher"; Africa, in Souleymane Bachir Diagne's paraphrase, was "exiled from reason, civilisation, true monotheistic religion and philosophical thinking"; and what had happened in the New World was "only an echo of the Old World".

---

## How it recurs

The question comes back in each tradition:

- **Japan, 1901.** Nakae Chōmin, in *A Year and a Half*: "我日本古より今に至る迄哲学無し", our Japan, from antiquity to the present, has had no philosophy (as quoted by a Philosophical Association of Japan workshop, which sets his question beside the African, Latin American and Chinese debates) ([Philosophy in Japan](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/japan/)).
- **China, 1919–1934 and 1999–2001.** The founding histories by Hu Shi and Feng Youlan selected from Chinese learning what could be named by the Western word; reviewing Feng, Jin Yuelin asked whether the result was a history of Chinese philosophy or a history of philosophy in China ([Philosophy in China](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/china/)). In 1999–2000 Zheng Jiadong raised the "legitimacy" of Chinese philosophy, aiming at the method of interpretation since Hu Shi. In 2001, in conversation in China, [Derrida](/positioning/persons/d/derrida/) said "中国没有哲学，只有思想", which can be read as "China has no philosophy, only thought" or "no philosophy, but it does have thought"; and Zheng and others paired his remark with the legitimacy question.
- **India.** Hermann Jacobi's equation of *ānvīkṣikī* with philosophy, Paul Hacker's reply (1958) that there was the concept but no word for it, and Halbfass's verdict (1988): commensurable thought, without the role the word played in Europe ([Philosophy in India](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/india/)).
- **Africa, 1945–1978.** Tempels's *La philosophie bantoue* (1945), Hountondji's "ethnophilosophy" and his "set of texts" (1976–77), Oruka's four trends (1978) ([Philosophy in Africa](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/africa/)).
- **Latin America, 1956–1969.** León-Portilla's *La filosofía náhuatl* (1956), then Salazar Bondy's *¿Existe una filosofía de nuestra América?* (1968) and Zea's reply (1969) ([Philosophy in Latin America](/positioning/subjects/m/metaphilosophy/latin-america/)).
- **The West, 2016.** [Jay Garfield](/positioning/persons/g/garfield/) and Bryan Van Norden proposed in *The New York Times* ("If Philosophy Won't Diversify, Let's Call It What It Really Is") that "any department that regularly offers courses only on Western philosophy should rename itself 'Department of European and American Philosophy'".

---

## Four answers

Carine Defoort ("Is There Such a Thing as Chinese Philosophy?", 2001) finds the question "seldom explicitly stated", though implicit answers dominate institutions; denial is "primarily, though not exclusively, implicit and Western", affirmation "most explicitly represented in contemporary China". She sorts four positions:

1. **It does not exist.** There is no Chinese philosophy, on historical or theoretical grounds.
2. **It exists.** Feng Youlan's history is the founding case.
3. **Redefine the word.** Widen the content of "philosophy" so that the masters fall within it.
4. **Question the project.** "Why should China even have had a philosophy?"

Her paradox: "Chinese philosophy" "seems to have died of its own birth", the masters' tradition having largely ended just as the modern discipline named it. She offers the Chinese case as "a case study for a wider problematic".
