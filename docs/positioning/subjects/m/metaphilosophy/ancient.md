# Ancient Philosophy

*Philosophia* is the love of wisdom (*sophia*). The story that [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras) coined the word comes from [Plato](/positioning/persons/p/plato/)'s pupil Heraclides Ponticus, as Cicero and Diogenes Laertius report: no one is wise but God, so Pythagoras called himself not wise but a lover of wisdom. The historian [Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot), following Walter Burkert, reads the anecdote as "an instance in which the Platonic notion of philosophia was projected upon Pythagoras"; in his account the words of the *philosophia* family "did not in fact appear until the fifth century B.C.", after the first Greek thinkers had begun their work.

The first thinkers called their work inquiry into nature; from Socrates on, in Hadot's account, philosophy became a way of life whose teaching took in everything there was to know. Each stage gave its own answers to the three questions: what philosophy is, what its point is, and how it should be done.

---

## The inquiry into nature

**What it is.** The first Greek thinkers did not call themselves philosophers. In Hadot's account it was [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/), "and with him the entire tradition of the history of philosophy", who applied the word "philosophers" to them. Their own word was *historia*, inquiry, and its object "was universal phusis". Plato's Socrates recalls his youthful eagerness "for the kind of wisdom which they call investigation of nature", *peri physeos historia* (*Phaedo* 96a); the title *On Nature* given to their books is later. Aristotle read them as his forerunners in the search for causes: "Of the first philosophers, then, most thought the principles which were of the nature of matter were the only principles of all things", with [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_of_Miletus) "the founder of this type of philosophy" (*Metaphysics* I.3). One early fragment joins the two words: "Men that love wisdom must be acquainted with very many things indeed" ([Heraclitus](/positioning/persons/h/heraclitus/) B35), where the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s rendering has "inquirers"; whether Heraclitus wrote "lovers of wisdom" is doubted. He also held that "The learning of many things teacheth not understanding" (B40).

**Its point.** Asked why one should choose to be born rather than not, [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras), so the story goes, answered "For the sake of contemplating the heavens and the whole order of the universe" (Aristotle, *Eudemian Ethics* 1216a). Aristotle says that the first philosophers began in wonder and "philosophized in order to escape from ignorance", "in order to know, and not for any utilitarian end" (*Metaphysics* I.2). A second answer points ahead: the *Republic* speaks of "a certain way of life the Pythagorean" (600b).

**How it is done.** By seeking rather than by revelation: "The gods have not revealed all things to men from the beginning, but by seeking they find in time what is better" ([Xenophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophanes) B18); and with a limit: "There never was nor will be a man who has certain knowledge about the gods and about all the things I speak of" (B34). By the senses read with understanding: "Eyes and ears are bad witnesses to men if they have souls that understand not their language" (Heraclitus B107); and by turning inward: "I have sought for myself" (B101). [Parmenides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenides)'s goddess warns against "a wandering eye or sounding ear or tongue": "judge by argument" (*logos*, B7). The fragments here are in John Burnet's translation (*Early Greek Philosophy*, 2nd ed., 1908).

The name "Presocratic" is modern, coined by J. A. Eberhard in 1788 and made standard by Hermann Diels's *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (1903). It is contested, not least because some of these thinkers were Socrates' contemporaries: André Laks gave a book to the question (*The Concept of Presocratic Philosophy*, 2006), and his and Glenn Most's edition, *Early Greek Philosophy* (2016), avoids the term.

## A way of life

**What it is.** Hadot reads ancient philosophy as a practice first and a doctrine second: "Philosophical discourse, then, originates in a choice of life and an existential option—not vice versa" (*What Is Ancient Philosophy?*, 1995). The teaching covered everything. The Stoics divided it into three parts, physics, ethics and logic, and compared it to an animal, "Logic corresponding to the bones and sinews, Ethics to the fleshy parts, Physics to the soul"; "No single part, some Stoics declare, is independent of any other part, but all blend together" (Diogenes Laertius VII.39–40). The division is older than the Stoics: Sextus Empiricus finds it in Xenocrates and the Peripatetics, with Plato "virtually, the pioneer". Hadot adds that the Stoics distinguished philosophy itself, "the lived practice of the virtues of logic, physics, and ethics", from "discourse according to philosophy", the three theories as taught. The wisdom it loved reached as far: "wisdom is the knowledge of things divine and human" (Clement of Alexandria, drawing on that tradition).

**Its point.** Plato's Socrates: "daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and … the unexamined life is not worth living" (*Apology* 38a). The citizens should care for "wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul" (29d–30a). For the Hellenistic schools, in the *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*'s summary, philosophy revealed what was valuable and what was not, and how to achieve the one and stop longing for the other. [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) answered his own "What, then, is Philosophy?" with "Philosophy is the supremely precious" (*Enneads* I.3.5). For Hadot it was "a method for training people to live and to look at the world in a new way" (*Philosophy as a Way of Life*).

**How it is done.** Socrates questions: "I proceed to interrogate and examine and cross-examine him" (*Apology* 29e). In the *Republic* the highest method is dialectic, which "alone, goes directly to the first principle and is the only science which does away with hypotheses in order to make her ground secure" (533c–d), "the coping-stone of the sciences" (534e). [Aristotle](/positioning/persons/a/aristotle/) starts from what is already believed: "We must, as in all other cases, set the observed facts before us and, after first discussing the difficulties, go on to prove, if possible, the truth of all the common opinions" (*Nicomachean Ethics* VII.1, 1145b2–7). The common opinions are the *endoxa*, those "accepted by every one or by the majority or by the philosophers", and for the philosophical sciences "the ability to raise searching difficulties on both sides of a subject" helps (*Topics* I.1–2).

## In one view

| | What it is | Its point | How it is done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Greek thinkers | inquiry into nature (*historia*) | "contemplating the heavens and the whole order of the universe" | seeking; the senses read with understanding; "judge by argument" |
| Socrates to late antiquity | a choice of life (Hadot); physics, ethics and logic as one animal | "the unexamined life is not worth living" | examining; dialectic; starting from the common opinions |
