# Plato and the Academy

[Plato](/positioning/persons/p/plato/)'s dialogues are where *philosophia* is first defined. It starts in a feeling: "this feeling of wonder shows that you are a philosopher, since wonder is the only beginning of philosophy" (*Theaetetus* 155d, tr. Fowler). The lover of wisdom "desires all wisdom, not a part and a part not" (*Republic* 475b, tr. Shorey).

"Those who pursue philosophy aright study nothing but dying and being dead", and "the true philosophers practice dying, and death is less terrible to them than to any other men" (*Phaedo* 64a, 67e, tr. Fowler); Cicero later put it in Latin as *Tota enim philosophorum vita … commentatio mortis est*, the whole life of philosophers is a rehearsal for death (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.74). In a passage of the *Theaetetus* that begins with the philosopher's leisure, Socrates says that "to escape is to become like God, so far as this is possible; and to become like God is to become righteous and holy and wise" (176b). The good it serves is also the city's: "unless … our kings and rulers take to the pursuit of philosophy seriously and adequately, and there is a conjunction of these two things, political power and philosophic intelligence … there can be no cessation of troubles … for our states, nor, I fancy, for the human race either" (*Republic* 473d).

Dialectic is the way: "is not dialectics the only process of inquiry that advances in this manner, doing away with hypotheses, up to the first principle itself in order to find confirmation there?" (*Republic* 533c). And what it reaches at the end cannot be set down like other learning. In the *Seventh Letter*, whose authenticity is disputed, the writer says of his own subject that "it does not at all admit of verbal expression like other studies, but, as a result of continued application to the subject itself and communion therewith, it is brought to birth in the soul on a sudden, as light that is kindled by a leaping spark" (341c–d, tr. Bury).

The school Plato left went on under his successors, and the ancients counted its phases. Of Arcesilaus, Diogenes Laertius writes: "With him begins the Middle Academy; he was the first to suspend his judgement owing to the contradictions of opposing arguments. He was also the first to argue on both sides of a question" (4.28, tr. Hicks). Sextus Empiricus counted "three" Academies, adding that "some, however, add as a fourth" (*Outlines of Pyrrhonism* 1.220, tr. Bury). In Cicero's *Academica*, Varro holds that the Academics and the Peripatetics, Aristotle's school, were one teaching under two names, *rebus congruentes nominibus differebant*, agreeing in substance and differing in name (1.17).
