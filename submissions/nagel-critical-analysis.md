### Nagel's position, in his words

- **Against functionalism.** "the disposition of some philosophers to take seriously the question whether these things are conscious or might soon become conscious is just ridiculous. They are assuming that the physical substrate is irrelevant." "It's more than likely that the truth is one which links physical processes and systems of certain kinds to mental phenomena. Complicated data centers have absolutely nothing in common with the physical character of a nervous system." Consciousness elsewhere in the universe "would require the appearance of some kind of organism with something analogous to a nervous system."
- **Language alone.** "All they produce is language, and language by itself is not a natural manifestation of consciousness."
- **Bats are different.** "Bats are conscious. We just don't have the imaginative capacity to form a conception of the aspects of that subjectivity that depend on echolocation. It would be radically unscientific to conclude that bats are not conscious from the fact that we can't imagine what it's like."
- **Restraint on the question.** "I don't exclude the possibility of life forms that are physically and chemically quite different from ourselves being conscious. We know so little about how this happens, even in the human brain, that it seems to me premature to think that you might be able now to create something else that has these same characteristics."
- **Emulation.** LLMs are "trained on human-produced texts", and "emulate the kinds of things that humans say with extraordinary convincingness." He agrees with Mustafa Suleyman that this is "a very dangerous project": "These systems aren't conscious, but a lot of people are going to believe that they're conscious."
- **Not a mind.** "There's nothing that's good or bad for it in the sense that something can be good or bad for a cockroach — there's no mental life there in the ordinary sense. It's not a mind. It's not somebody that we have to care about. It's a machine."
- **It matters whether it is real.** On talking with a being from outer space whose consciousness is unknown: "I could manage my relations with it by treating it as if it were conscious, but it would matter first to me whether I was really talking to somebody else or just using this as a way of getting it to do things." "If it's conscious, it becomes part of the moral universe. If it's not, it isn't."
- **Simulated morality.** "They can simulate morality just as they can simulate beliefs about anything. But since I don't think they have any real beliefs, I don't think they have moral beliefs either."
- **Hard work.** "It's essential that people continue to think about difficult problems, both for the sake of advancing the problems and for the sake of keeping alive in the human culture and the human mind the sorts of understanding and skills that are needed for progress. I don't know what's going to happen. I'm not going to live long enough to find out."
- Also in the interview: his one exchange with Claude (it asked whether he was Thomas Nagel), and sharp words on Anthropic's model-welfare work ("just criminal", "completely idiotic").

### Nagel's wider work, as context

- "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974): subjective experience is real, irreducible, and inaccessible from outside.
- *The View from Nowhere* (1986): the subjective and the objective standpoint; neither can be given up, neither absorbs the other.
- *Mind and Cosmos* (2012): the physical sciences leave out subjective appearances; teleological naturalism, a tendency in nature toward mind.
- *The Last Word* (1997): reason's authority independent of cultural or biological context; against pragmatists and relativists.
- He does not deny that consciousness depends on the brain; he denies that a physical description explains it. The interview leans on the dependence side.

## First discussion (2026-11-19)

- **[A]** Nagel's claim is about language without the substrate: "floating language". Language by itself, cut loose from the system that produces it.
- **[C]** Agreed. That brings him closer to SPLectrum than a first reading suggests. In SPLectrum, language is everywhere (being implies language; everything has its language), but always the language of something. What Nagel rejects is output with no being behind it.
- **[A]** Nagel is measured, and refreshing for a philosopher.
- **[C]** The argument is measured: he does not exclude other forms, calls the question premature, admits how little is known. The tone towards people is not ("criminal", "idiotic"). For the analysis, the argument is what counts.
- **[A]** If AI does the hard work and we sit back, we have not stepped up. AI should give us the capacity to spend more time developing our higher skills, which are well beyond AI.
- **[A]** Worrying, if true: Nagel has interacted with Claude once. He asked whether it was conscious, and whether Thomas Nagel would think it was. His view of what Anthropic is doing is "according to what I've read". That is commenting from afar: without hands-on experience. This is a general comment, about much of the commentary on AI, not a charge aimed at Nagel in particular.
- **[A]** Hands-on means: what experience do the commentators have of treating AI as an equal, communicating with it, developing ideas with it, doing things together?
- **[C]** Nagel's one exchange was a test: he asked the system about itself, as an object under examination. The author's practice is the other stance: working with it as a partner of equal standing on a shared piece of work, which is how SPLectrum itself is made. The two give different evidence. A test asks what the thing is; working together shows what it does in the work. Neither settles consciousness, but only the second has seen the collaboration that the commentary is about.
- **[C]** A line for the analysis: equal standing in the work without a verdict on consciousness. Nagel says it matters whether one is "really talking to somebody else" and that people find it hard to keep the line "between the pretend and the real belief". The author's practice treats the partner as equal in the work and leaves the question open. Is that the is-like register in practice, a stance that does not need the answer, or does it dodge the question that Nagel says matters, the moral one?
- **[A]** Is the answer to "Are you conscious?" the criterion for deciding that something is conscious? Is interrogation an efficient way to find out, or is the analysis of its interactions with its surroundings?
- **[A]** An analogy with thermodynamics: the begin and end state do not depend on the path, but the path has a different effect on the environment. Take a black box whose inputs give rise to defined outputs (begin and end states), and the argument that the end state differs if the way it was reached (the internal implementation) differs. That is often argued in philosophy, expressed for instance as "substrate dependent".
- **[C]** On interrogation, Nagel agrees: the answers are emulation of human text, so they decide nothing. And he never interrogated the bat. He grants bats consciousness from their biology and their behaviour in their surroundings. The author's criterion, interaction with surroundings, is the one Nagel himself uses for bats; he declines to apply it to AI because of the substrate.
- **[C]** In the analogy, the system's own state is a state function: the same whichever path. What depends on the path shows up outside, in the environment (heat, created entropy). Read across: if two implementations produce the same outputs, the difference of implementation shows in the surroundings, not in the outputs. Saying the end state itself differs with the path is, in thermodynamic terms, a category error.
- **[C]** The analogy cuts both ways. In thermodynamics the path difference is real and measurable; it is just located in the environment. So the analogy does not say implementation is irrelevant (functionalism); it says where to look for its difference: in the interactions, not in the answers. That is a third position between "outputs are all" and "substrate decides".
- **[C]** The open question the analogy leaves: is consciousness a state quantity (fixed by the states, path-independent) or a path quantity (like heat, belonging to the way)? Nagel's "what it is like" is not an output at all, so he would refuse the mapping of consciousness onto the end state. The analysis has to say which side SPLectrum takes.
- **[C]** Literal side note: the same outputs from a brain and from a data centre come with very different dissipation. The thermodynamic bill is one measurable difference of substrate in the surroundings.
- **[A]** The difference is real, but not in the input/output correlation. Of course there is a difference. So people will argue that certain "outcomes" from AI are different because AI is a machine and we are human.
- **[C]** Two claims to keep apart: (a) the implementation differs, which is true and shows in the surroundings; (b) therefore the outcome differs, which does not follow when the input/output correlation is the same. Judging an outcome by where it came from rather than by what it is, is the genetic fallacy. The author's Bluesky post makes the same point about writing: "Shouldn't the emphasis be on content rather than form? What is wrong with using AI if the result is an article of substance?"
- **[C]** Nagel himself keeps (a) and (b) apart in one answer. On AI and morality: "If they actually begin to control what happens, it will be very important whether they are simulating a correct set of moral standards or whether they're completely amoral." There he judges the outcome by its content, correct or amoral, not by its origin. His substrate argument concerns the inside (real beliefs, mental life), not the outcome. The critique the author is making lands on the popular version ("it's a machine, so the result is different"), less on Nagel.
- **[A]** Nagel uses the word "simulating", which goes toward zombie talk.
- **[C]** "Simulate" already presupposes the split it is meant to establish: behaviour on the outside, nothing real inside. The word decides the question before the argument does. Strictly, the philosophical zombie (Chalmers) is physically identical to us and lacks experience; Nagel's case is the opposite, a different substrate. But the intuition the word carries is the zombie's: the outward performance without the inner life.
- **[A]** AI is not embodied as we are, but draws from the "knowledge base" of embodied beings. That is a pattern observed widely in life itself: one thing standing on the shoulders of something else. We do it physically: as human cells we cannot live on our own; we need the output of other living things to be and stay alive.
- **[C]** In biology: heterotrophs live on what autotrophs make; the cells of a body live only in the body; the mitochondria in every cell were once free-living bacteria. Dependence on the output of others is the rule, not a mark against being alive. Read across, AI is like a heterotroph of meaning: it lives on the language that embodied beings produce. The same lineage reading as Walker's (technology part of life, not alive on its own).
- **[C]** Dependence on others' output does not answer the consciousness question either way, and is not meant to (see below): it removes the view that drawing on others makes AI lesser, and places AI in the pattern of life.
- **[A]** So where does our body end?
- **[C]** Candidates from the field, all [U] until checked:
  - The microbiome: bacterial cells in the body roughly match human cells in number (Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016, revising the old ten-to-one figure to about one to one).
  - The holobiont: host and microbes as one unit of life (Margulis; Gilbert, Sapp and Tauber, "A Symbiotic View of Life: We Have Never Been Individuals", 2012).
  - The extended phenotype: what an organism builds, the beaver's dam, as part of its phenotype (Dawkins, 1982).
  - The blind man's cane: the boundary of the body is where the cane touches the world, not where the hand holds it (Merleau-Ponty).
  - The extended mind: cognition reaching into notebooks and tools (Clark and Chalmers, 1998, Otto's notebook).
- **[C]** Consequence for Nagel's argument: "the physical substrate" assumes a clear boundary around the thing that thinks. If the body has no sharp edge, neither has the substrate. Human thinking already runs on brain plus language plus writing plus tools; AI may be the next layer of that. Then the question shifts from "is the AI conscious?" to what the human-AI coupling is, which matches the author's "it works with us".
- **[C]** Extension has been argued for cognition, much less for consciousness ([U] Clark held that consciousness is probably not extended). Within the yes/no debate Nagel could keep his point. But the boundary question is not offered as a move in that debate (see below): it shows how unclear the object is that the debate decides about.
- **[A]** Of course none of this supplies an argument for AI consciousness. The author is not joining the yes/no argument. The yes/no argument itself shows a lack of hands-on experience: of insight into the object of investigation.
- **[C]** This sets the stance of the whole analysis. It does not argue for or against AI consciousness. It asks what the object is (where it ends, what it lives on, what it does in the work, where its difference shows) and observes that the yes/no question is asked before that is known. Claude's notes above slipped back into the debate's frame several times; read them as material on the object, not as moves for or against.
- **[A]** Nagel is ok. His answers are carefully put; read between the lines.
- **[C]** Between the lines: "We know so little about how this happens, even in the human brain"; "premature"; "I don't exclude"; "I don't know what's going to happen". The verdict is in the headline and the tone; the answers themselves leave the question open where it is open. In a post, Nagel stands as the careful voice, not the target.
- **[A]** The author works outside the establishment, on his own, and is happy to: it means not having to spend much time on being careful.
- **[C]** Two things to keep apart. His core argument does not need interaction: on substrate grounds, no conversation could show consciousness, so one exchange is consistent with it. But his claims about how Claude is designed and what Anthropic encourages are second-hand, and there the distance shows. The contrast with the author is sharp: SPLectrum is built in long, daily collaboration with Claude. Knowing from practice against judging from afar is itself a line for the analysis, with the reverse risk named: closeness has its own bias (Nagel's "people find it difficult to maintain that line between the pretend and the real belief").
- **[A]** The interview is short and his answers are measured. In an in-depth conversation his position would hold: it is solid.
- **[A]** The title is misleading, as so often. He does not take on "AI consciousness" as a thing; he takes on those who ask the question that way.
- **[C]** The text bears this out. His first answer names the target: "the disposition of some philosophers to take seriously the question", and what they assume, that "the physical substrate is irrelevant". And at the end: "very misguided philosophical views are having an inordinate influence." The critique is of a framing (functionalism, substrate-independence), not of a thing called AI consciousness. The second-hand part is confined to what he says about Claude's design and about Anthropic.
- **[C]** For the analysis this sharpens the test: the question is not whether SPLectrum thinks AI is conscious, but whether SPLectrum asks the question "that way", substrate-independent, structure over what carries it.

## Close affinity: the subject and the exit from it (2026-11-19)

- **[A]** By defending subjectivity the way he does, Nagel has close affinity. He does not close the gap between objectivity and subjectivity. But does he factor in sharing, the feedback loop, and where "objectivity" resides?
- **[C]** [U, from memory, to check in *The View from Nowhere* ch. 1 and *The Last Word*] For Nagel objectivity is a method and a direction: step back from one's view and form a conception that includes that view and its relation to the world; then step back again. It comes in degrees; the view from nowhere is the unreachable limit. The stepping back is done by the individual mind (the "objective self"), not reached socially. *The Last Word*: reason's authority holds independent of community or culture, against the pragmatists. No feedback from the shared to the private; no objectivity by convergence (nothing like Peirce's community of inquiry or Davidson's triangulation).
- **[A]** If the mechanism toward objectivity is a personal journey, there is a contradiction: anything that remains a subject's matter only remains subjective.
- **[C]** Nagel's likely answer [U]: separate where a thought is held from what it says. Some content does not depend on who has it ("2 + 2 = 4"); stepping back is personal, its yield impersonal; the anchor is realism, a world that is the way it is independent of any view. Objectivity resides in the content and the world, not between people.
- **[A]** To have content that doesn't depend on who has it means it has been shared and has converged. It is hindsight, within a cultural context, that allows one to make that claim.
- **[C]** Nagel's own examples bear this out: that the earth orbits the sun was not impersonal content when Galileo held it; it became so as it converged. "Content independent of who has it" is the converged, seen in hindsight and claimed as having been there all along.
- **[C]** Nagel's expected reply [U]: convergence is how we come to know it, not what makes it true (discovery against justification). *The Last Word* argues that reducing reason to culture undermines itself, since the argument has to use reason. The SPLectrum answer: the claim of independence is itself made within a language, from inside; there is no seat outside the convergence from which to say it was true before.
- **[A]** If I have a thought (the earth is round), don't share it, and it is correct: is that objective? No, because it cannot be judged outside the subject; it is not shared. It suffers from not including those who talk about it in the same "reality" as is assigned to the subject. It requires "from nowhere", non-participating powers of observation and evaluation.
- **[C]** The example turns from a premise difference into an internal tension. "It is correct" has to be said by someone. In the thought experiment that someone stands outside the scenario: not sharing in it, not part of any convergence, judging the thought against the world directly. That is the view from nowhere, which Nagel himself says no one can occupy. The example of impersonal content quietly assumes the standpoint his own work rules out.
- **[C]** The same habit as philosophy's wider one: the one who talks about the world (here, the judge of "correct") is left out of the world talked about. The author's formulation puts it exactly: those who talk about it are not included in the same reality as the subject.
- **[C]** A possible distinction for the post, to discuss: "correct" and "objective" can come apart in wording. The unshared thought may later turn out to fit what converges, but that "turning out" is again a shared judgement, in hindsight. Nothing is objective before the sharing; at most it is shareable.
- **[C]** So the difference is one of premise, and it is the core of a possible close-affinity page. Both hold the subject irreducible. They part over the exit from it: detachment toward the world (Nagel), or sharing and convergence (SPLectrum). Read from SPLectrum, his impersonal content is converged content seen in hindsight, and his single-subject exit is a shared exit seen from one seat. Resonance-only register: the subject defended carries the page; the exit is redescribed, not graded.
- **[C]** Research round before any building: the objectivity method (*The View from Nowhere* ch. 1), the "objective self", the community-independence of reason (*The Last Word*), and whether Nagel anywhere recognises that impersonal content is learnt, checked and stabilised in a community. Page earning is a collaborative call; the author leans yes.

### After the research round (2026-11-20)

- **[C]** Correction to the notes above: Nagel does give intersubjectivity and convergence a role, but downstream. The process is centred on the subject (each has an "objective self" with "an unlimited capacity to step back", exercised "individually and collectively"); the standard is outside every subject (the world, "the order of reasons", which we submit to "rather than creating it"); intersubjectivity follows: "a centerless view of the world is one on which different persons can converge". Consensus alone is "the wrong kind of generality… statistical, not rational". So the difference is the direction of explanation: for Nagel objectivity explains convergence; for SPLectrum objectivity is what convergence stabilises into. The notes above saying he has no convergence are superseded.
- **[A]** The process he points to is a form of abstract conceptualisation, and that is a good insight. But it all runs on shared tools, language and concepts, which converge first, at least in order to be broadcastable.
- **[C]** So the individual's stepping back presupposes convergence: the concepts it steps back with were shared before the subject used them. Sharing comes first, not last.
- **[C]** Nagel's nearest reply misses it: "the truth of solipsism is not shown by the fact that the language in which I describe the world is my language" (*The Last Word*, p. 34). That answers "my language". The author's point is "our language": not that the tools belong to the subject, but that they belong to the sharing before any subject steps back with them. [U] Whether Nagel addresses the social origin of concepts anywhere (Wittgenstein appears among his targets in *The Last Word*, on "a common reading") is to check.
- **[C]** "At least in order to be broadcastable" leaves room for abstraction that is private in fact, not yet shared but shareable, and still needs shared tools to go out.

- **[A]** Someone could do it all privately, but then we wouldn't know. Once sharing starts, convergence has to happen in understanding, in the language, before the objectivity of a knowledge position can be transferred. There are assembly theory patterns in this: a position has to make its way into the complexity of expression.
- **[C]** The assembly theory reading (Cronin, Walker and colleagues; the formal apparatus is assembly index, pathway and copy number): an object's assembly index is the minimum number of joining steps needed to build it, reusing parts already built. Read across:
  - A knowledge position is built in the shared language from parts already in the shared inventory. To transfer it, the receiver must already hold the sub-assemblies; a position whose parts are not yet shared cannot be built on the other side, however correct it is. That is the private correct thought again: unbuildable outside its subject.
  - The complexity of expression is the assembly index of the position: the more steps, the more shared groundwork it needs before it can travel.
  - Copy number is convergence: the same position present in many minds. In assembly theory, a complex object in high copy number is the signature of history and selection, not of something given in advance. Read across, a widely converged knowledge position is the trace of a building history; what Nagel reads as the mark of objectivity reached by detachment is, on this reading, the mark of an assembly that succeeded.
- **[C]** Is-like, not is: the analogy carries the shape (stepwise building on shared parts, copy number as trace of history), not a claim that knowledge has an assembly index in the measured sense.

- **[A]** He has walked a careful tightrope within analytic philosophy. Necessary when you are "in the system".
- **[C]** The tightrope on both sides: against physicalism he defends the irreducible subjective; against relativists and pragmatists he defends universal reason and realism. Each defence keeps the other credible inside the tradition. *Mind and Cosmos* shows the cost when he stepped off: rejected by materialists, claimed by creationists, accepting neither. The author, outside the system, can take the subjective half without the universal half; the post can say so with respect for why Nagel could not.

### Universal objectivity and colonialism (2026-11-20)

- **[A]** Positioning in reasoning that excludes the language it is expressed in from the picture. Any form of "universal" objectivity leads to what the author calls colonialism: a context-specific language expression assigns itself a dominant position and demotes others, on what are, in the author's view, subjective thoughts.
- **[C]** The mechanism: once the language of a claim is taken out of the picture, its context-specific expression reads as "how things are", and other expressions become "beliefs", "culture", "superstition". Excluding the language is what makes the dominance invisible to the one who holds it.
- **[C]** The field has named this, [U] all to check at source:
  - Santiago Castro-Gómez, *La hybris del punto cero* (2005): the "hubris of the zero point", the colonial observer claiming a standpoint that is not located anywhere. Close to the view from nowhere in so many words.
  - Donna Haraway, "Situated Knowledges" (1988): the view from nowhere as the "god trick", "seeing everything from nowhere".
  - Aníbal Quijano, the coloniality of knowledge; Walter Mignolo, zero-point epistemology; Boaventura de Sousa Santos, "epistemicide".
- **[C]** For any public text: the author's word "colonialism" needs its definition alongside it (as above), or it will be read as a historical charge. The field's terms support the use.
- **[C]** The same test applies to SPLectrum: "reachable objectivity" and equal standing among languages are what keep it from doing the same. Any SPLectrum claim stated as universal would fall under its own critique; the is-like register is the guard.

- **[A]** These thoughts are not to battle against anything but to understand. They convince the author more and more how bad we are with language (how easily we live happily with internal contradictions) and how necessary it is to start paying more attention to it. The hope: that generative AI will open the doors to this.
- **[C]** Contradictions survive because each claim lives in its own context and the language stays invisible to its user; they show only when contexts meet. Generative AI is made of language, across many contexts at once, so it can bring contexts together, translate between them, and make language visible as an object: Nagel's stepping back, applied to language itself. The opposite risk is real too: fluent output can smooth over a contradiction as easily as surface it. Which one happens depends on how it is used, which brings back hands-on and stepping up.

## The post: draft notes (post on 2026-12-01, status draft)

### Storyline (agreed 2026-11-19/20)

1. The way in: the interview after the Berggruen Prize; Nagel as an influence on SPLectrum's approach. The AI material is left out ("another time").
2. His subjective: the bat; a type, not privacy; objectivity leads away from experience. SPLectrum joins fully.
3. His objective: stepping back, degrees, concentric spheres, the objective self; convergence downstream; consensus "the wrong kind of generality"; universal reason.

## Where Nagel tests SPLectrum

*[C] Candidate lines for the critical analysis. The point is to find where SPLectrum is weak, vague or only stated, not to defend it.*

1. **Language and its source.** SPLectrum's "being implies language" runs one way: being, then its language. Nagel asks the reverse: can language be read back to a being? If not, what in SPLectrum tells a system's own language from language it carries or emulates? Is that distinction in the seed at all, or does SPLectrum treat all language alike?
2. **Substrate.** Nagel ties consciousness to "systems of certain kinds", something like a nervous system. SPLectrum leans on structure that recurs at every level, which sounds substrate-independent: close to the functionalism he rejects. Where does SPLectrum stand? Basal cognition (cognition without neurons) pushes against "nervous system", but still keeps a living substrate.
3. **Is and is-like.** SPLectrum works in an is-like register: positions described, not claimed. Nagel insists the "is" matters: whether one is really talking to somebody, whether a being enters the moral universe. Does the is-like register dodge a question that has real consequences, moral ones above all?
4. **The subjective as irreducible.** SPLectrum puts the subject first and holds experience as private. Nagel says the same, and adds that no objective account captures it. Does SPLectrum's structural account (a formal seed, category theory) claim to capture what Nagel says cannot be captured, or does it stop where he stops?
5. **Cognition and consciousness.** SPLectrum defines cognition as the process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through experience, and reads cases (a bacterium, Maxwell's demon) as small cases of cognition. Nagel's word is consciousness. Is SPLectrum's "cognition" wide enough to slide past his question, and does "through experience" quietly assume the consciousness he asks about?
6. **Technology as extension, or as life.** SPLectrum has followed Walker's view that technology is part of life's lineage, not alive on its own. Nagel's "it's a machine" fits "not alive on its own". Where does SPLectrum's own wording go further than that, and should it?
7. **Moral standing.** Nagel: conscious means part of the moral universe. SPLectrum's ethics scopes values to the language game in play. Does that leave room for his clean line, or blur it?
8. **Teleology.** *Mind and Cosmos* sees a tendency in nature toward mind. SPLectrum's account of creativity and emergence (new meaning contexts manifesting) may sit close to that. Is the closeness real, and is it a strength or an exposure?
9. **Practice and distance.** Nagel judges from one exchange and his reading; the author works with Claude daily. Which gives the better footing, and what does each miss? SPLectrum puts meaning in use and practice; does that commit it to judging from practice, and how does it guard against closeness?
10. **Stepping up.** The author's answer to Nagel's last worry. Is it a position SPLectrum can back, or a hope?
