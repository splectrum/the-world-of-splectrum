## Part 1 — The position, in the author's sequence

1. **[A]** The complexity-science sense of emergence — "derivable, but only by running it" — is a very subjective statement. The behaviour is in the rules; what is missing is only what we judge as "derivable". "Derivable" probably leans towards the deterministic.
2. **[A]** Strong emergence suffers from it in the same way: a subjective position that gets elevated to absolute.
3. **[A]** That the readings of emergence are measured against a deterministic ideal is *contingent*.
4. **[A]** Emergence is something that *becomes apparent*. It is always against a background of knowns, and we like to work with it as if there were no unknowns.
5. **[A]** This slots in with evolution. It is another example where a here-now position gets turned into a static forever-after.
6. **[A]** Taking the outside view: there is a reality we describe. If that reality has evolution, then the question is *which reality?* A lot of the core concepts of discussion rely on non-evolution.

### Compact statement

Emergence names what comes into view against a background taken as known. The judgement that something "cannot be derived" belongs to a here-now background; read as "cannot, in principle, ever" it becomes a static forever-after. The same move — a here-now position frozen into a permanent one — recurs wherever a concept relies on non-evolution of the reality it describes.

## Part 2 — Supporting external material, by claim (all [C], all to verify)

### On claim 1: weak emergence

- Mark Bedau, "Weak Emergence" (*Philosophical Perspectives* 11, 1997): a macro-state is weakly emergent if it is derivable from the micro-dynamics and external conditions, but only by simulation — no shortcut. Epistemic by design: the behaviour is fully fixed by the rules; what is absent is a shorter route than running them.
- "No shortcut" is a judgement relative to our methods of derivation; in general it cannot be proved (cf. Wolfram's "computational irreducibility").
- Derivation-by-running presupposes rules that fix outcomes; for stochastic rules Bedau has to patch, deriving per run or over ensembles.

### On claim 2: strong emergence

- C. D. Broad, *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925): some properties of wholes cannot be derived, even in principle, from the most complete knowledge of the parts. "In principle" is where a judgement relative to available knowledge is read as a fact about nature (new properties, new laws).
- Historical illustration: chemistry was the British emergentists' confident example; quantum mechanics then began deriving chemical bonding, so the "in principle" turned out to depend on the theory available at the time. Philosophers of chemistry still dispute how complete that derivation is.
- The weak/strong (epistemic/ontological) distinction is the field's own: e.g. Chalmers, "Strong and Weak Emergence" (2006).

### On claim 4: becoming apparent

- Etymology: Latin *emergere*, "to rise out of, come up out of (water), come into view". At root the word is about appearing, not about being created.
- George Henry Lewes, *Problems of Life and Mind* (1875), contrasted "resultant" effects, traceable to their causes, with "emergent" ones.
- How each reading relates to its background of knowns:
  - weak emergence — the knowns are the rules; the unknown is the route from rules to behaviour;
  - strong emergence — the knowns are "full knowledge of the parts"; "in principle" closes off the unknown, treating the knowns as complete;
  - physics (universality, renormalisation) — an account of *why* the unknowns (micro-details) do not matter: the unknowns are built in rather than assumed away.
- Analogy: the closed-world assumption in logic and databases — what is not derivable from what is held is taken as false.

## Part 3 — Corrections and pitfalls

- **[corrected]** Claude: both readings, weak and strong, are measured against a deterministic ideal of deduction from the parts, differing only in whether the failure to derive is put down to us or to the world. Author: that is *contingent* — the deterministic ideal is where the debate happened to start, not something the concept needs. The deeper structure is the background of knowns (claim 4); determinism is one way of closing that background, not the only one.
- **Register.** On the site the observation is SPLectrum-side. Pages and posts that present the field's senses of emergence keep them on their own terms: show the history, do not score the positions (no "strong emergence elevates an epistemic judgement to an absolute" in a field-voice post).
