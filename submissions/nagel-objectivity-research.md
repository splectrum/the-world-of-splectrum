# Nagel on objectivity: research round (2026-11-19)

*Working note, not a site page. Nothing here is a decision.*

**Context, in place.** A post is planned: SPLectrum joins Thomas Nagel on the subjective (irreducible, first-person) but has questions about his objective. Nagel's objectivity is detachment by an individual; SPLectrum's is converged, shared subjectivity ("reachable objectivity"). The claims below were stated from memory in discussion. This round checks them at source. The checked material goes onto the Nagel person page first, then into the post.

**Marks.** [C, S#] = checked against source S#, opened, quoted as found (OCR slips silently corrected, nothing else). [C, as quoted in S#] = verbatim, but second-hand: the primary was not opened. [U] = unverified. "Reading [C]" = Claude's interpretation, not Nagel's words. Verdicts: CONFIRMED / CORRECTED / UNVERIFIED. Sources (S1…) are listed at the end.

**Word test, up front.** "Objectivity", "the objective self", "the view from nowhere", "intersubjectivity", "convergence" and "detachment" are all Nagel's own words (sources below). "Converged subjectivity" and "reachable objectivity" are SPLectrum's terms, not his.

## 1. *The View from Nowhere*: objectivity as method, degrees, limit, objective self, realism

**Verdict: CONFIRMED** in substance and largely in wording. Text of the Introduction checked against a verbatim reproduction (S1). Page numbers are from secondary citations. The objective-self material was checked in its 1979 and 1983 forms (S3, S4), which the book's ch. IV reworks.

**Method, stepping back, repeated** (VfN, Introduction; text [C, S1]; p. 4 per Tomasello 2014 and Stoljar 2006, [C, as cited in S11]):
- "Objectivity is a method of understanding. It is beliefs and attitudes that are objective in the primary sense. Only derivatively do we call objective the truths that can be arrived at in this way. To acquire a more objective understanding of some aspect of life or the world, we step back from our initial view of it and form a new conception which has that view and its relation to the world as its object. In other words, we place ourselves in the world that is to be understood. The old view then comes to be regarded as an appearance, more subjective than the new view, and correctable or confirmable by reference to it. The process can be repeated, yielding a still more objective conception."
- The opening sentence (p. 3, [C, as cited in S12]): "This book is about a single problem: how to combine the perspective of a particular person inside the world with an objective view of that same world, the person and his viewpoint included." [C, S1]

**Degrees** (Introduction, [C, S1]; p. 5 per several citing works, [C, as cited in S11]):
- "the distinction between more subjective and more objective views is really a matter of degree, and it covers a wide spectrum. A view or form of thought is more objective than another if it relies less on the specifics of the individual's makeup and position in the world, or on the character of the particular type of creature he is. The wider the range of subjective types to which a form of understanding is accessible—the less it depends on specific subjective capacities—the more objective it is."
- "The standpoint of morality is more objective than that of private life, but less objective than the standpoint of physics. We may think of reality as a set of concentric spheres, progressively revealed as we detach gradually from the contingencies of the self."

**Limits, built in from the first page** (Introduction, [C, S1]):
- The process "will not always yield a result", and can give "a false objectification of an aspect of reality that cannot be better understood from a more objective standpoint" (he cites Nietzsche here).
- "Appearance and perspective are essential parts of what there is, and in some respects they are best understood from a less detached standpoint. Realism underlies the claims of objectivity and detachment, but it supports them only up to a point."

**The view from nowhere as an unreachable limit.** The clearest statement found is in the 1979 essay "Subjective and Objective" (*Mortal Questions*, pp. 196–213): "To grasp this by detaching more and more from our own point of view is the unreachable ideal at which the pursuit of objectivity aims." [C, as quoted in S5; page of the passage within the essay U]. In the book, the degrees and concentric-spheres passages carry the same idea. I did not find the words "unreachable" or "limit" in the book's own text: [U] whether the book restates it in those terms.

**The objective self, a capacity within each subject:**
- Tanner Lectures, 1979 (S3, p. 94): the "I" in "I am TN" is identified "with an objective self that each of us contains, that has an unlimited capacity to step back from the standpoint of the person I am in order to form a new conception of the world in which that person and his states are located." "Essentially I am a subject that apprehends a centerless world. Essentially I have no particular point of view." [C, S3]
- "The Objective Self" (1983, S4, p. 226): "I shall call it the objective self, because it is the self that apprehends objective reality. The objective self views the world through TN." p. 227: "It is the perspectiveless subject that constructs a centerless conception of the world by casting all perspectives into that world." pp. 227–8: "the objective self is only part of the point of view of an ordinary person, and its objectivity is developed to different degrees in different persons and at different stages of life and civilization." p. 228: "each of you has one. Or perhaps I should say each of you is one, for I do not mean to imply that the objective self is a distinct entity." [C, S4]
- The same "basic step" passage is in VfN ch. IV, p. 63 [C, as cited in S10 and in a snippet of Bauhn, *The Value of Courage*]. Caution: Velleman (S9, n. 2) judges the book's chapter "considerably watered down" compared with the 1983 paper. The exact book wording is [U].

**Realism** (VfN ch. VI, pp. 90–91, [C, as quoted in S13 and S14, which agree]): "I shall defend a form of realism according to which our grasp on the world is limited not only in respect of what we can know but also in respect of what we can conceive. In a very strong sense, the world extends beyond the reach of our minds." From *The Last Word*, p. 30 [C, S2], one of the "commonplaces" he says the subjectivist cannot account for: "(3) If a belief is true, it would be true even if no one believed it."

## 2. Does Nagel give sharing, community, intersubjective agreement or convergence a role? (key question)

**Verdict: CORRECTED.** The memory version was: the individual does the stepping back, nothing is reached socially, and there is no convergence. Nagel does give intersubjectivity and convergence a role, and he names them himself. The role is as a **consequence** and as **evidence** of objectivity, never as its **ground** or as what constitutes it. The capacity sits in each individual ("within"). It can be exercised individually *and* collectively. The standard it answers to is independent of both the individual and the community. He considers the constitutive view explicitly, Peirce and Rorty included, and rejects it.

**Intersubjectivity: closely connected, not sufficient, not the essence**
- "Subjective and Objective" (1979): "Since a kind of intersubjective agreement characterizes even what is most subjective, the transition to a more objective viewpoint is not accomplished merely through intersubjective agreement. Nor does it proceed by an increase of imaginative scope that provides access to many subjective points of view other than one's own. Its essential character, in all the examples cited, is externality or detachment." And: "Problems arise because the same individual is the occupant of both viewpoints." And: "the less an appearance depends on contingencies of this particular self, the more it is capable of being arrived at from a variety of points of view." [C, as quoted in S5. Fuchs puts some words in capitals for emphasis; the capitals are his, not Nagel's, and are removed here.]
- "The Objective Self" (1983, p. 228): "Because a centerless view of the world is one on which different persons can converge, there is a close connection between objectivity and intersubjectivity. By placing TN in a world along with everyone else I pursue a conception of him and his point of view that others may share. At the first stage the intersubjectivity is still entirely human, and the objectivity is correspondingly limited." Going beyond the human "requires that we find within ourselves the capacity to view the world in some sense as very different creatures also might view it… The pursuit of objectivity requires the cultivation of a rather austere universal objective self." [C, S4] In VfN this is p. 63 [C, as quoted in S10, which reads "different people can converge"; exact book wording U].
- Tanner Lectures (1979, p. 96): stated from the subjective side, the problem is how "given my personal experiential perspective" one can form a conception of the world as it is, "(The question may also be asked from the point of view of the collective human perspective rather than from that of an individual.)" [C, S3]. Reading [C]: the collective human perspective is placed on the *subjective* side of his divide. It is something objectivity steps back from, not where objectivity resides.

**Consensus: an effect of reasoning, not its ground** (*The Last Word*, [C, S2]):
- p. 31: "In most cases we will then conclude that reason and objectivity are not grounded in consensus, but on the contrary, that where consensus is available, it arises from the convergence among different individuals, all reasoning to get at the truth. There is a consensus on the nondenumerability of the real numbers because the demonstration of it is conclusive, and not vice versa."
- p. 5: relativists may introduce "a simulacrum of it in the form of a condition of consensus among a linguistic or scientific or political community", but "it is the wrong kind of generality—since at its outer bounds it is statistical, not rational."
- p. 69: "Instead of logic resting on agreement in judgments and usage by members of a community, the agreement, where it exists, has to be explained in terms of the logic whose validity we all recognize."
- p. 40, his concession: for word usage, "one has to recognize that objectivity can't really outstrip community practice. That is not true, however, of the content of thought, as opposed to the meanings of words." Some concepts (humour) have an extension "determined ultimately by agreement in response among some set of persons", but these are "special cases." p. 41: "the possibility of a language requires at some level automatic agreement in judgments and linguistic practice… But such agreement is not all the concept consists in."
- p. 128, on Peirce: "the familiar Peircian idea of convergence at the end of inquiry", but "it is certainly not presented as a definition of truth, but as a hope that rational inquiry will lead us to truths that depend not on our minds but on nature."
- p. 117: "Thus I find within myself the universal standards that enable me to get outside of myself."
- p. 143, the book's last sentence: "If we think at all, we must think of ourselves, individually and collectively, as submitting to the order of reasons rather than creating it."

**Collective processes granted, community independence kept** (*Mind and Cosmos*, [C, S6]):
- p. 72: "Thought and reasoning are correct or incorrect in virtue of something independent of the thinker's beliefs, and even independent of the community of thinkers to which he belongs."
- p. 85: "The great cognitive shift is an expansion of consciousness from the perspectival form contained in the lives of particular creatures to an objective, world-encompassing form that exists both individually and intersubjectively. It was originally a biological evolutionary process, and in our species it has become a collective cultural process as well."
- p. 86: "by starting from the way things initially appear to us, we can use reason collectively to achieve justified beliefs about some of those objective truths—though some of those beliefs will probably be mistaken."
- p. 103: "In every area of thought we must rely ultimately on our judgments, tested by reflection, subject to correction by the counterarguments of others… Antirealism is always a conjectural possibility: the question can always be posed, whether there is anything more to truth in a certain domain than our tendency to reach certain conclusions in this way, perhaps in convergence with others." (He then argues against that antirealism.)

**Evidence role** (Tanner Lectures, pp. 116–17, on ethics, [C, S3]): "the degree to which agreement can be achieved and social prejudices transcended in the face of strong pressures suggests that something real is being investigated". In the same passage, social inculcation and cultural disagreement are "a poor reason to conclude that values have no objective reality", because "Other areas of knowledge are taught by social pressure". His examples of motivated disagreement over matters of fact include "the heliocentric theory" and "the theory of evolution."

**Headline for the post.** Nagel does not overlook sharing. He considers it and places it downstream of objectivity: convergence happens *because* reasoners track the same truth. So the difference with SPLectrum is not that he leaves sharing out. It is the direction of explanation. For him, convergence is explained by objectivity. In SPLectrum, objectivity is what convergence amounts to. Reading [C].

**His own replies to the post's questions, in his words** (the post should know they exist):
- To "it is correct has to be said by someone": "If there were nonsubjective thoughts, someone would still have to think them. So the formula that simply notes this cannot be used to demonstrate that everything is based on our responses. A tautology with which all parties to a dispute must agree cannot show that one of them is right." (*The Last Word*, p. 35, [C, S2])
- To "it remains expressed in language, within a cultural context": "the truth of solipsism is not shown by the fact that the language in which I describe the world is my language, and the truth of some other form of subjectivism is not shown by the fact that justification comes to an end at certain points at which there is natural agreement in judgments." (p. 34, [C, S2]) And ch. 3: the particular language one speaks "is essentially a tool of thought… Understanding it is a form of thought, but it is not the material of which thoughts are made" (p. 38). Explaining logical necessity by "the practices… of a linguistic community" is "the attempt to explain the more fundamental in terms of the less fundamental" (p. 39). [C, S2]
- To "the subjective/objective distinction is itself drawn within a human standpoint": the bat paper already declines this: "even if the distinction between more subjective and more objective descriptions or viewpoints can itself be made only within a larger human point of view. I do not accept this kind of conceptual relativism" (1974, p. 443 n. 9, [C, S7]).
- On adding "absolutely": "'2 + 2 = 4 and cruelty is evil, not just for us, but absolutely'… may, in the immortal words of Bernard Williams, be one thought too many" (p. 35, [C, S2]). He prefers to "just come to a stop with certain kinds of judgments". So "2 + 2 = 4" is his example. But he does not claim an outside seat: he says the thoughts cannot be got outside of.

## 3. *The Last Word* (1997): reason's universal authority, self-employment, targets, title

**Verdict: CONFIRMED,** with one sharpening: the argument is less "any critique must use reason" than "some thoughts cannot be got outside of".

- **Universal, against community and person** (p. 3, [C, S2]): "Reason, if there is such a thing, can serve as a court of appeal not only against the received opinions and habits of our community but also against the peculiarities of our personal perspective. It is something each individual can find within himself, but at the same time it has universal authority." pp. 3–4: whoever appeals to reason "purports to discover a source of authority within himself that is not merely personal, or societal, but universal". p. 10: "Reason purports to offer a method of transcending both the merely social and the merely personal."
- **The issue in one line** (p. 3): "whether the first person, singular or plural, is hiding at the bottom of everything we say or think." p. 5: "To reason is to think systematically in ways anyone looking over my shoulder ought to be able to recognize as correct."
- **Self-employment argument** (p. 7): reason "must be a form or category of thought from which there is no appeal beyond itself—whose validity is unconditional because it is necessarily employed in every purported challenge to itself." p. 19: Descartes "reveals that there are some thoughts which we cannot get outside of." p. 20: "one can't criticize something with nothing; and one can't criticize the more fundamental with the less fundamental." p. 143: "Reason is whatever we find we must use to understand anything, including itself."
- **Targets** (p. 7): the limitation of reason "in different ways in the treatments of Hume, Kant, and, on a common reading, Wittgenstein. More recently, versions of it are found in W. V. Quine, Nelson Goodman, Hilary Putnam, Bernard Williams, and Richard Rorty." His sympathies "are with Descartes and Frege." He calls the opposing tendency "subjectivism" and his own position "rationalist" (pp. 4–6). He mostly argues "not against the positions of particular philosophers but against a general tendency". **Rorty** is quoted and answered at length (pp. 28–30): "Science as Solidarity" ("replace the desire for objectivity… with the desire for solidarity with that community") and a 1994 *Academe* piece on mountains. Nagel's reply (p. 30): "The claim that there is nothing more to objectivity than solidarity with your speech community… directly contradicts the categorical statements it purports to be about". Sabina Lovibond is the other named target (p. 28). Ch. 7 turns the same argument on evolutionary naturalism.
- **Title** (Preface, p. vii): Kripke on logic and Dworkin on morality led him "to the general conclusion that the last word in philosophical disputes about the objectivity of any form of thought must lie in some unqualified thoughts about how things are—thoughts that remain, however hard we may try to get outside of them or to regard them merely as contingent psychological dispositions." In the text (p. 34): "Everything depends on the outcome of this peculiar contest over the last word. The subjectivist wants to give it to the recognition that justifications come to an end within our language and our practices. I want to give it to the justifications themselves". p. 35: "the constant temptation to give the last word—even the unsayable last word—to the first person, singular or plural". The book is dedicated to Dworkin and Kripke.
- Reading [C]: the title answers Wittgenstein (pp. 33–34), not a rhetorical flourish. The memory version, "reason cannot be stepped outside", holds. Nagel himself frames it as thoughts "we cannot get outside of", not as a seat outside language.

## 4. "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) on objectivity

**Verdict: CONFIRMED,** plus one finding that bears on how the site frames Nagel.

- **Objectivity as less dependence on viewpoint** (p. 444, [C, S7]): "In other areas the process of reduction is a move in the direction of greater objectivity, toward a more accurate view of the real nature of things. This is accomplished by reducing our dependence on individual or species-specific points of view toward the object of investigation… The less it depends on a specifically human viewpoint, the more objective is our description."
- **Why experience resists it** (p. 445): "If the subjective character of experience is fully comprehensible only from one point of view, then any shift to greater objectivity—that is, less attachment to a specific viewpoint—does not take us nearer to the real nature of the phenomenon: it takes us farther away from it."
- **Objectivity as a common reality** (p. 445): "Thus it is a condition of their referring to a common reality that their more particular viewpoints are not part of the common reality that they both apprehend." p. 442: the physical is "the kind that can be observed and understood from many points of view and by individuals with differing perceptual systems."
- **Not privacy: a type of viewpoint** (p. 441): "I am not adverting here to the alleged privacy of experience to its possessor. The point of view in question is not one accessible only to a single individual. Rather it is a type." p. 442: "There is a sense in which phenomenological facts are perfectly objective: one person can know or say of another what the quality of the other's experience is. They are subjective, however, in the sense that even this objective ascription of experience is possible only for someone sufficiently similar to the object of ascription to be able to adopt his point of view". Reading [C]: in 1974 the barrier is between *types* of experiencer (human/bat), not between one person and another. "Inaccessible from outside" holds for an outside of a different type. "Privacy" is not the bat paper's word, and Nagel sets it aside explicitly.
- **Objective phenomenology** (closing section, p. 449): "I should like to close with a speculative proposal. It may be possible to approach the gap between subjective and objective from another direction… This should be regarded as a challenge to form new concepts and devise a new method—an objective phenomenology not dependent on empathy or the imagination. Though presumably it would not capture everything, its goal would be to describe, at least in part, the subjective character of experiences in a form comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences." He gives the example of explaining sight to someone blind from birth: "One would reach a blank wall eventually". He adds that "structural features of perception might be more accessible to objective description, even though something would be left out."

- Objectivity as a method in degrees: step back, include the old view, repeat; "concentric spheres"; limits built in ("Realism… supports them only up to a point"). VfN Introduction, quotable now; pin pages when the book is fetched.
- The objective self as a capacity in every person, developed "to different degrees in different persons and at different stages of life and civilization" (1983; Tanner 1979). Cite the 1983 essay until the VfN p. 63 wording is checked.
- His own account of intersubjectivity: "a close connection between objectivity and intersubjectivity", but objectivity is "not accomplished merely through intersubjective agreement"; consensus "arises from the convergence among different individuals, all reasoning to get at the truth" (*The Last Word*, p. 31). This makes the page's "view from nowhere" paragraph more accurate than it is now.
- *The Last Word*: what the title means (Preface p. vii; p. 34); reason's authority "not merely personal, or societal, but universal"; the "cannot get outside of" argument; Rorty named and answered (pp. 28–30); the closing "individually and collectively, as submitting to the order of reasons rather than creating it."
- Bat paper: objectivity as less dependence on "individual or species-specific points of view"; the point of view is "a type", not privacy; the closing proposal of an "objective phenomenology".
- *Mind and Cosmos*: reason as "a collective cultural process" in our species, yet correct "even independent of the community of thinkers to which he belongs".
- Dated fact: named 2026 laureate of the Berggruen Prize for Philosophy & Culture (announced 17 Nov 2026; ceremony spring 2027). Add the Wired interview with its date once fetched.

**What the post can lean on (once the page carries it):**
- The join on the subjective: bat paper pp. 441–445, 449.
- The fork, stated fairly: Nagel *does* give convergence a place, as effect and evidence, explained by reasoning toward a truth independent of all of us. SPLectrum reverses the direction of explanation. That is a sharper and fairer contrast than "Nagel leaves sharing out", which the texts do not support.
- His own replies to the post's questions (p. 35 "someone would still have to think them"; p. 34 solipsism and "my language"; the bat footnote on "conceptual relativism"; "one thought too many"). In a descriptive register, the post shows his answer before redescribing it.
- "Reachable objectivity" against his degrees: he already concedes the view from nowhere is never reached (1979, "unreachable ideal") and that objectivity "is developed to different degrees". The open question between them is the ground (*The Last Word*), not reachability.

## Discard list

- "The stepping back is done by the individual mind, not reached socially; no objectivity by convergence; nothing like Peirce": wrong as stated. He discusses Peirce's convergence (*The Last Word*, p. 128) and grants collective and intersubjective processes. What he denies is that they constitute objectivity.
- "Galileo / earth orbits the sun" as Nagel's own example of impersonal content: not found. He cites "the heliocentric theory" only as a case of motivated disagreement (Tanner p. 116). If the post uses Galileo, it is the post's example, not his.
- "Privacy" as Nagel's framing of the bat argument: he explicitly sets "the alleged privacy of experience" aside (p. 441).
- "Received the Berggruen Prize this week": he was named in November 2026; the award is presented in spring 2027.
- Any VfN page number not listed above: not yet checked.
