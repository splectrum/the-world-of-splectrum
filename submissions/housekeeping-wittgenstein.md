## 1. Is the comprehensive treatment up to date?

**Finding.** The later concepts are well covered and mostly well quoted. The overview misses headlines:
- the life after 1929: Ramsey and Sraffa, the professorship, the withdrawn manuscript, the death;
- in the *Tractatus*, saying and showing, ethics and the mystical, and the ladder;
- the Vienna Circle's debt to the *Tractatus*;
- the influence on ordinary-language philosophy;
- mathematics, which he once named his chief contribution;
- *On Certainty*, which appears only in Key works;
- seeing aspects;
- the main reception dispute: two Wittgensteins, or one ("resolute" readers).

The SEP entry (Biletzki and Matar) had a substantive revision on 17 September 2026 [C, S1]. Its new §4 ("The Middle Wittgenstein") and §5 ("After the Investigations") frame the work in more than two stages.

**Proposed, line 12:**
> Wittgenstein's two books on language, thought and the world are usually read as two philosophies. The *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) held that a proposition is a picture of reality, sharing its logical form with what it depicts, that the propositions of logic are tautologies, and that what cannot be said can only be shown. The [logical empiricism](/positioning/subjects/p/philosophy-of-science/) of the Vienna Circle was indebted to it. The *Philosophical Investigations* (published in 1953, after his death) turned against the dogmatism of that book and of the philosophy before it. For a large class of cases, it says, the meaning of a word is its use in the language; the speaking of a language is part of an activity, or of a form of life; and philosophy describes rather than explains. Around and after the two books lie remarks on mathematics, which he once named his chief contribution, on psychology and on certainty. Oxford ordinary-language philosophy ([Ryle](/positioning/persons/r/ryle/), [Austin](/positioning/persons/a/austin-jl/)) drew on the later work. How the early and the later work relate is itself disputed. The orthodox reading sees a break between two philosophies; the "resolute" readers find one therapeutic aim running through both.

Born on 26 April 1889 in Vienna, the youngest of eight children of Karl Wittgenstein, who led the iron and steel industry of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. He became professor of philosophy at Cambridge in 1939, worked as a hospital porter in London during the Second World War, and resigned his chair in 1947. He had prepared the *Investigations* for publication in 1945 but withdrew it, and authorised only its posthumous publication. He died of cancer in Cambridge on 29 April 1951.

### 1c. New concepts and additions (exact text, all [C])

**Language games (line 28).** The builders of §2 back two posts (2026-03-22, 2026-04-16) and the natural-languages bottom-row page. None of them is on the page. The §23 list is also trimmed without marks (check 5). Replace the whole entry with:
> **Language games.** The *Investigations* begins with a picture of language drawn from Augustine and then imagines a language for which it is right: a builder A and his assistant B use the words "block", "pillar", "slab", "beam"; "A calls them out;—B brings the stone which he has learnt to bring at such-and-such a call" (§2). Wittgenstein introduces the term at §7: "I shall also call the whole, consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven, the 'language-game'." At §23 there are "countless" kinds of use, and new language-games "come into existence, and others become obsolete and get forgotten"; "the term 'language-game' is meant to bring into prominence the fact that the speaking of language is part of an activity, or of a form of life." The list that follows is deliberately open-ended: "Giving orders, and obeying them— / Describing the appearance of an object, or giving its measurements— … Reporting an event— / Speculating about an event— / Forming and testing a hypothesis— … Making a joke; telling it— … Translating from one language into another— / Asking, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying."

All quotations are verbatim in S4 (§2, §7, §23). The current "He adds:" puts the language-game sentence after the list, but in §23 it comes before the list.

**Rule-following (line 38).** The entry gives §201's sentence as §202, and §199 not in Anscombe's wording (check 5). It also lacks the training and custom that seed/cognition ("Wittgenstein's background of training") and cognition-in-core-philosophy ("against a background of custom and practice") lean on, and Kripke's reading. Replace the whole entry with:
> **Rule-following.** "This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule" (§201). What the paradox shows, he goes on, is that "there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases" (§201). What connects a sign-post with my actions is training: "I have been trained to react to this sign in a particular way, and now I do so react to it". And "a person goes by a sign-post only in so far as there exists a regular use of sign-posts, a custom" (§198). "To obey a rule, to make a report, to give an order, to play a game of chess, are customs (uses, institutions)" (§199). The learning of language starts the same way: "Here the teaching of language is not explanation, but training" (§5). One influential reading, introduced by Robert Fogelin (1976) and [Saul Kripke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Kripke) (*Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, 1982), takes Wittgenstein to voice a sceptical paradox and to offer a sceptical solution. G. P. Baker and P. M. S. Hacker, Colin McGinn and Stanley Cavell are among those who reject it (as the *Stanford Encyclopedia* reports).

Backing: §5, §198, §199 and §201 verbatim in S4. §199 "customs (uses, institutions)" is confirmed in S5. The S4 OCR reads "custom". The reading and its critics are given as S1 §3.5 reports them [C]. Kripke has no page on the site; he is linked externally on the constructive branch as well. This is the one-sentence home of the Kripke story (check 6).

**The private language argument (line 40).** This is rebuilt (check 5). Replace the whole entry with:
> **The private language argument.** The sections after the rule-following remarks, from §243, are what interpreters call "the private-language argument" (Wittgenstein never labelled it one). They ask whether there could be a language whose "individual words … are to refer to what can only be known to the person speaking; to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language" (§243). In §258 someone keeps a diary of a recurring sensation, marking it "S" and fixing the sign by attending to the sensation inwardly. "But in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right'." The rule-following remarks had reached the same point: "To think one is obeying a rule is not to obey a rule. Hence it is not possible to obey a rule 'privately': otherwise thinking one was obeying a rule would be the same thing as obeying it" (§202).

**Forms of life (line 34) and the cut section "The anthropocentric boundary" (lines 54–58).** See check 5 for the error and check 8 for the section. Replace the "Forms of life" entry with:
> **Forms of life.** "To imagine a language means to imagine a form of life" (§19). "What has to be accepted, the given, is—so one could say—forms of life" (Part II, p. 226), said where he notes that mathematicians do not quarrel. Language belongs to human natural history: "Commanding, questioning, recounting, chatting, are as much a part of our natural history as walking, eating, drinking, playing" (§25), and "What we are supplying are really remarks on the natural history of human beings" (§415). Readers divide on whether there is one human form of life or many. Newton Garver reads the concept as biological: for him the identification of forms of life with cultures "has no basis whatsoever in the text" (as Danièle Moyal-Sharrock quotes him). P. M. S. Hacker, with G. P. Baker, reads it as cultural: "There is no uniquely human form of life, characteristic of the species – rather there are multiple human forms of life, characterized by different languages, and characteristic of different cultures and epochs." Stanley Cavell distinguished a vertical, biological sense from a horizontal, ethnological one, and Moyal-Sharrock argues that Wittgenstein meant both. "If a lion could talk, we could not understand him" (Part II, p. 223) closes a remark that one human being can be "a complete enigma to another", as in "a strange country with entirely strange traditions … We cannot find our feet with them". It is often read as saying that understanding needs a shared form of life, a reading Hacker counts among the misunderstandings of the concept.

Backing:
- §19, §25, §415, p. 223 and p. 226 verbatim, with context, in S4 [C]. p. 226: "I have not said why mathematicians do not quarrel, but only that they do not. … What has to be accepted, the given, is—so one could say—forms of life." p. 223: "We also say of some people that they are transparent to us. It is, however, important as regards this observation that one human being can be a complete enigma to another. We learn this when we come into a strange country with entirely strange traditions; and, what is more, even given a mastery of the country's language. We do not understand the people. (And not because of not knowing what they are saying to themselves.) We cannot find our feet with them. … If a lion could talk, we could not understand him."
- Hacker: "Wittgenstein's concept of a form of life is not primarily biological, but cultural. There is no uniquely human form of life, characteristic of the species – rather there are multiple human forms of life, characterized by different languages, and characteristic of different cultures and epochs" [C, S8 p. 18]. He lists "other forms of life would be unintelligible to us. 'If a lion could talk, we wouldn't be able to understand it' (PPF §327)" among the readings that "are awry" [C, S8 pp. 15–16].
- Garver, Cavell's two senses and Moyal-Sharrock's bilateral reading [C, S9 §2]. Garver's words are as Moyal-Sharrock quotes them (1994, 266). Garver's book itself was not opened.

The readings are a headline, since they decide what the concept is. The page names them once, without staging. A shorter option: keep only the Garver and Hacker sentences and drop Cavell and Moyal-Sharrock.

**New concept, after "Agreement in judgments" (exact):**
> **Certainty.** *On Certainty* collects the notes of his last eighteen months on G. E. Moore's claims to know such things as "Here is one hand". "The questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (§341). Nor is the picture of the world reached by checking it: "I did not get my picture of the world by satisfying myself of its correctness; nor do I have it because I am satisfied of its correctness. No: it is the inherited background against which I distinguish between true and false" (§94). "The child learns to believe a host of things" (§144). The giving of grounds ends not in propositions that strike us as true but in "our acting, which lies at the bottom of the language-game" (§204; tr. Paul and Anscombe). Readers call these certainties "hinge propositions"; why they cannot be doubted is disputed.

Backing:
- §94, §144, §204 and §341 verbatim in S7 [C].
- The preface: the notes belong "to the last year and a half of Wittgenstein's life"; Malcolm "acted as a goad to his interest in Moore's 'defence of common sense' … 'Here is one hand, and here is another'" [C, S7].
- The dispute "whether this be due to their being epistemically foundational, naturally certain, or logically unavailable to doubt" [C, S1 §5].
- Link Moore externally: `[G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore)`, which is live [C, S10] and the same link the Reid page uses.

**Word test:** Wittgenstein's own word is the image "like hinges"; "hinge propositions" is the readers' term (the SEP puts it in quotation marks).

This backs the social-construction subject ("the inherited world-picture of *On Certainty* … socialised into rather than argued into"; "inherited" is §94) and the Reid page (Moore and *On Certainty*).
